Proposal #008

Accord Subwoofer Install

Hook up the Kicker sub and amp kit that have been sitting in the trunk of the 2017 Accord EX for months. DIY driveway job, one afternoon. ANC disable included.

The Situation

There is a Kicker subwoofer (in an enclosure) and an amp wiring kit sitting in the trunk of the Accord right now. They've been there for a while. The car has the stock head unit, which works fine and doesn't need to be replaced. The sub just needs to be wired up to the amp, the amp needs power from the battery, and the whole system needs an audio signal tapped from the factory speakers.

The stock head unit has no RCA outputs, so we need a way to get a clean signal to the amplifier. There are two options: use an amp with high-level (speaker wire) inputs, or add a Line Output Converter (LOC) to convert the speaker signal to RCA. Either way works. The LOC route (something like the AudioControl LC2i) gives you a cleaner signal with bass boost and remote level control, but a basic amp with high-level inputs works fine for a single sub setup.

The Install (Step by Step)

Step 1: Run Power Wire Battery → Trunk
  1. Disconnect the negative battery terminal before touching anything. This is non-negotiable.
  2. Locate the pre-drilled firewall grommet on the driver's side, near the gas pedal area. It's a rubber plug. Pop it out with a flat screwdriver.
  3. Feed the main power cable (the thick red/yellow wire from the amp kit) through the firewall hole from the engine bay into the cabin.
  4. Install the inline fuse holder on the power cable within 18 inches of the battery. Do not connect to battery yet.
  5. Route the power cable along the driver's side under the door sill trim (just pop the plastic trim off, tuck wire under, snap back). Run it all the way to the trunk.
  6. Keep the power cable on the opposite side from any speaker or RCA cables to avoid interference.
Step 2: Remote Turn-On Wire Fuse Box → Trunk
  1. Open the fuse box under the steering wheel (driver's side kick panel).
  2. Find an accessory fuse slot that only has power when the ignition is on. On the 2017 Accord, the front accessory power outlet fuse works well.
  3. Plug the Add-a-Fuse tap into that slot. This gives you a 12V signal that turns on with the car and off when you shut it down.
  4. Run the remote wire (thin blue wire from amp kit) from the fuse box, along the same path as the power cable, all the way to the trunk.
Why this matters: The remote wire tells the amp to turn on only when the car is running. Without it, the amp either stays on and kills your battery, or you'd have to manually flip a switch every time.
Step 3: Get the Audio Signal Speakers → LOC/Amp
  1. Since the EX has a factory subwoofer on the rear deck, the easiest signal tap is the factory sub wires in the trunk. This saves you from pulling the head unit or rear door panels.
  2. If your amp has high-level inputs: Tap the factory sub wires (or rear speaker wires) directly and run them to the amp's speaker-level inputs.
  3. If using an LOC: Tap the wires, run them to the LOC. From the LOC, run RCA cables to the amp. Mount the LOC somewhere accessible in the trunk near the amp.
  4. Use proper splice connectors or solder + heat shrink. No scotch locks if you can help it, they corrode.
EX-specific tip: Some owners get a cleaner, fuller signal by tapping the rear 6.5" speaker wires instead of the factory sub wire. The factory sub signal is heavily filtered by the factory amp. Try the sub wire first since it's easiest, but if it sounds thin or weak, switch to tapping the rear speakers and use the amp's low-pass filter to cut the highs.
Step 4: Ground, Connect, Tune The Fun Part
  1. Ground the amp: Find bare metal in the trunk. Sand any paint down to shiny metal. Bolt a ring terminal with a short ground wire (under 18 inches) tightly to the bare metal. A trunk bolt works, but avoid seatbelt bolts.
  2. Connect all wires to the amp: power, ground, remote, and audio signal (RCA or speaker-level).
  3. Connect amp output to the subwoofer enclosure's terminals.
  4. Double-check every connection. Make sure the power cable fuse is installed and the inline fuse holder is closed.
  5. Reconnect the negative battery terminal.
  6. Turn the car on. Set the amp gain to minimum. Play music. Slowly turn the gain up until it sounds full but not distorted. Set the low-pass filter to ~80Hz so the sub only plays bass.
  7. Secure the sub enclosure so it doesn't slide around. Velcro strips, a strap, or L-brackets bolted to the trunk floor all work.

ANC Disable (Required)

The 2017 Accord EX has the 7-speaker premium audio system with Active Noise Cancellation. ANC uses cabin microphones and the factory subwoofer to pump out-of-phase sound that cancels engine noise. The moment you add an aftermarket sub, ANC detects the new bass, tries to cancel it, and the more powerful aftermarket sub amplifies the cancellation signal instead. The result is extremely loud, pulsing feedback through the speakers that gets worse when accelerating or decelerating. This is not optional to fix.

This MUST be done before you test the sub. Every single Accord forum thread about "my new sub sounds broken" traces back to ANC still being active. Do not troubleshoot anything else until ANC is disabled.

How to Disable ANC

  1. Remove the center console storage tray below the radio. Two Phillips screws hold it in. Pull it straight out.
  2. Reach behind the opening. You'll feel a silver metal box (the ANC module) mounted to the dash brace.
  3. Disconnect the wiring harness from the ANC module. It's a tight fit. Wiggle it, don't yank it.
  4. That's it. Tuck the harness out of the way and reinstall the tray.

Bluetooth, hands-free calling, steering wheel controls, and everything else still works normally after this. You may notice slightly more road/engine noise with the stereo off, but it's minimal and honestly most people can't even tell.

Bonus: better stock sound too. The ANC module steals power from the head unit to produce cancellation frequencies through all speakers. Disconnecting it frees up that power, and multiple forum users report noticeably improved audio quality from the factory system even before the aftermarket sub is connected.

Tools Needed

Everything here is stuff you already own or can grab from the garage: 10mm socket and ratchet, Phillips screwdriver, flat screwdriver (for trim clips), wire strippers/crimpers, electrical tape and/or heat shrink, zip ties, sandpaper or a wire brush (for the ground point), panel removal tool or a plastic butter knife. A multimeter is nice to have for verifying the remote wire has 12V with ignition on.

Setting the Gain (Don't Skip This)

Gain is not a volume knob. It matches the amp's input sensitivity to the head unit's output voltage. Set it too high and the amp clips the signal, sending a squared-off waveform to the sub that generates heat instead of bass. Clipping is the #1 killer of subwoofers. Set it too low and the sub barely plays, and you'll crank the head unit to compensate, which introduces distortion from the head unit side instead. The goal is a clean, full signal at the volume you actually listen at.

Method 1: By Ear (No Tools)

  1. Turn the amp gain all the way down (counterclockwise).
  2. Turn off all EQ, bass boost, loudness, and any sound processing on the head unit. Set bass/treble to flat/zero. If the head unit has "SVC" (Speed Volume Compensation), turn that off too.
  3. Play a song you know well that has clean, sustained bass. Not something with distorted 808s. Something like a hip-hop track with a deep bass line, or any track where you can clearly hear bass notes.
  4. Turn the head unit volume up to about 75% of max. This is your reference volume. Leave it here for the rest of the process.
  5. Slowly turn the amp gain up. You'll hear the bass start to come in and get louder. Keep turning until you hear any of these: crackling, buzzing, rattling that isn't a loose panel, or the bass note sounding "fuzzy" instead of clean and round. That's clipping.
  6. The moment you hear any of that, stop and turn the gain back down about 10-15% from where you heard it. That's your max clean output.
  7. Turn the head unit back to your normal listening volume. The sub should sound full, deep, and clean. If it doesn't sound loud enough, the issue isn't the gain, it's the amp/sub match or the signal level from the LOC.

Method 2: With a Multimeter (More Precise)

If you want to do this right, use the multimeter you already have. This method sets the gain to a mathematically correct voltage so the amp delivers its rated power without clipping.

  1. Find your target voltage. You need two numbers from your amp's manual: RMS wattage and the ohm load of your sub. Formula: voltage = square root of (watts x ohms). Example: 500W amp into a 2-ohm sub = square root of (500 x 2) = square root of 1000 = 31.6 volts. Write this number down.
  2. Disconnect the speaker wire from the amp's output terminals (the wires going to the sub). Leave them disconnected during tuning.
  3. Turn the amp gain all the way down. Turn off all EQ/bass boost on the head unit and the amp.
  4. Download a 50Hz test tone (0dB sine wave) to your phone. YouTube has them, or search "50Hz sine wave test tone" and download an MP3. Play it on loop through the car stereo via Bluetooth or aux.
  5. Set the head unit volume to 75% of max.
  6. Set your multimeter to AC Volts. Touch the red probe to the amp's positive speaker output terminal and the black probe to the negative terminal.
  7. Slowly turn the gain up while watching the voltage. Stop when it hits your target number (31.6V in our example).
  8. Turn everything off. Reconnect the sub. Done. The gain is now set to deliver clean rated power.
Quick voltage reference chart: 200W @ 2 ohms = 20.0V | 300W @ 2 ohms = 24.5V | 500W @ 2 ohms = 31.6V | 500W @ 4 ohms = 44.7V | 1000W @ 2 ohms = 44.7V | 1000W @ 1 ohm = 31.6V
Never touch the gain again to "make it louder." If you want more volume after this, turn up the head unit (up to 75-80% max). The gain stays where you set it. Cranking the gain past the correct setting doesn't make it louder, it makes it distorted and damages equipment.

Low-Pass Filter (Crossover)

A subwoofer's job is to play only the deep bass frequencies, roughly 20Hz to 80Hz. Your door speakers handle everything above that: vocals, guitars, cymbals, etc. The low-pass filter (LPF) on the amp tells the sub where to stop playing. Without it, the sub tries to reproduce mid-range frequencies it was never designed to play, and the result is a boomy, muddy, undefined mess where you can't hear the actual bass notes clearly.

How to Set It

  1. Find the LPF knob on your amp. It's usually labeled "LPF" or "Low Pass" or "Crossover" and has a frequency range printed around it (commonly 50Hz to 250Hz).
  2. Make sure the crossover switch is set to "LPF" or "LP" mode, not "Full" or "Off." Full/Off mode sends ALL frequencies to the sub, which you never want.
  3. Start at 80Hz. This is the industry standard crossover point for most sub setups and will be correct for 90% of people.
  4. Play a song with both bass and vocals. Listen for whether you can "locate" the sub, meaning it sounds like the bass is clearly coming from the trunk rather than blending into the front speakers. If you can pinpoint where the sub is, the crossover is set too high. Turn it down to 70Hz or 60Hz.
  5. The sweet spot is where the bass sounds like it's coming from everywhere in the car, blending seamlessly with the door speakers. For most trunk-mounted subs in a sedan, this is between 60Hz and 80Hz.
What the numbers mean: 80Hz = sub plays everything below 80Hz, door speakers handle 80Hz and up. Lower number = tighter, more focused bass but less total bass volume. Higher number = more overall bass volume but sounds boomier and more "trunk-y." You're looking for the balance point.

If your amp also has a "subsonic filter" or "high-pass filter" (HPF) knob specifically for the sub channel, set it to 25-30Hz. This cuts the ultra-low frequencies that you can't hear but force the sub cone to make huge excursions, wasting power and potentially bottoming out the driver. Think of it as a safety floor.

Phase Switch

Sound is a wave. Your door speakers push air out, then pull it back. Your sub does the same thing. If the sub is pushing air out at the exact same moment the door speakers are pulling air in, the two waves cancel each other out and you lose bass. This is called being "out of phase." The phase switch on your amp flips the sub's wave by 180 degrees so it pushes when the doors push, and pulls when they pull. When they're in sync, the bass waves add together and sound full and punchy. When they're out of sync, the bass sounds thin, hollow, and weak even though the sub is physically moving.

How to Set It

  1. Find the phase switch on your amp. It's usually a toggle switch labeled "0" and "180" (degrees), or "Normal" and "Reverse," or sometimes just "Phase" with a 0-180 knob.
  2. Play a bass-heavy song at moderate volume. Something with a sustained, clean bass line works best.
  3. Sit in the driver's seat. Listen to the bass for about 15-20 seconds with the switch at 0 degrees.
  4. Flip the switch to 180 degrees. Listen again for 15-20 seconds.
  5. One setting will have noticeably more bass impact and fullness. It should sound like someone turned up the sub volume. The other setting will sound thinner and weaker. Pick the one that sounds louder and fuller. That's the correct phase for your setup.
Why it varies: The "correct" phase depends on the physical distance between the sub and your ears relative to the door speakers, plus the direction the sub is facing. A sub firing toward the rear of the trunk might need 180, while one firing toward the seats might need 0. There's no formula. Just A/B test it and pick the one that sounds better. It takes 30 seconds.

Some amps have a continuously variable phase knob (0 to 180 degrees) instead of a toggle switch. In that case, start at 0 and slowly sweep it while listening. Stop at whatever angle sounds the fullest. This is more precise than a simple switch but the same concept.

Securing the Enclosure

A Kicker loaded enclosure weighs somewhere around 30-50 lbs depending on the model. In a hard brake from 60 mph, that box experiences roughly 1G of force, so it hits the back of the rear seat with its full weight. In an actual collision, forces can exceed 20-30G. An unsecured sub box in a sedan trunk is less dangerous than in a hatchback (the trunk wall and rear seat back act as a barrier), but it will still destroy wiring connections, scratch up the trunk, and potentially crack the back of the rear seat. Plus a box that vibrates and shifts even slightly during normal driving kills bass quality because the enclosure's movement absorbs energy that should be moving air.

Option A: Hook Velcro (Easiest, No Drilling)

This is the move for most people. The trunk carpet in the Accord is a low-pile automotive carpet. The hook side of Velcro (the scratchy/rough side) grabs directly into this carpet without needing the loop side at all. The grip is surprisingly strong.

  1. Buy VELCRO Brand Industrial Strength tape (the 2" wide roll, available at Home Depot, Lowe's, or Amazon). The 4-foot roll is about $10. Do not buy generic dollar-store velcro, it will fail within weeks from bass vibration and heat.
  2. Cut four strips, each about 4-6 inches long.
  3. Peel the adhesive backing and stick the HOOK side (rough/scratchy) to the bottom of the sub enclosure, one strip near each corner. Press firmly and let the adhesive set for at least an hour before placing it in the trunk.
  4. Do NOT put the loop (soft/fuzzy) side on the trunk carpet. The hooks grab the carpet fibers directly. Putting loop-side down just adds a weak point.
  5. Place the enclosure in position and press down firmly. The hooks will bite into the carpet.
  6. Test by trying to slide the box sideways. It should resist strongly. It's removable with a firm upward pull when you need trunk space.
Pro tip: The hook velcro will eventually make the trunk carpet a little fuzzy where it grips. That's cosmetic and unavoidable. If you care about that, add a ratchet strap for a completely non-contact solution instead.

Option B: Ratchet Strap to Cargo Hooks (No Drilling, More Secure)

Better than velcro alone, and completely reversible.

  1. Buy a 1" wide ratchet strap with S-hooks, about 6-10 feet long. Any hardware store, ~$8-12. Don't use bungee cords, they stretch and eventually lose tension.
  2. Check the Accord trunk for factory tie-down hooks or anchor points. There are typically small metal loops or hooks in the trunk corners or along the sides. The LATCH child seat anchors on the rear seat are another option if accessible from the trunk side.
  3. Position the sub enclosure where you want it.
  4. Run the strap over the top of the enclosure and hook each end to anchor points on opposite sides.
  5. Ratchet it snug. Not gorilla-tight (you'll warp the enclosure), just firm enough that the box doesn't shift when you push it.
  6. Tuck any excess strap so it doesn't rattle against the trunk.

Option C: L-Brackets (Most Secure, Permanent-ish)

For the "set it and forget it" approach. This is what shops do.

  1. Buy 3-4 steel L-brackets (3.5" size, about $5-8 for a pack at Home Depot) and #10 or #12 self-tapping sheet metal screws (3/4" length max).
  2. Position the sub enclosure where you want it. Mark the bracket positions on the floor around the enclosure.
  3. CRITICAL: Check underneath the trunk floor before drilling. On the 2017 Accord, the spare tire well is below the trunk floor. Lift the trunk floor mat and look. You need to drill into the raised metal shelf area, NOT into the spare tire well or anywhere near fuel lines. The area directly behind the rear seat fold-down is usually solid sheet metal and safe to drill into.
  4. Screw one side of each L-bracket into the trunk floor through the carpet (the carpet is thin, the screw will punch right through). Use short screws, 3/4" max, so they don't poke through to the underside.
  5. Screw the other side of each L-bracket into the sub enclosure. Pre-drill pilot holes in the MDF to avoid splitting.
  6. To remove the sub later, just unscrew the bracket-to-enclosure screws. The floor brackets can stay in place under the carpet.
Drilling safety: Absolutely do NOT drill anywhere near the center or rear of the trunk floor without checking underneath first. The fuel tank is under there. Stick to the area right behind the rear seat backs where it's structural sheet metal. If in doubt, use velcro or straps instead.

The Bottom Line

You already own everything that costs real money. This is a free-to-$55 afternoon project that's been putting itself off. No new head unit needed. No shop visit needed. Disable ANC, wire up the amp, tune it, and enjoy the bass you paid for months ago.
