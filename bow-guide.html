The Build at a Glance

This guide walks you through building a 72" flatbow from a standard red oak board. Target specs: ~45 lbs @ 28" draw. Total cost: roughly $75 for materials and basic tools. Time investment: one weekend of active work plus drying/curing time.

Why Red Oak?

Red oak is the classic beginner bow wood because it's cheap ($8-12 per board at Home Depot), available everywhere, and teaches you the fundamentals. It's not the best bow wood, it's weak in compression and benefits from backing, but everybody learns on it. Expect to break your first one. That's part of the process.

Safety First

Bows store serious energy. Wear safety glasses during tillering. If you hear cracking, STOP immediately. Never dry-fire (release without an arrow). Chrysals (compression fractures on the belly) are warning signs, take them seriously.

Phase 1 Select Your Board

This is where 90% of bow failures originate. A bad board means a bad bow. Dig through the entire stack at Home Depot. It's worth it.

What You're Looking For

Growth rings (end grain): Look at the end of the board. You want THICK late growth (dark lines) and THIN early growth (light porous areas). Heavy board = dense = good.
Straight grain: Look at the 3/4" sides. The grain lines should run straight down the entire length without "running off" to the sides. Perfect grain = less backing needed.
No knots. Period. Any knot is a failure point. Walk away.
No side warping: A little front-to-back curve is fine (becomes reflex). Side warping makes alignment hell.
Darker = better: Darker red oak is typically denser and stronger.

Pro Tip

Pick up every board in the stack and compare weights. The heaviest board of the same dimensions is the densest. That's your bow wood.

Step-by-Step Layout

Find dead center of board (36" mark on a 72" board)
Mark handle area: 4" centered on that mark (2" each side of center)
Mark fade transitions: 2" on each side of handle (total handle+fades = 8")
Mark where taper begins: 15" from center on each side
Mark tip width: 3/8" to 1/2" centered on each tip

Align the Tips

Run a string with weights on each end down the length of the board. Make sure it passes through dead center. Mark under the string at each tip. This ensures tips are aligned even if the board has slight warping.

Remove Wood

Sides: Use surform rasp or bandsaw. Cut from the 15" mark to the tips following your taper lines. Leave handle area full width (1.5").

Belly (bottom side):

  • Leave handle area at full 3/4" thickness
  • Taper belly from fades toward tips
  • At tips: ~3/8" thick
  • At mid-limb: ~1/2" thick
  • Round the belly corners slightly (makes tillering easier)

Phase 3 Glue On Handle Riser

The riser piece adds mass and stiffness to the handle section, preventing it from bending and concentrating all the flex in the limbs where it belongs.

Dimension Value
Length 10"
Width Same as bow (1.5")
Thickness 3/4"
Find center of riser piece, mark it clearly
Line up center marks on bow and riser
Apply Titebond III to BOTH surfaces, spread with finger
Clamp with 2-3 C-clamps
Wipe excess glue with damp paper towel
Let dry 24 hours minimum

Pro Tip

Use a different colored wood for visual contrast. Walnut on red oak looks sharp and lets you see exactly where the handle transitions to the limbs.

Phase 4 Back the Bow

Why back it? Oak is weak in tension (the back side stretches when you draw). Backing prevents splinters from lifting on the back and snapping the bow. Fiberglass drywall tape with Titebond III is cheap, easy, and effective.

Process (Drywall Tape + Titebond III)

Lay bow flat, back facing up
Cut 3 strips of drywall tape slightly longer than bow
Squeeze generous line of glue down the back, spread with finger
Lay first strip of tape, smooth from center to tips (saturate the mesh)
Wait 15-20 minutes
Add second layer same way, wait 15-20 minutes
Add third layer
Let dry 24 hours
Trim edges with rasp/file (wear gloves, fiberglass itches)

Fiberglass Warning

Drywall tape is fiberglass mesh. The dust will make you itch. Wear gloves when trimming and consider a dust mask. Don't sand it bare-handed.

Phase 5 Cut String Nocks

Mark 1" from each tip on the back
Start groove with triangle file at 45° angle
Finish with rattail file
Round the edges so string doesn't have sharp corners
Cut both sides evenly

Final bow length: 70" nock-to-nock

Phase 6 Tillering

This is the real work. Tillering is the art of making both limbs bend evenly and symmetrically. A well-tillered bow shoots accurately and won't break. Take your time.

Make a Tillering Stick

  • 2×4 about 36" long
  • Cut notches every 1" from 6" to 31"
  • Cut a notch at top to rest bow handle in

Make a Tillering String

  • Long string with loop on one end, timber hitch on other
  • Must be long enough to be slack when on the bow
  • Timber hitch lets you shorten it as you go

The Process

Stage 1: Long String (Slack) → 30# @ 20"

Put bow on tillering stick, pull string down just a few inches. Look at limb bend. Are they even? Fix any differences before pulling farther. If one limb is stiffer, remove wood from its belly (long strokes, fade to tip). Exercise limbs 30 times after every wood removal. Fix ANY hinges or flat spots before pulling farther. Goal: 30# @ 20" with even bend.

Stage 2: Low Brace (~3") → 40# @ 22"

Shorten string, brace bow at ~3". Continue tillering one inch at a time. Stay under 40# as you work toward 22". Leave outer 5-6" of tips alone (keep them stiff). Watch for hinges (concentrated bend) and flat spots (stiff sections).

Stage 3: Full Brace (Fist + Thumb) → 45-46# @ 27"

Brace to full height (~7"). Continue one inch at a time. Work slowly, you're close to final weight. Take wood off evenly from both limbs (count strokes). Stop at 45-46# @ 27" (it'll settle to 45# @ 28" after shooting).

Checking Your Work

Tiller check: Measure from fade to string on both sides. Difference should be <1/4". Limb that bends more = top limb.

String alignment: Look down from tip. String should run through center of handle.

Critical Warning

If you hear cracking at ANY point, STOP. Inspect for chrysals (compression fractures on the belly). If you find them, you can heat-treat the belly or the bow may be salvageable, but proceed with extreme caution. Many bows break at this stage. It's part of learning.

Phase 7 Shape Handle & Arrow Rest

Cut arrow rest 1.25" above center (saw then rasp to shape)
Radius the shelf so arrows/feathers pass smoothly
Round all handle edges for comfort
Shape fades into limbs smoothly (no sharp transitions)

Phase 8 Finish

Sand: 100 grit (remove tool marks) → 220 → 320 → 400 → 600
Wipe down with damp cloth, let dry, sand again with 400
Optional: Spray paint the ugly drywall tape backing
Apply finish: 5 coats Deft spray (20 min between coats)
After coat 3: write specs with Sharpie, then final 2 coats
Wrap handle with leather, lace with artificial sinew
Add rest/strike plate: Self-adhesive velcro (soft side) or leather

String It & Shoot

  • Final string: Flemish twist B-50 Dacron, 66-67" for a 70" bow
  • Brace height: fist + thumb (~7")
  • Break it in gradually, watch for problems
  • Expect 1-2" of string follow after break-in

You Did It

If you made it this far with an intact, shooting bow, congratulations. You now understand wood in a way most people never will. Your second bow will be better. Your third will impress people. Keep at it.

Oak vs Hickory: Quick Comparison

Factor Red Oak Hickory
Availability Home Depot, Lowe's, everywhere Harder to find, lumber yards better
Beginner Forgiveness Less forgiving, weak in compression Most forgiving of small errors
Failure Mode Gets chrysals on belly Better tension/compression balance
Backing Required? Highly recommended Can go unbacked if following growth ring
Best For Learning (cheap, expect failures) Better success rate first try

Bottom line: Hickory is objectively better bow wood. But red oak at $8/board teaches you everything you need to know before investing in quality material. Break a few cheap boards, then graduate to hickory.
