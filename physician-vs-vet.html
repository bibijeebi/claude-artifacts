Policy Analysis · February 2026

Physician vs. Veterinarian Supply Pipelines in the U.S.

Bottom line: Both professions constrain supply, but the mechanisms are completely different. Physicians are bottlenecked downstream by a federal residency cap that the AMA itself lobbied for, propped up by $15B/year in Medicare funding that inflates compensation. Veterinarians are bottlenecked upstream by a tiny number of accredited schools with limited seats, operating in a free market with no government payer. The result: it's statistically harder to get into vet school than medical school, but physicians still earn roughly 1.5–2.5x more depending on specialty.

North Carolina Specifics

NC ranks toward the lower end nationally for vet pay, but still solidly six figures. Salary.com puts the NC average at $112K, ERI at $116K, ZipRecruiter at $150K. Raleigh-Durham area averages $190K total comp per Glassdoor. The spread depends heavily on practice type, urban vs. rural, and whether production bonuses are included. Companion animal new grads in the Triangle area are likely starting $120–140K+ with signing bonuses common.

The Two Pipelines

Physicians The Residency Bottleneck

Education Gate (relatively wide)

Roughly 159 MD-granting medical schools plus ~44 DO schools produce about 29,000 graduates annually. Medical school acceptance rates sit around 44%. This gate has widened: the 2025 entering class was the largest ever at 23,440 MD matriculants, and total MD enrollment hit an all-time high of 100,723.

The Bottleneck (residency)

You cannot practice medicine independently without completing residency. The Balanced Budget Act of 1997 froze Medicare-funded residency positions at each hospital's 1996 level. In the 2025 match: 40,041 PGY-1 positions were offered to 47,208 active applicants, and about 9,500 went unmatched. That's 0.85 seats per applicant. Hospitals can fund residents above their cap (70% do), but the unfunded cost discourages expansion.

The Payment System

Medicare is the largest single payer of U.S. healthcare and spends ~$15 billion annually on GME alone. Medicare reimbursement rates set the floor for physician compensation because private insurers benchmark against them (often paying 150–250% of Medicare). This government payment infrastructure is a massive wage amplifier with no veterinary equivalent.

The AMA's Role

The AMA actively lobbied for the 1997 cap. At the time, the AMA and AAMC believed the U.S. faced a physician surplus and recommended cutting residency positions by 25% (from ~25,000 to ~19,000). Congress was happy to oblige because capping GME saved Medicare money. This was not the first time: in the early 1900s, the AMA lobbied to shut down medical schools (halving them from 160 to 80 after the Flexner Report), lobbied for supply reductions during the Great Depression, and in the 1980s–90s lobbied to restrict foreign-trained doctors' access. The AMA's own RUC committee (29 mostly-specialist physicians) also helps set Medicare reimbursement rates, giving organized medicine influence over both supply and price.

Veterinarians The School Bottleneck

Education Gate (this IS the bottleneck)

35 AVMA-accredited vet schools in the U.S., of which 30 are fully accredited and 5 hold provisional accreditation, spread across 28 states and Puerto Rico. Total U.S. DVM enrollment is ~15,000. About 3,500 DVMs graduate annually from U.S. programs. Class sizes range from 40–75 (newer programs) to 169 (Iowa State). Acceptance rates are 10–15%, making vet school significantly harder to enter than medical school.

No Downstream Bottleneck

Unlike physicians, vets have no mandatory post-graduate residency. Earn the DVM, pass the NAVLE, practice independently. Veterinary residencies exist for specialization but are optional. There's no Medicare-style federal funding mechanism controlling residency slots.

The Payment System

There is no Medicare for animals. Vet services operate in a pure market. Pet owners pay out of pocket or through private pet insurance (still uncommon). Compensation is capped by what clients will spend, which is limited by the fact that animals are not legally or economically valued the same as humans. New companion animal grads average ~$140K starting, established vets ~$154K. Specialists can reach $200–350K. Equine ($95K) and food animal ($100K) vets earn considerably less.

Why So Few Schools?

Unlike the physician pipeline, vet school scarcity wasn't engineered by deliberate cartel lobbying. Vet schools are extraordinarily expensive to build: teaching hospitals treating multiple species, large animal facilities, specialized equipment, faculty with clinical expertise across a huge range of medicine. Most exist as land-grant university programs, and many states simply never built one, instead sending students to neighbors through interstate compacts. AVMA accreditation standards serve quality assurance but also create a high barrier to entry.

Historical Timeline

1910
Flexner Report published. AMA uses it to shut down "substandard" medical schools, reducing the count from 160 to 80. Physician supply drops sharply.
1930s–40s
AMA lobbies for physician supply reductions during the Great Depression. In 1938, the AMA House of Delegates calls for full U.S. citizenship as a prerequisite for medical licensure. By decade's end, 30+ states require citizenship or naturalization papers, effectively blocking refugee physicians, including Jewish doctors fleeing Nazi Europe.
1965
Medicare enacted. Payments to teaching hospitals for residency training begin, creating the GME funding pipeline that still exists today.
1980s–90s
AMA lobbies to restrict foreign-trained doctors and limit residency slots for IMGs. "Physician surplus" fears dominate organized medicine's narrative.
March 1997
AMA and consortium of medical organizations recommend cutting residency positions by 25%, warning of "serious oversupply of physicians."
August 1997
Balanced Budget Act passes. Medicare GME caps frozen at 1996 levels. This is the single most consequential policy creating today's physician shortage.
2007–2020
Western University (2007), Midwestern (2014), Long Island (2016), and Lincoln Memorial (2020) become first wave of new U.S. vet schools in decades.
2021
Congress adds 1,000 new Medicare-funded residency positions via Consolidated Appropriations Act, first cap increase since 1997.
2023
Congress adds 200 more residency positions. Tennessee becomes early adopter of alternative IMG licensing (no U.S. residency required).
2024–2025
~11 new vet school programs in pipeline (33% increase). Rowan (NJ, 75 students, provisional Sept 2025), Utah State, Arkansas State, Lyon College, Clemson, and others open or begin accepting students.
2025
18 states now offer alternative licensing for foreign-trained physicians without U.S. residency. Texas joins September 2025. ABIM launches Pathway E for board certification.
2025 (pending)
Resident Physician Shortage Reduction Act (H.R. 3890) proposes 14,000 new Medicare GME positions over seven years.

What's Changing

Physicians Three Disruptions

1. Medicare GME Expansion

Congress has distributed over 600 of 1,200 new residency positions authorized in 2021 and 2023. H.R. 3890 proposes 14,000 more over seven years. These are meaningful but modest against a projected 86,000-physician shortfall by 2036.

2. State-Level IMG Pathways (the big one)

18 states now let foreign-trained doctors practice without completing a U.S. residency. Requirements typically include ECFMG certification, USMLE passage, 3–5 years of clinical practice abroad, a U.S. job offer, and 2–3 years of supervised practice. Most restrict to underserved areas or primary care. ABIM's Pathway E now allows these physicians to pursue board certification. This could eventually erode the residency bottleneck that's kept physician salaries elevated for three decades, though early adoption is modest (Washington state has granted ~40 licenses so far).

3. Scope of Practice Expansion

More states are granting nurse practitioners full practice authority, increasing the supply of primary care providers without adding physicians. The AMA continues lobbying against this but is losing ground.

Veterinarians The School Explosion

~11 new DVM programs are in development, representing a potential 33% increase in accredited schools. Existing schools are also expanding class sizes. This is unprecedented. Rowan University in New Jersey opened its doors to 75 inaugural students in September 2025, becoming the first vet school in the state and receiving provisional AVMA accreditation the same day students matriculated.

The Counterargument

Some within the profession argue this expansion is overshooting. DVM enrollment has nearly doubled since 2000. The applicant-to-seat ratio is declining. Many new programs appear driven by tuition revenue (disproportionately adding high-tuition out-of-state seats) rather than genuine workforce needs. If expansion continues at this pace, it could flip the market: more graduates competing for the same positions, potentially driving down compensation while carrying ever-larger debt. Unlike medicine, there's no government payment system to prop up wages if supply increases.

The Core Economic Logic

The physician system is a government-subsidized cartel with price supports. Medicare pays hospitals to train doctors (supply control) and then pays those doctors above-market rates for services (price support). The AMA historically lobbied for both sides. The result: physician salaries 2–5x higher than comparable professionals in other developed countries. U.S. orthopedic surgeons average $550K+. Their UK NHS counterparts make $150–200K.

The veterinary system is a free market with a natural supply constraint. Nobody deliberately restricted vet schools to drive up wages. They're just genuinely expensive to build and accredit. The absence of a government payer means compensation is capped by what pet owners will spend. That said, vet salaries have climbed substantially in recent years: new companion animal grads now average $140K, and established vets average $154K. The gap with physicians, while still significant, is narrower than commonly believed.

The liability argument is real but secondary. Physician malpractice errors can kill people, and wrongful death lawsuits yield seven-figure verdicts. Veterinary malpractice damages are generally limited to the animal's fair market value plus treatment costs. This justifies some pay gap, but not the full 1.5–2.5x earnings differential. The structural supply and payment mechanisms are the primary drivers.
