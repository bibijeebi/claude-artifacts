Article I

Why a Faster Internet Plan Does Not Help

Internet speed and Wi-Fi range are two different things. Five facts control this project.

  1. Fiber is the pipe. Wi-Fi is the sprinkler. The fiber line brings water to the house. The router sprays it into the air. A bigger pipe does not make the sprinkler throw water farther.
  2. The government caps the radio power. The FCC sets a legal limit on Wi-Fi transmit power. Every home router sits at about that same limit. Craw-Kan cannot raise it. A $500 router cannot raise it.
  3. Your phone is the weak talker. Wi-Fi is a two-way conversation. The router can shout across a field. The phone must answer with a radio the size of a fingernail. Range ends where the phone’s whisper dies, not where the router’s shout dies. The cure is a radio near the phone, not a louder radio far away.
  4. Your house fights you. The barndominium has metal walls and a metal roof. Metal traps radio signals like a closed can. A signal born inside the metal mostly stays inside. This is also why the connection stalls indoors while the speed test looks good. The signal bounces around the can.
  5. Trees eat signal. The timber around the pond blocks Wi-Fi almost as well as a wall does. A radio covers what it can see.

Conclusion: you cannot buy range from the internet company. You place more radios instead. The rest of this document is where the radios go.

Article II

The Plan, in Three Phases

All the gear is from one company, Ubiquiti UniFi. Every device shows up in one phone app. One network name covers the whole property, and your phone switches between radios on its own.

Phase I. The House and the Yard

≈ $700

This phase ends the indoor stalls and covers everything within about 300 feet of the house.

  • One gateway. A small box that runs the whole network. It connects to the Craw-Kan fiber box.
  • Two indoor access points. One in the house, one in the shop. Each one gets a wire back to the gateway. Wires defeat the metal walls.
  • One outdoor access point, mounted high on the outside gable. Its signal starts outside the metal can, so it can reach the yard, the garden, and the near pasture.

Phase II. The Cabin

≈ $620

The cabin needs its own connection back to the house. There are two ways to build it.

Option A · My pick

A wireless bridge

Two small dishes, one on the house and one on the cabin, pointed at each other. They act as an invisible Ethernet cable through the air. Then one outdoor access point covers the cabin and its yard.

  • Rated for miles. Your distance is easy for it.
  • Carries hundreds of Mbps, far more than any device needs.
  • Installs in an afternoon. No digging.
Option B · The trench

A buried Ethernet cable

Rent a walk-behind trencher. Bury a burial-rated Cat6 cable 18 to 24 inches deep, and put a surge protector on each end.

  • Copper Ethernet dies past 328 feet. If the run is longer, the cable alone cannot do it.
  • Costs about the same as the bridge, plus a day of digging.
  • Right answer for short runs to spots with no power.
Verdict: take the bridge. The cabin sits on its own electric meter. A buried copper wire between two meters ties two grounds together, and lightning loves that wire. A radio link carries no electricity, so a strike cannot ride it into the house.

Phase III. The Far Pasture (optional)

≈ $850

One more outdoor access point on a 16 to 20 foot pole at the far side, fed by a second bridge link from the house. This puts a working phone at the far fence line.

The pole is the hard part, because the pole needs power. Two ways exist: run a power line out from the cabin meter, or set a small solar panel with a battery box at the pole. Do this phase last, and only if the far ground earns it.

Article III

The Installation, in Order

  1. Connect the gateway to the Craw-Kan fiber box.
  2. Wire the two indoor access points to the gateway.
  3. Mount the outdoor access point high on the gable, outside the metal.
  4. Mount one bridge dish on the house and one on the cabin. Make sure that each dish can see the other.
  5. Point the dishes at each other, then lock them down.
  6. Put a drip loop in every outdoor cable, so water runs off before the plug.
  7. Connect a ground wire from each mast to a ground rod.
  8. Give every access point the same network name and password in the app.
  9. Walk the property with a phone. Mark the dead spots on this map. Then adjust the radios.
Surveyor’s Notes · Read Before You Spend
  • Best case is usable internet everywhere a radio can see you. It is not a gigabit at every fence post. No plan on earth gives you that.
  • Under the timber, expect a weak signal or none. That is physics, not a defect.
  • Phase I fixes about 80 percent of daily life. Do it first. Then decide if Phases II and III earn their money.
  • Missouri storms will test every mount. High mounts, drip loops, and ground wires are not optional.
  • You have the labor and the tools on site. Every step in this plan is owner-installable.
