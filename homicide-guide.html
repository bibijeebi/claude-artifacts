The Common Law Foundation

American homicide law derives from English common law, which originally recognized just two categories.

Murder

Unlawful killing with "malice aforethought"

Manslaughter

Unlawful killing without malice aforethought

What is "Malice Aforethought"?

Despite the name, this doesn't require hatred or premeditation. It's a legal term of art covering four mental states:

01
Intent to kill
02
Intent to cause serious bodily harm
03
Extreme recklessness
"Depraved heart"
04
Felony murder
Death during dangerous felony

If you had any of these four mental states when you killed someone, it was murder. If you killed someone unlawfully but without these states, it was manslaughter.

The Degree System

Pennsylvania invented murder degrees in 1794 to distinguish who deserves the death penalty versus prison. Most states adopted some version.

First-Degree Murder

Premeditated and deliberate killing, OR felony murder (killing during enumerated felonies like robbery, rape, arson, burglary). The "willful, deliberate, premeditated" standard. Some states require actual planning; others say premeditation can form in an instant.

Second-Degree Murder

All other murder. Intent to kill or cause serious harm, but without premeditation. Classic example: bar fight where someone pulls a gun in rage and shoots. Also includes "depraved heart" murder in many states.

Third-Degree Murder Limited States

Only exists in Pennsylvania, Florida, Minnesota, and Wisconsin. Usually covers depraved heart killings or drug delivery resulting in death. Pennsylvania uses it for unintentional killings during non-enumerated felonies.

Depraved Heart Murder

A weird middle ground. No intent to kill anyone specific, but acting so recklessly that you clearly didn't care if someone died. Playing Russian roulette with someone, shooting into an occupied building, drag racing through a crowded street. States categorize this differently: some call it second-degree murder, some third-degree, some create a separate "reckless murder" category.

Manslaughter: Voluntary vs. Involuntary

Voluntary Manslaughter

Intentional killing with legal mitigation.

You meant to kill them, but you were so emotionally overwhelmed by provocation that malice aforethought is negated.

Classic example: Walking in on spouse in bed with someone else and killing them on the spot. The provocation must be the kind that would cause a reasonable person to lose control, and you must not have had time to cool off.

Involuntary Manslaughter

Unintentional killing through negligence or recklessness.

Criminal negligence (gross deviation from reasonable care), recklessness (conscious disregard of substantial risk), or misdemeanor-manslaughter (death during commission of non-dangerous misdemeanor).

Some states split this further into "reckless manslaughter" vs. "negligent homicide."

The Full Hierarchy

From most to least culpable in a typical degree-system state:

Crime Mental State Typical Sentence
First-Degree Murder Premeditated intent to kill, or felony murder Life or death
Second-Degree Murder Intent to kill without premeditation, or depraved heart 15 years to life
Third-Degree Murder Depraved heart or drug delivery death 10–40 years
Voluntary Manslaughter Intent to kill + heat of passion 3–15 years
Involuntary Manslaughter Recklessness or criminal negligence 1–6 years

State Variations

Every state does its own thing. There's no single "correct" system.

Model Penal Code States

Use "murder" (purposely/knowingly killing, or extreme recklessness) and "manslaughter" (reckless killing, or murder mitigated by extreme emotional disturbance). No degree subdivisions within murder.

Texas

Capital murder, murder, manslaughter, criminally negligent homicide. No degree subdivisions within murder. Classifies felony sentencing ranges by degree (first-degree felony = 5–99 years or life), which is why media incorrectly says "first-degree murder."

California

First and second degree murder, voluntary and involuntary manslaughter. Second-degree is the "default" murder if first-degree requirements aren't met.

New York

First-degree murder is extremely narrow (cops, witnesses, multiple victims, torture). Most intentional killings are second-degree murder. Has "aggravated manslaughter" as an intermediate category.

Florida

Has third-degree murder for killings during non-enumerated felonies and drug delivery deaths.

Minnesota

Third-degree murder is "depraved mind" killings. This is the statute Derek Chauvin was convicted under for George Floyd's death.

Key Distinctions to Remember

Murder

Had one of the four malice aforethought mental states: intent to kill, intent to cause serious harm, extreme recklessness, or felony murder.

Manslaughter

Killed someone unlawfully but without malice aforethought. Either mitigated by provocation (voluntary) or unintentional through recklessness/negligence (involuntary).

First-Degree Murder

Was it premeditated? Did it happen during a dangerous felony? If yes to either, it's first degree.

Second-Degree Murder

Intent to kill or cause serious harm, but no premeditation. The "default" murder category.

Applied: The Karmelo Anthony Case (Texas, 2025)

Karmelo Anthony is charged with Murder under Texas Penal Code §19.02, a first-degree felony. Sentencing range: 5 years to 99 years, or life.

Why not capital murder? None of the aggravating factors apply. It wasn't during a robbery, the victim wasn't a cop, wasn't under 10, etc.

Why not manslaughter? Stabbing someone in the chest with a knife shows intent to cause at least serious bodily injury. You can't argue "I didn't mean to hurt him" when you put a blade in his chest.

The self-defense angle: This doesn't change the charge—it's an affirmative defense at trial. If the jury buys self-defense, he's acquitted entirely. If they don't, he's convicted of murder with that 5–99/life range.

Compromise verdict possibility: Texas allows lesser-included offense instructions. The jury could theoretically convict on manslaughter instead of murder if they think he genuinely believed he was in danger but that belief was unreasonable.

In a degree state, prosecutors would debate: was "touch me and see what happens" while reaching into his bag evidence of premeditation (first-degree), or was this a spur-of-the-moment reaction (second-degree)?
