Why Squirrel?

Squirrel is the original American small game. Before deer hunting was a cultural institution, squirrel was the everyday protein of rural America. Your great-grandparents ate it regularly. It's lean, high-protein, genuinely good-tasting dark meat that's somewhere between rabbit and the dark meat on a chicken thigh, with a slightly nutty, earthy quality from the animal's diet of acorns and hickory nuts.

Here's the real pitch: if you own even a small piece of land with hardwood trees, you are sitting on a self-replenishing source of free-range, organic protein that requires zero feed costs, zero infrastructure, and minimal equipment. Eastern gray squirrels breed twice a year and populations bounce back fast. A half-acre lot with oak trees can sustainably yield 15-20+ squirrels per season without putting a dent in the local population.

By the Numbers A single dressed gray squirrel yields roughly 2.5-3 oz of edible meat after cooking and deboning. That's lean protein at about 25g per squirrel with virtually zero fat. Ten squirrels gives you about 1.5 lbs of picked meat, enough for 2-3 hearty meals for two people.

Hunting Squirrels

Squirrel hunting is the most accessible form of hunting in America. You don't need a lease, a blind, scent control, or a truck full of gear. You need a gun, some patience, and a few hardwood trees. In most states, the season runs from October through February with generous bag limits (often 5-8 per day), and in many places squirrels are classified as a nuisance species with year-round seasons on your own property.

Firearms

The two standard choices are a .22 LR rifle and a pellet gun. Both work. The .22 is the traditional squirrel gun: accurate, cheap to feed, and lethal at any reasonable range. The downside is noise (your neighbors will know) and it can blow through more meat than necessary at close range, especially backstraps on a body shot. A quality .177 or .22 caliber air rifle in the 800-1000 FPS range is quieter, more than adequate inside 30 yards, and ideal for backyard hunting where noise matters. Either way, you're aiming for headshots. A clean head shot preserves all the meat and drops them instantly.

Safety First Always know your backstop. When shooting up into trees, know what's behind and beyond your target. A .22 LR round can travel over a mile. Pellets are safer in suburban settings but still demand awareness. Check your local laws on discharge of firearms within city/county limits. Many rural areas and unincorporated land allow it; subdivisions typically don't.

When & Where

Squirrels are most active in the first two hours after sunrise and the last two hours before sunset. You'll hear them before you see them: the sound of cutting (chewing on nuts) and the rustle of movement through leaves. Find a spot under hardwood trees (oak, hickory, walnut, pecan) and sit still. That's it. Sit against a tree trunk, don't move, and they'll come to you within 15-20 minutes. The hardest part of squirrel hunting is being patient enough to sit still.

Bird feeders are squirrel magnets. If you've got feeders and you're tired of them raiding the seed, well, there's your hunting setup already built. Early fall when acorns are dropping is prime time, but squirrels are active feeders all winter. Cold mornings after a frost can be especially productive since they're hungry and careless.

Pro Tip If you bump a squirrel and it runs to the far side of a tree trunk, just wait. If you're alone, toss a stick to the opposite side, and it'll circle back around to you. Or just wait 5 minutes. Curiosity always wins. They'll peek back around.

The Shot

Headshots are the standard. It's an instant kill, wastes zero meat, and at typical distances (10-25 yards) it's a very makeable shot with a scoped .22 or a decent pellet rifle. Body shots work but you'll lose the backstraps (the two strips of meat running along the spine), which are the best individual cut on the animal. If you're shooting into the canopy, wait for the squirrel to stop moving and present a clear shot. Shooting through leaves and branches leads to deflected rounds and wounded animals.

Field Dressing & Processing

Processing a squirrel is simple once you've done it twice. The whole process from dead squirrel to bagged quarters takes under 5 minutes per animal with practice. There are two main skinning methods and the quartering is straightforward.

Skinning: The Two Methods

A

The Tail-Pull Method (Fastest)

This is the standard for speed. Step on the tail at the base. Pull up on the hind legs, and the skin peels off the back half of the body like pulling off a sock. Then flip the squirrel, step on the hind feet, and pull the remaining skin down off the front half. The whole hide comes off in two pulls. Cut the head, feet, and tail off with a sharp knife or game shears. Takes 60 seconds.

B

The Belly-Cut Method (More Control)

Make a lateral cut across the back (not through the guts), work your fingers under the skin, and peel it off in sections. This is slower but gives you more control if you want to save the hide for tanning. You pull the skin toward the head and toward the tail separately, then remove the head, feet, and tail.

Gutting

Once skinned, make a small incision at the base of the ribcage (be careful not to puncture the intestines) and cut down to the pelvis. Everything falls out. You can scoop the cavity clean with your fingers. Rinse with cold water. That's it. The key mistakes to avoid: don't puncture the bladder (small and at the very bottom of the cavity) and don't rupture the gallbladder (tiny green sac attached to the liver). Either one will taint the meat with off flavors. If you do nick them, rinse immediately and thoroughly.

Quartering

A quartered squirrel has five usable pieces: two front legs, two hind legs, and the saddle (the back section with the backstraps). The front legs pull off at the shoulder with a twist and a cut, no joint to saw through. The hind legs require cutting through the hip joint, which is easy to find once you flex the leg. The saddle is everything between the shoulders and pelvis, and it's the best part. Trim the ribcage off (almost no meat there) and keep the saddle as one piece.

Pro Tip Kitchen shears or game shears are more useful than a knife for quartering squirrels. The bones are small and the shears cut right through ribs and spine without the struggle of trying to knife through tiny joints.

Cleaning Up

Soak your quartered pieces in cold salt water (1 tablespoon per quart) for at least an hour, overnight in the fridge is better. This draws out blood, mellows any gamey flavor, and firms up the meat. Some people use buttermilk instead, which does the same thing plus tenderizes. Either way, rinse before cooking or freezing. Pat dry before bagging.

04

Storage & Preservation

Vacuum sealing and freezing is the gold standard. A single dressed and quartered squirrel fits neatly into a small vacuum seal bag and runs about 5 oz. Vacuum sealed squirrel keeps 6-12 months in a chest freezer without any quality loss. If you don't have a vacuum sealer, double-bag in zip-lock freezer bags with as much air pressed out as possible, and use within 3-4 months.

Packaging Strategy

Bag them one squirrel per bag for the most flexibility. That way you can pull exactly how many you need for a recipe without thawing a huge block. If you're planning a specific cook (a big pot of stew), you can bulk-bag 4-5 together. Label your bags with the date and number of squirrels. A typical harvest over a season might look like 6-10 bags of various sizes in your freezer, totaling 2-4 lbs of bone-in meat.

Yield Cheat Sheet 1 squirrel ≈ 5 oz bone-in ≈ 2.5-3 oz picked meat. 5 squirrels ≈ 1.5 lbs bone-in ≈ 12-15 oz meat. 10 squirrels ≈ 3 lbs bone-in ≈ 1.5-1.7 lbs meat. That last number is your benchmark: 10 squirrels fills a good stew pot.

Thawing

Thaw in the fridge overnight, never on the counter. Squirrel is small enough that it thaws fast: individual bags are ready in 6-8 hours in the fridge. In a pinch, cold water thaw in 30-45 minutes (leave in the sealed bag, submerge in cold water, change water every 15 minutes). Never microwave thaw game meat. It cooks the edges before the center is thawed and you'll ruin the texture.

Cooking Methods

Squirrel meat is lean, tough, and collagen-rich. This means it rewards slow, moist cooking methods and punishes fast, dry ones. Think of it like a tiny, bony beef shank rather than a chicken breast. Here's every major cooking method ranked by how well it works with squirrel.

Getting the Meat Off the Bone

Before anything else, you need to know the universal move: parboiling. Cover your squirrel quarters in water, bring to a boil, drop to a low simmer, and cook 45-60 minutes until the meat pulls away from the bone. You can twist a hind leg and the meat will start sliding. Let it cool, then pick the meat off. You'll get clean pulled meat and a pot of rich squirrel stock as a byproduct. That stock is gold: full of collagen, gels up in the fridge, and gives incredible body to any soup, stew, or gravy you make with it.

The alternative is a pressure cooker (Instant Pot): 25-30 minutes at high pressure, same result, faster. Either method works for bulk processing your whole freezer stash in one session. Raw deboning is technically possible but impractical on an animal this small. The bones are tiny and irregular. Cook first, then pick. Always.

Method Breakdown

🟢 Excellent Methods

Braising / Stewing: The king. Low and slow in liquid. Breaks down all the connective tissue, renders the meat fork-tender, and creates its own gravy. This is what squirrel was born for.

Pressure Cooking: Same result as braising in a fraction of the time. 25-30 min to fall-apart tender. Best for bulk processing.

Slow Cooker: Set it and forget it. 6-8 hours on low with liquid. Perfect for stews and dumplings.

🟡 Works With Technique

Frying: Classic, but requires a long buttermilk soak and careful technique. Par-fry at 300°F, rest, then finish at 350°F. Or fry then braise in gravy (the Appalachian move).

Grilling: Possible if you parboil first to tenderize, then finish on the grill with sauce. Direct grilling from raw = leather.

Smoking: Low and slow at 225°F works but the yield is tiny. Better as a novelty than a primary method.

Important Never cook squirrel rare or medium. Always cook to well-done (internal temp 165°F+). Squirrel can carry parasites and bacteria that require full cooking to eliminate. This is non-negotiable with any wild game.

Dealing with Gaminess

Squirrel is mild as game goes, but some people are sensitive to it. The gamey flavor lives in the fat, not the meat. Trim all visible fat before cooking if you or your audience is squirrel-skeptical. Beyond that, a salt water or buttermilk soak overnight pulls blood and mellows flavor significantly. Strong seasonings (Cajun, taco spice, Italian herbs) mask any remaining gaminess easily. In a well-seasoned stew or taco, most people cannot tell it's not chicken or rabbit.

Making Ground Squirrel

Yes, you can make squirrel burgers. Parboil, pick all the meat, then pulse in a food processor until ground. Pure squirrel is too lean to hold a patty together, so mix it 60/40 or 70/30 with pork fat, beef tallow, or even bacon grease. Season aggressively: garlic, onion powder, black pepper, maybe Cajun or smoked paprika. Form into thin smash burger patties. This is a legitimate way to use squirrel and people who've never had it will be impressed. Ten squirrels gives you enough for 4-5 burgers blended with fat.

Recipes

These are the core squirrel recipes, ranked by how good they turn out and how many squirrels they need. Every one of these has been made by actual squirrel hunters, not food bloggers who bought farm-raised squirrel once for content.

Squirrel & Dumplings

Essential
4-5 squirrels ~2.5 hrs Feeds 4-6

★★★★★

The single best use of squirrel meat. Parboil your squirrels 45-60 min, pick the meat, save the stock. Sauté onion, carrot, celery in butter, add back the stock, bring to a simmer. Add picked meat. Make drop dumplings: 2 cups flour, 1 tbsp baking powder, 1 tsp salt, 3 tbsp cold butter cut in, ¾ cup milk. Drop by spoonfuls into the simmering stock. Cover and cook 15 min without lifting the lid. Season with salt, pepper, thyme. This stretches 4-5 squirrels into a pot that feeds a family.

Brunswick Stew

Classic
5-6 squirrels ~3 hrs Feeds 6-8

★★★★★

The Southern classic that was originally built around squirrel. Parboil and pick your meat. In a Dutch oven: sauté onion, add picked meat, canned diced tomatoes, lima beans, corn (frozen is fine), diced potatoes, chicken stock (or squirrel stock), a splash of BBQ sauce or ketchup for tang, Worcestershire, salt, pepper, hot sauce. Simmer low for 2+ hours until everything melds together and the potatoes start breaking down to thicken it. The longer it goes, the better it gets.

Southern Fried Squirrel

Moderate
3-4 squirrels ~1.5 hrs + overnight soak Feeds 2-3

★★★★☆

Soak quarters overnight in buttermilk in the fridge. Dredge in seasoned flour (salt, pepper, garlic powder, paprika, cayenne). Fry at 300°F for 10-12 min (par-cook), pull, let oil come back to 350°F, then finish-fry 3-4 min until golden and crispy. Alternatively: fry until golden, then braise the fried pieces in cream gravy for 30 min. That's the old-school Appalachian move and it's the best of both worlds: crispy exterior, tender fall-apart meat.

Squirrel Tacos

Easy
3-4 squirrels ~1.5 hrs Feeds 3-4

★★★★☆

The best way to introduce someone to squirrel. Parboil and pick the meat. Sauté with taco seasoning (cumin, chili powder, garlic, onion powder, paprika, oregano), a squeeze of lime, splash of water. Serve on corn tortillas with cilantro, diced onion, salsa, a squeeze of lime. The seasoning does the heavy lifting and the pulled texture works perfectly as a taco filling. Most people genuinely can't tell it's not chicken.

Squirrel Pot Pie

Moderate
4-5 squirrels ~2 hrs Feeds 4

★★★★☆

Same concept as chicken pot pie. Parboil and pick your meat. Make a cream gravy: butter, flour, squirrel stock, heavy cream. Add picked meat, frozen peas, diced carrot, diced potato (parboiled). Pour into a baking dish, top with pie crust (store-bought is fine), egg wash, vent slits. Bake at 400°F for 25-30 min until golden and bubbling. 1.5 lbs of picked meat is the perfect amount for one deep dish.

Squirrel Burgers

Easy
5+ squirrels ~1.5 hrs Feeds 2-3

★★★☆☆

Parboil, pick, and pulse the meat in a food processor. Mix 60/40 with pork fat or beef tallow (pure squirrel is too lean to bind). Season hard: garlic, onion powder, black pepper, smoked paprika, Worcestershire. Form thin smash patties. Sear in a ripping hot cast iron. Top with sharp cheddar, pickles, mustard. A novelty more than a staple, but people are always impressed. Need a lot of squirrels for a small number of burgers.

Feeding Squirrel to Dogs

Squirrel is an excellent treat and meal topper for dogs. It's lean, high-protein, and most dogs go absolutely nuts for it (pun intended). A few important rules:

Always remove all bones. Cooked small game bones splinter and are genuinely dangerous for dogs. Parboil the squirrel plain (no seasoning), pick the meat clean, and double-check for bone fragments. Raw bones are less likely to splinter but cooked bones are a real hazard, so just debone everything.

No seasoning. Plain parboiled squirrel meat only. No salt, no onion, no garlic (both toxic to dogs), no spices. The meat itself is the treat.

Portion appropriately. For a large breed dog (70+ lbs), one whole squirrel's worth of picked meat (2-3 oz) is a reasonable treat or meal topper, not a meal replacement. For small breeds, half a squirrel is plenty. The unseasoned stock (made without onion or garlic) poured over regular kibble is another easy way to use the byproduct and dogs love it.

Dedicating 1-2 bags from your freezer stash as "dog bags" is a great way to use your harvest. Parboil plain, pick, portion into ice cube trays or small containers, and freeze. Pop one out as a high-value training treat or meal topper.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is squirrel safe to eat?

Yes. Squirrel has been eaten by humans for centuries across the Americas and Europe. Cook it thoroughly to well-done (165°F+ internal temperature) to eliminate any parasites or bacteria. Avoid squirrels that appear sick, lethargic, or have visible lesions or mange. Healthy, active squirrels from your backyard are perfectly safe table fare.

What does squirrel taste like?

Dark meat with a slightly nutty, earthy flavor. Closest comparison is a cross between rabbit and dark-meat chicken thigh. It's mild for game meat. The flavor is influenced by diet: squirrels eating acorns and hickory nuts taste nuttier and richer than those eating bird seed. Most people's reaction is "this is way better than I expected."

Do I need a hunting license?

It depends on your state. Most states require a small game hunting license during the official season. However, many states allow landowners to take squirrels on their own property year-round without a license, especially if the squirrels are causing damage (raiding bird feeders counts in many jurisdictions). Check your state's wildlife agency website for specifics. North Carolina, for example, has a defined season but is lenient on property owners dealing with nuisance animals.

How many squirrels do I need for a meal?

For two people: 3-4 squirrels for a main dish like tacos or fried squirrel. 4-5 for a stew or dumplings (which stretches further with broth and vegetables). For a dinner party of 4-6 people, plan on 6-8 squirrels. When in doubt, cook more than you think. There's never leftover squirrel and dumplings.

Can I eat the brains?

Traditionally, squirrel brains were eaten (scrambled with eggs in parts of Appalachia). However, there is a documented (though rare) risk of Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease variant from consuming squirrel brains. Most modern hunters discard the head. The risk is very low but the reward is also low, so it's not worth it. Headshots also render this moot.

What about the hide?

Squirrel hides can be tanned and used for small craft projects, fly-tying material, or just for the satisfaction of using the whole animal. A simple salt-and-alum tan works: flesh the hide, salt it heavily for 24 hours, then soak in an alum/salt solution (1 lb alum, ½ lb salt per gallon of water) for 3-4 days. Rinse, stretch, and work the hide as it dries until it's soft. Small hides like squirrel are a great introduction to tanning.

Can I use a shotgun?

Yes, a .410 or 20-gauge with #6 shot is a traditional squirrel hunting setup, especially for treetop shots where you're shooting through leaves and need a spread pattern. The trade-off is picking shot out of the meat. A .22 or air rifle gives you clean meat with no pellets to worry about, but a shotgun is more forgiving on moving targets. Personal preference and situation.

Is this legal in my area?

Squirrel hunting is legal in all 50 US states during their respective seasons. Firearm discharge laws vary by municipality. Rural and unincorporated areas generally allow shooting on your own property. Suburban areas and HOA communities typically prohibit it. Air rifles fall into a gray area in many jurisdictions and are sometimes exempt from firearm discharge ordinances. Always verify your local county and municipal codes before shooting anything in your yard.

What about diseases? Should I be worried?

The main concerns are: Tularemia (rare, killed by cooking to 165°F), mange/mites (visible on the animal, don't harvest visibly sick squirrels), and internal parasites (killed by thorough cooking). Wear gloves while processing if you want to be extra cautious, and always wash your hands and equipment after handling raw game. If an animal looks sick, moves erratically, or has patchy fur and lesions, leave it alone. Healthy, active squirrels are safe to harvest and eat.
