♫ Audio Codecs

Audio Codecs

The lossy audio codec landscape spans 30 years, from the MP3 revolution to Opus dominance. The critical insight: the encoder implementation matters as much as the codec spec itself. A great encoder on an old codec beats a bad encoder on a new one.

The Hierarchy

Stereo music, equivalent bitrates Opus > xHE-AAC ≥ AAC-LC (Apple/FDK) > Vorbis ≈ WMA Pro > MP3 (LAME) > MP3 (other) > WMA Std

Very low bitrate (<64 kbps) Opus > xHE-AAC > AAC-HE v2 > AAC-HE v1 > everything else falls apart

"Transparent" means indistinguishable from CD in ABX testing. These numbers come from decades of Hydrogen Audio blind testing.

MP3 (MPEG-1 Audio Layer III)

Standardized 1993. The codec spec is just math. The encoder determines quality, and they vary wildly.

LAME
The only MP3 encoder anyone should use
Refined for 20+ years, effectively "solved." V0 (~245 kbps) is transparent. V2 (~190 kbps) is the sweet spot. CBR 320 is wasteful compared to V0 but psychologically comforting since VBR can't redistribute bits like CBR allocates uniformly.
open sourcelegacy but gold
Fraunhofer / Xing / BladeEnc
The bad old days
Fraunhofer (patent holders) made decent encoders. Xing and BladeEnc were absolute garbage. A lot of "MP3 sounds bad" from the late 90s came from these terrible encoders ripping at 128k CBR. That combination sounds genuinely awful.
avoid

MP3 tops out at 320 kbps, stereo only, 48 kHz max. Dead format in terms of development. Still the most universally compatible audio format in existence.

AAC Family

Not one codec but a family under the MPEG-4 umbrella with multiple profiles.

AAC-LC
Low Complexity — the "normal" AAC
Better spectral modeling than MP3, no 16 kHz low-pass at low bitrates. Transparent ~192 kbps with a good encoder. Used by iTunes Store, Apple Music lossy, YouTube audio.
patenteddominant
AAC-HE v1
High Efficiency — AAC-LC + SBR
Spectral Band Replication: encodes lows normally, reconstructs highs from a small metadata side-channel. Designed for 48-96 kbps. Great for streaming radio, podcasts. Actually worse than AAC-LC above 128k because SBR overhead.
patented48-96 kbps sweet spot
AAC-HE v2
HE v1 + Parametric Stereo
Encodes mono downmix + spatial parameters to reconstruct stereo image. Killer at 32-48 kbps. Used by many low-bitrate streaming services. Decent for speech, falls apart on complex stereo music.
patented32-48 kbps
xHE-AAC
Extended HE-AAC / USAC
Newest AAC profile. Seamlessly switches between speech and music coding modes. Decent quality at absurdly low bitrates (12-32 kbps). Used by newer streaming services and digital radio.
patentedrising
AAC-LD / ELD
Low Delay / Enhanced Low Delay
Optimized for real-time communication. Used in FaceTime, some VoIP. Quality-per-bit isn't the goal, latency is.
patentedreal-time

AAC encoder quality matters enormously. Apple's CoreAudio is the best (or tied). FDK-AAC (Fraunhofer, open source, ffmpeg default) is very good. Nero AAC was great, now abandoned. FAAC is garbage. ffmpeg's native AAC encoder was terrible for years, now acceptable but still behind.

Opus

Opus
RFC 6716 (2012) — IETF Standard
Built from CELT (Xiph, music) and SILK (Skype, speech), seamlessly blends between them. Handles everything: music, speech, low latency (5ms), low bitrate, high bitrate, mono through surround. At any given bitrate it matches or beats every other lossy codec. Patent-free, mandatory in WebRTC. Only downside: hardware support on old devices.
royalty-freeopen sourceking

Vorbis

Ogg Vorbis
Xiph.org, 2000
Xiph's patent-free answer to MP3. Quality between MP3 and AAC. q5 (~160 kbps) is good, q6 (~192 kbps) transparent for most. Won in gaming (Unreal Engine) and was Spotify's desktop codec for years. Opus obsoleted it. Xiph themselves created Opus as its successor.
royalty-freeopen sourcesuperseded by Opus

WMA

WMA Standard / Pro / Lossless / Voice
Microsoft — effectively dead
WMA Standard was MP3-competitive. WMA Pro was multi-channel, roughly AAC-LC tier at ~192 kbps. WMA Lossless was Microsoft's FLAC competitor. Voice was speech-optimized. Entire family is dead outside legacy Windows ecosystems.
proprietarydead

Others Worth Knowing

Musepack (MPC)
Audiophile darling, mid-2000s. Optimized purely for high-quality transparency. At ~180 kbps it was arguably the most transparent codec of its era. Tiny community, historical curiosity.
abandoned
AC-3 / Dolby Digital
Surround sound for film/TV/streaming. 384-640 kbps for 5.1. Found in DVDs, Blu-rays, streaming.
patentedsurround
E-AC-3 / Dolby Digital Plus
Improved AC-3 used by Netflix, Disney+, etc. Better quality at lower bitrates, supports Atmos metadata.
patentedstreaming standard
Dolby AC-4
Newest Dolby codec for broadcast TV (ATSC 3.0). Supports Atmos natively.
patentedemerging

Bluetooth Audio Codecs

SBC is mandatory baseline (mediocre). aptX / aptX HD / aptX Adaptive (Qualcomm) try to improve quality. LDAC (Sony) pushes up to 990 kbps for near-CD quality. LC3 is the new Bluetooth LE Audio standard and is genuinely good. AAC over Bluetooth works well on Apple devices, inconsistent on Android.

◧ Image Codecs

Image Codecs

Image codecs split into two worlds: lossless (archival, editing, pixel-perfect) and lossy (web delivery, storage reduction). Most modern formats support both. The current battle is AVIF vs JPEG XL, with WebP as the safe incumbent.

The Hierarchy

Lossy web delivery, size vs quality AVIF ≥ JPEG XL > HEIC > WebP > JPEG (jpegli) > JPEG (standard)

Lossless compression JPEG XL > WebP lossless ≈ AVIF lossless > PNG (optimized) > PNG (naive) > BMP / TIFF uncompressed

Browser support (Feb 2026) JPEG / PNG / WebP → 100% | AVIF → ~95% | JPEG XL → Safari native, Chrome 145 behind flag, Firefox behind flag

The Classics

JPEG
1992 — JPEG Committee
The original lossy photo format. DCT-based, 8-bit, no alpha, no animation. Still the most universally compatible image format. Gradually losing ground to modern formats, but will be a fallback for a decade more. jpegli (from the JPEG XL team) borrows JXL techniques to squeeze better quality from the old JPEG format, especially at high quality.
royalty-freeeternal legacy
PNG
1996 — Lossless
Lossless, supports alpha transparency. The standard for screenshots, UI elements, anything requiring pixel-perfect reproduction. Large files compared to modern lossless options. APNG adds animation support.
royalty-freeuniversal
GIF
1987 — 256 colors max
Limited to 256 colors per frame. Famous for animations. Objectively terrible by any modern metric. Use MP4/WebM for animations, WebP/AVIF for everything else. Culturally immortal, technically dead.
royalty-freetechnically dead
BMP
Uncompressed bitmap
No compression (or minimal RLE). Huge files. Only encountered in legacy Windows apps and as an intermediate format. No reason to use in 2026.
dead
TIFF
Tagged Image File Format
Supports virtually everything: lossy, lossless, multi-page, 16/32-bit, CMYK. Standard in printing, publishing, scientific imaging, GIS. Not a web format. Huge files but extremely versatile for professional workflows.
professionalprint/archival

The Modern Contenders

WebP
Google, 2010 — VP8-derived
Google's first attempt to replace JPEG/PNG. Supports lossy, lossless, alpha, and animation. 25-35% smaller than JPEG at equivalent quality. 100% browser support. The "safe bet" for web delivery. Starting to show its age: AVIF and JXL both outperform it, and lossy WebP can get blurry at low file sizes.
royalty-freeopen sourcesafe incumbent
AVIF
AV1-based, AOMedia, 2019
Derived from AV1 video codec. Excellent lossy compression, especially at low-to-medium quality where it beats everything. Supports 10/12-bit HDR, wide color gamut, alpha, animation. ~20% smaller than WebP at equivalent quality. Main drawbacks: slow encoding (CPU-intensive), no progressive loading, max dimensions limited. Currently the best format for web delivery.
royalty-freeopen sourceweb king 2026
JPEG XL
JPEG Committee, 2022
The true JPEG successor. Superior at high quality and lossless. Unique killer features: lossless JPEG transcoding (convert JPEG→JXL, save ~35%, convert back to pixel-perfect original), progressive loading (blurry-to-sharp like old JPEG), up to 32-bit HDR, huge dimensions, fast encode/decode. Uses XYB colorspace matching human cone response. Chrome 145 (Feb 2026) re-added support behind a flag after controversial 2022 removal. Safari supports natively. Firefox behind flag.
royalty-freeopen sourcethe comeback
HEIC / HEIF
HEVC-based, Apple's default
Based on HEVC (H.265) video codec. ~50% smaller than JPEG. Default capture format on iPhones since iOS 11. Supports HDR, depth maps, live photos. Patent-encumbered (HEVC licensing mess), minimal browser support, basically Apple-only in practice.
heavily patentedApple ecosystem

AVIF vs JPEG XL: who wins? AVIF wins at low-to-medium quality web delivery and has far better browser support right now. JPEG XL wins at high quality, lossless, archival, progressive loading, and professional photography. The answer is "use both" with <picture> fallbacks. JPEG XL's Chrome comeback (Feb 2026) may change the calculus significantly.

Specialty & Professional

RAW
Camera-specific sensor data
Not one format but hundreds (CR2, NEF, ARW, DNG, etc). Unprocessed sensor data preserving maximum dynamic range and editing flexibility. Large files. Essential for professional photography. DNG (Adobe) attempts to standardize.
professionalcamera-specific
SVG
Scalable Vector Graphics
XML-based vector format, not a raster codec. Resolution-independent. Perfect for logos, icons, illustrations, data visualizations. Infinitely scalable. Can be styled with CSS and manipulated with JavaScript.
open standardvector
QOI
Quite OK Image Format, 2021
Intentionally simple lossless format. ~300 lines of code. Compresses worse than PNG but encodes/decodes 20-50x faster. Niche use: game engines and real-time applications where speed matters more than size.
public domainopen source
WebP 2
Google's abandoned successor
Google's attempt to leapfrog AVIF. VP9-derived. Development was abandoned. AVIF and JPEG XL made it redundant before it shipped.
cancelled
▶ Video Codecs

Video Codecs

Video compression is where the real money and politics live. Each generation roughly halves the bitrate of the previous one for equivalent quality. But codec adoption is driven as much by patent licensing, hardware support, and ecosystem momentum as by technical merit.

The Hierarchy

Compression efficiency at equivalent quality (2026) VVC (H.266) > AV1 ≈ HEVC (H.265) > VP9 > AVC (H.264) >> MPEG-2

Real-world adoption (2026) AVC (H.264) → plays everywhere, still #1 | HEVC (H.265) → 4K/HDR standard | AV1 → YouTube, Netflix, growing fast | VP9 → YouTube legacy | VVC (H.266) → near-zero deployment

The Generational Timeline

1995
MPEG-2 / H.262 — DVDs, digital TV, early satellite. Still used in broadcast. The codec that brought digital video to living rooms.
2003
H.264 / AVC (MPEG-4 Part 10) — The king. Blu-ray, streaming, video calls, surveillance, everything. Single most important video codec ever created. ~50% better than MPEG-2. Patent pool exists but licensing is relatively clear.
2008
VP8 — Google (acquired On2). Open source. Basis for WebP images. Decent but couldn't unseat H.264.
2012
VP9 — Google's answer to HEVC. Royalty-free. YouTube adopted it massively. ~30-40% better than H.264. Hardware decode in most modern devices.
2013
HEVC / H.265 — ~50% better than H.264. 4K/HDR standard. Disastrous patent licensing: multiple competing pools, unclear costs, companies sued. This fragmentation directly caused the creation of AV1.
2018
AV1 — Alliance for Open Media (Amazon, Apple, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Netflix, Nvidia, Samsung). Royalty-free response to HEVC's licensing disaster. ~30% better than HEVC. Slow to encode but hardware support growing fast.
2020
VVC / H.266 — ~50% better than HEVC. Technically excellent but effectively dead on arrival: no killer use case (8K isn't mainstream), patent licensing fragmented again, and AV1 exists as the free alternative.
~2028
AV2 — AOMedia's next generation, expected late 2025/2026 spec, deployment years later. And H.267 / "Beyond VVC" — JVET's next standard with two tracks: ECM (evolutionary, 25%+ over VVC) and NNVC (neural network-based, revolutionary). Both years from deployment.

Detailed Breakdown

H.264 / AVC
MPEG-4 Part 10, 2003
Plays literally everywhere. Every phone, every browser, every TV, every camera, every drone, every webcam. Still the default for compatibility. x264 is the gold-standard open-source encoder, one of the most optimized pieces of software ever written. Hardware encode/decode is universal.
patented (clear licensing)universal standard
HEVC / H.265
2013 — 4K/HDR era
~50% bitrate savings over H.264 at equivalent quality. The codec behind 4K streaming, HDR, Apple's ecosystem (FaceTime, iPhones shoot HEVC by default). x265 is the main open-source encoder. Patent licensing is a fragmented mess with multiple competing pools (Access Advance, Via-LA) and many essential patent holders outside any pool. This mess directly caused AV1's creation.
fragmented patents4K/HDR standard
VP9
Google, 2013
Google's royalty-free HEVC competitor. YouTube's primary codec for years. ~30-40% better than H.264, roughly on par with HEVC. Hardware decode widespread. Being gradually replaced by AV1 on YouTube but still serving tons of traffic.
royalty-freeopen sourcebeing replaced by AV1
AV1
Alliance for Open Media, 2018
The industry's collective middle finger to HEVC's patent mess. ~30% better than HEVC. Royalty-free. Backed by every major tech company. YouTube, Netflix, Meta all deploying. Hardware decode in newer chips (Intel 12th+, AMD RDNA 3, Nvidia RTX 40+, Apple M3+, MediaTek Dimensity). SVT-AV1 (Intel/Netflix) is the dominant encoder. libaom is the reference. Encoding is CPU-intensive but improving rapidly. The clear next universal standard after H.264.
royalty-freeopen sourcethe next universal
VVC / H.266
2020 — Technically superior, practically stuck
~50% better compression than HEVC, ~10-20% better than AV1. Supports up to 16K, 360-degree video, HDR. But: encoding is 5-10x more complex than HEVC, patent licensing is fragmented again (Access Advance, Via-LA, plus many holdouts), no killer use case (8K isn't here yet), and AV1 is free. Near-zero streaming deployments. Brazil's TV 3.0 and ATSC 3.0 adopted it for broadcast. Hardware decode starting in Intel Lunar Lake, MediaTek Pentonic chips. May find a niche in broadcast but unlikely to become mainstream for streaming.
fragmented patentslimbo
EVC (Essential Video Coding)
MPEG-5 Part 1
Designed with a royalty-free baseline profile and a patent-encumbered main profile. Trying to split the difference. Little progress, minimal adoption. Not on anyone's radar.
irrelevant
LCEVC
MPEG-5 Part 2 — Enhancement layer
Not a standalone codec. An enhancement layer that sits on top of any base codec (H.264, HEVC, AV1) to add quality. Encode at lower resolution, LCEVC upscales with residual correction. Used in Brazil's TV 3.0 alongside VVC. Niche but clever.
patentedniche

Surround & Cinema Codecs

MPEG-2
1995 — DVDs, broadcast TV
Still used in over-the-air broadcast, DVDs, and some satellite. Extremely inefficient by modern standards. Hardware decode universal. Being phased out of broadcast as ATSC 3.0 rolls out.
patents expiredbroadcast holdover
ProRes
Apple — Professional editing
Visually lossless editing codec. Designed for fast decode during editing, not for delivery. Multiple profiles from Proxy to 4444 XQ. Standard in the Apple/Final Cut Pro ecosystem. iPhones can shoot ProRes.
Apple proprietaryediting codec
DNxHR / DNxHD
Avid — Professional editing
Avid's answer to ProRes. Visually lossless editing codec. Cross-platform (unlike ProRes which was Apple-only for encoding until recently). Standard in Avid Media Composer workflows.
Avid proprietaryediting codec
FFV1
Lossless archival
Mathematically lossless video codec from FFmpeg. Used by archives (Library of Congress, national film archives). Compresses ~50% vs uncompressed with zero quality loss. Not for delivery.
royalty-freeopen sourcearchival

Containers vs Codecs

A container is not a codec. MP4 is a container that can hold H.264, H.265, AV1, AAC, Opus, etc. MKV (Matroska) can hold virtually anything. WebM is a restricted MKV that holds VP8/VP9/AV1 + Vorbis/Opus. AVI is ancient. MOV is Apple's container. The container handles muxing, chapters, subtitles, metadata. The codec handles compression. When someone says "an MP4 file," you still don't know which video codec is inside.

⚡ The Future

Neural & Next-Gen Codecs

The next frontier in compression replaces hand-crafted algorithms with learned neural networks. This is where things get genuinely exciting and genuinely uncertain.

Neural Video Codecs (NVCs)

Neural video codecs use deep learning architectures (primarily Variational Autoencoders and Implicit Neural Representations) instead of traditional block-based DCT transforms. The state of the art in 2025/2026:

DCVC Family
Deep Contextual Video Compression
The leading NVC research line. DCVC-FM and DCVC-RT achieve state-of-the-art compression. Latest research (CVPR 2025) shows ~22% bitrate reduction over H.266/VVC on standard benchmarks. DCVC-RT is the first NVC deployable for real-time coding on consumer GPUs. Still requires GPU hardware for practical speeds.
research frontierGPU required
NNVC Track (H.267)
Neural Network Video Coding
One of the two tracks for the H.267 "Beyond VVC" standard (~2028). Instead of evolving traditional block-based coding, NNVC aims to replace it entirely with learned models. Revolutionary potential but massive practical challenges: cross-device consistency, deterministic floating-point math, rate control, hardware deployment.
~2028+ standard

Reality check on neural codecs: Despite beating VVC in academic benchmarks, full neural video compression hasn't beaten well-tuned conventional encoders in real-world conditions with human opinion scores. Traditional encoders enhanced with AI tools (like AI-assisted encoding decisions) currently outperform pure neural codecs. AI and conventional approaches will work in tandem for the foreseeable future. Pure neural codecs are "black boxes" that can't be debugged, which is a serious practical concern. The compute requirements are orders of magnitude higher than conventional encoders.

Neural Image Codecs

Neural image compression is further along than video. Learned image codecs have achieved genuine state-of-the-art performance in both MSE-optimized and perceptual quality metrics. Google's work on combining autoencoders with diffusion models enables extreme compression (very low bitrates) while maintaining perceptual quality through generative reconstruction. However, these still require significant GPU compute for encoding and often for decoding too.

AI-Enhanced Traditional Codecs

The more practical near-term approach. Instead of replacing codecs, AI enhances them:

AI-assisted encoding
ML models optimize encoding decisions: per-scene bitrate allocation, content-aware quantization, optimal mode decisions. Companies like Beamr, Bitmovin, and the major streaming services use this today. 10-30% bitrate savings on top of conventional codecs.
deployed today
Neural super-resolution
Encode at lower resolution, use ML upscaling on decode. NVIDIA's DLSS concept applied to video. LCEVC is a standardized approach. Practical today with modern GPUs.
growing
Video Coding for Machines (VCM)
MPEG-AI standard
Compression optimized for machine analysis rather than human viewing. Designed for surveillance, autonomous vehicles, IoT. Different optimization targets: object detection accuracy rather than human perceptual quality.
emerging standard

The pattern is clear: HEVC and AV1 will remain dominant for several more years. Next-gen codecs (AV2, VVC, H.267) will ramp slowly because there's no killer use case driving adoption the way 4K/HDR drove HEVC. The real revolution may come from AI-enhanced encoding rather than new codec standards.

⚖ Licensing

The Patent Wars

You can't understand codec adoption without understanding licensing. Patents have killed or delayed better technology repeatedly.

CodecLicensingImpact
H.264MPEG LA pool, relatively clearWidely adopted, predictable costs
HEVC3 competing pools + holdoutsFragmentation directly caused AV1's creation
VP9 / AV1Royalty-free (AOMedia)Industry rallied behind free alternative
VVC2 pools + major holdouts (Apple, Google, Microsoft...)Unclear costs, slowing adoption further
Opus / VorbisRoyalty-freeUniversal adoption in web/VoIP
JPEG XL / AVIFRoyalty-freeNo licensing friction
AACPatented but licensing establishedStandard but costs factored in

The lesson: royalty-free codecs with backing from major tech companies win the long game. HEVC's licensing disaster is the cautionary tale that reshaped the entire industry. Every codec decision since has been influenced by it. AV1 exists because HEVC got licensing wrong. JPEG XL and AVIF are both royalty-free specifically because the image world learned from video's mistakes.
