🎯The big picture

The American criminal justice system has three components: police (enforcement), courts (adjudication), corrections (punishment/rehabilitation). CJC-111 surveys all three. CJC-112 asks why people commit crime (theory). CJC-113 zooms into the parallel system for offenders under 18.

These three classes share core vocabulary. Terms like parens patriae, due process, discretion, recidivism, and deterrence recur across all three. Learn them once, use them three times.

For your career trajectory (PPO/WLEO/municipal LE in Wake County), CJC-113 is the dark horse. Wildlife officers handle juvenile violators constantly (underage hunting/fishing without licenses, alcohol on public lands, ATV violations). Probation officers half their workload is juveniles or young adults aging out. Pay extra attention to NC-specific juvenile law in CJC-113.

01The three components

Every criminal case moves through three interconnected systems. They're often called the "criminal justice trilogy."

ComponentFunctionKey Agencies
PoliceDetect crime, arrest suspects, gather evidence, maintain orderMunicipal PD, Sheriff, State Highway Patrol, FBI, ATF
CourtsDetermine guilt, impose sentences, review legal questionsMagistrate, District, Superior, Court of Appeals, Supreme
CorrectionsCarry out sentences (incarceration, probation, parole)Jails, prisons, probation/parole offices, halfway houses

These three are sometimes called a "loosely coupled system" because each operates with significant independence — police decide who to arrest, prosecutors decide who to charge, judges decide who's convicted, corrections decides how time is served. Discretion (the power to make decisions about how to act) operates at every level.

02The CJ process flow

Most students get this wrong on exams because the order is fiddly. Memorize this:

1Crime committed
2Investigation
3Arrest
4Booking
5Initial appearance
6Preliminary hearing / grand jury
7Arraignment
8Plea / trial
9Sentencing
10Corrections
11Release / parole

What happens at each stage

Booking: Suspect's info, fingerprints, photo recorded; held in jail.

Initial appearance: Within 48 hours of arrest. Judge advises of charges and rights, sets bail.

Preliminary hearing: Judge determines whether probable cause exists. In NC, felonies usually go to grand jury instead.

Arraignment: Defendant formally hears charges, enters plea (guilty, not guilty, no contest/nolo contendere).

Sentencing: If convicted, judge imposes punishment per NC's Structured Sentencing grid.

03Crime control vs Due process models

Herbert Packer's 1968 framework. Every CJ policy question is fundamentally a tension between these two:

Crime Control ModelDue Process Model
PriorityEfficiency, quick convictionProtect individual rights
AssumptionPolice got the right personInnocent until proven guilty
Metaphor"Assembly line""Obstacle course"
FavorsPlea bargains, fewer appealsTrials, appeals, exclusionary rules

Both models exist in tension in every system. The Warren Court (1953-69) pushed due process. Modern policy has swung back toward crime control. Exam tip: When asked which model favors a given practice (Miranda warnings, plea bargains, exclusionary rule, mandatory minimums), think about whether it protects rights or speeds conviction.

04History — brief but testable

English common law roots: American CJ system inherited from England. Jury trial, habeas corpus, presumption of innocence — all English.

Sir Robert Peel (1829): Founded the Metropolitan Police in London. The "Peelian Principles" still shape modern policing — police are citizens in uniform, legitimacy comes from public consent, prevention over reaction.

August Vollmer (1905-1932): Father of American professional policing. Berkeley CA chief. Pushed for college-educated officers, scientific investigation, polygraph use.

Three eras of American policing:

  1. Political era (1840s-1920s) — police were political party employees, corrupt
  2. Reform era (1920s-1970s) — professionalization, separation from politics, "thin blue line"
  3. Community era (1980s-present) — community policing, problem-solving

05Levels and jurisdiction

Federal: FBI (broad investigative), DEA (drugs), ATF (alcohol/tobacco/firearms/explosives), US Marshals (fugitives, witness protection), Secret Service (counterfeiting + protection), ICE/CBP (immigration).

State: NC State Highway Patrol (traffic + general LE on highways), SBI (State Bureau of Investigation — major crimes, support for locals), ALE (Alcohol Law Enforcement), WRC Wildlife Enforcement.

Local: County Sheriff (elected; runs jail, serves civil process, patrols unincorporated areas), Municipal PD (city/town police), Campus police.

Limited jurisdiction: Park rangers, transit police, school resource officers.

Wake County practical: Garner PD (where Binns works) handles inside town limits. Wake County Sheriff handles unincorporated areas + runs the Detention Center. SHP handles I-40, US-70. Multiple agencies overlap constantly — interagency cooperation is essential.

06Courts structure

Dual court system: Federal courts AND state courts run in parallel. Most criminal cases go through state courts.

NC court structure (bottom up):

  1. Magistrate Court — first stop for most arrests; sets bail, issues warrants
  2. District Court — misdemeanors, traffic, juvenile, civil under $25K. No jury for criminal cases.
  3. Superior Court — felonies, civil over $25K. Jury trials.
  4. Court of Appeals — reviews lower court decisions (15 judges)
  5. NC Supreme Court — final state appeal (7 justices, elected)

Federal: US District Court → US Court of Appeals (NC is in 4th Circuit) → US Supreme Court.

07Corrections

Jail vs Prison: Jails are county-run, hold pretrial detainees and sentences under 1 year. Prisons are state or federal, hold sentences over 1 year.

Probation: Court-supervised release in the community instead of incarceration. Conditions apply (drug testing, curfews, employment, no new offenses). In NC, supervised by the NC Department of Adult Correction, Community Corrections division — this is what Bekah's PPO job target is.

Parole: Conditional release after serving part of a prison sentence. NC abolished discretionary parole in 1994 with Structured Sentencing, but post-release supervision still exists.

Five goals of punishment: retribution (deserved), deterrence (specific = offender, general = others), incapacitation (prevent future crime), rehabilitation (change behavior), restoration (repair harm to victim/community).

CJC-112 · Scott Davis (M.A.) · Online
Criminology asks why crime happens. This is theory-heavy and reading-dense. Davis is rated for engaging lectures but he's a "tough grader" — meaning he expects you to use theory names correctly and apply them. Memorize the theory names and main thinkers cold.

01What is criminology?

Criminology is the scientific study of crime, criminals, and society's response. Coined by Raffaele Garofalo (1885). Distinct from criminal justice (which studies the system).

Three core questions criminologists ask:

  1. How much crime is there, and what kinds? (crime measurement)
  2. Why do people commit crime? (theories of causation)
  3. How should society respond? (policy)

02Measuring crime

Three main sources. Each has blind spots.

SourceWho runs itWhat it measuresWeakness
UCR (Uniform Crime Reports)FBI, since 1930Crimes reported to policeMisses unreported crime
NCVS (Nat'l Crime Victimization Survey)BJS, since 1973Victim self-reports via household surveyMisses homicide, drugs
NIBRS (Nat'l Incident-Based Reporting)FBI, replacing UCRDetailed incident-level dataNot all agencies participate yet

Dark figure of crime: The amount of crime that goes unreported and unrecorded. Estimated to be very large — most assaults, sexual assaults, and minor thefts never enter the system.

UCR Part I crimes (Index crimes): Homicide, rape, robbery, aggravated assault (violent); burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft, arson (property). These are the "serious eight."

03Classical School

The first systematic theory of crime. 18th century Enlightenment thinkers.

Cesare Beccaria — Italian, wrote On Crimes and Punishments (1764). Key ideas: crime is a rational choice; punishment should be swift, certain, and proportionate; the goal is deterrence, not revenge; cruel punishment is counterproductive. Influenced the US Constitution.

Jeremy Bentham — English philosopher. Developed utilitarianism and the hedonistic calculus — people weigh pleasure against pain before acting. Crime is rational if the expected benefit exceeds the expected punishment. Designed the panopticon prison.

Modern descendant: Rational choice theory and deterrence theory — increase certainty and severity of punishment to reduce crime.

04Positivist School

19th-century reaction to Classical. Argued criminals are different from non-criminals — not just rational actors making bad choices. Look for causes beyond free will.

Cesare Lombroso — Italian physician, The Criminal Man (1876). Claimed criminals are biologically distinct — "born criminals" with atavistic (primitive evolutionary) features. Wrong on the science, but founded the field of biological criminology.

Three branches of positivism:

  • Biological — genetics, brain structure, hormones (testosterone), neurochemistry
  • Psychological — personality disorders, low intelligence, psychopathy, childhood trauma
  • Sociological — social structure, environment, group dynamics (the dominant modern approach)

05Social structure theories

These say crime is caused by position in society, especially poverty and inequality.

Strain theory (Robert Merton, 1938)

Society teaches everyone to pursue the same goals (American Dream — wealth, success) but doesn't give everyone equal legitimate means. The gap creates strain. People adapt in five ways:

  1. Conformity — accept goals, accept means (most people)
  2. Innovation — accept goals, reject means → crime, especially property crime
  3. Ritualism — reject goals, accept means (going through the motions)
  4. Retreatism — reject both (addicts, vagrants)
  5. Rebellion — replace both with new goals/means (revolutionaries)

Robert Agnew's General Strain Theory (1992): Updated Merton. Three sources of strain: failure to achieve goals, removal of positive stimuli (losing a relationship), presentation of negative stimuli (abuse). Strain produces negative emotions → coping via crime.

Social disorganization (Chicago School, 1920s)

Shaw and McKay mapped delinquency in Chicago. Found high-crime zones persisted regardless of which ethnic group lived there. Concluded crime is rooted in neighborhood characteristics — poverty, residential turnover, ethnic heterogeneity — that weaken informal social control.

Concentric zone theory: Cities have rings — innermost "zone in transition" (rooming houses, factories) has highest crime; outer rings (suburbs) lowest.

06Social process theories

Crime is learned through interaction.

Differential association (Edwin Sutherland, 1939)

Crime is learned, like any behavior. Nine propositions, key ones:

  • Criminal behavior is learned through interaction in intimate personal groups
  • What's learned includes techniques AND motives/rationalizations/attitudes
  • A person becomes delinquent because of an excess of definitions favorable to law violation over unfavorable
  • Differential associations vary in frequency, duration, priority, intensity

Social learning theory (Ronald Akers)

Extends Sutherland by adding operant conditioning — behavior is shaped by rewards and punishments. Four components: differential association, definitions, differential reinforcement, imitation.

Social control / social bond theory (Travis Hirschi, 1969)

Asks the opposite question: why don't people commit crime? Answer: social bonds hold them back. Four elements:

  • Attachment — emotional ties to family, friends, school
  • Commitment — investment in conventional activities (career, education)
  • Involvement — time spent in legitimate activities ("idle hands")
  • Belief — acceptance of the moral validity of laws

Weak bonds → higher likelihood of crime.

Self-control theory (Gottfredson and Hirschi, 1990)

"General Theory of Crime." Low self-control, established in childhood (~age 8) through poor parenting, explains all crime across all types and ages. Low self-control traits: impulsivity, risk-seeking, short-term focus, low frustration tolerance.

07Labeling theory

Howard Becker, Edwin Lemert — 1960s. Crime isn't inherent in an act; it's defined by society's reaction. The act of labeling someone "criminal" (especially through formal CJ contact) produces:

  • Primary deviance — the original act, often situational
  • Secondary deviance — behavior caused by the label itself (the "criminal" embraces the identity, opportunities close off, returns to crime)

Policy implication: minimize formal system contact, especially for juveniles. Big influence on juvenile justice diversion programs (relevant to CJC-113).

08Routine activities theory

Cohen and Felson (1979). Crime requires three things converging in time and space:

  1. A motivated offender
  2. A suitable target (visible, accessible, valuable, inertia-low)
  3. The absence of a capable guardian

Practical theory — explains why crime rates rose in the 1960s-70s (more women in workforce = homes empty, more valuable portable goods like TVs/cars) and underlies modern situational crime prevention (CCTV, lighting, target hardening).

09Conflict / critical theories

Marxist roots. Crime reflects class conflict; law is a tool of the powerful to control the powerless.

Karl Marx, Willem Bonger, Richard Quinney — different versions. Modern descendants include critical race theory, feminist criminology, peacemaking criminology. Often appear on exams as "the powerful define what crime is."

10Crime typologies

Violent crime: Homicide, rape, robbery, assault. Often driven by relationships, intoxication, conflict escalation.

Property crime: Burglary, larceny, motor vehicle theft, arson. Higher rate than violent but lower clearance rate.

White-collar crime: Edwin Sutherland coined the term (1939). Financial crimes committed by people of high social status in the course of their occupation — fraud, embezzlement, insider trading.

Organized crime: Continuing enterprise with formal hierarchy, monopolizes illegal markets through force/corruption.

Cybercrime: Computer-based offenses. Fastest growing category.

Victimless / public order crime: Drugs, prostitution, gambling. Controversial — are they really victimless?

CJC-113 · Chief John Slaughter (Wendell PD) · Online
The juvenile system is a parallel system to the adult system, founded on totally different philosophy. Slaughter being a current chief means real-world examples and likely heavy NC-specific content. Master the case law and NC's Raise the Age Act — those will be exam gold.

01Core concept: parens patriae

Parens patriae — Latin for "parent of the country." Legal doctrine holding that the state has a duty to act as guardian for those who cannot protect themselves, especially children. This is the philosophical foundation of the entire juvenile system.

Under parens patriae, the juvenile court isn't trying to punish — it's trying to help. This explains every structural difference from the adult system:

  • Different terminology (delinquent not criminal; adjudication not conviction; disposition not sentencing)
  • Confidential proceedings, sealed records
  • Indeterminate, treatment-focused outcomes
  • No jury trial (historically)
  • Judge has broad discretion

02History

Pre-1800s: Children treated as miniature adults. Common law rule: under 7 = no criminal capacity; 7-14 = rebuttable presumption of incapacity; 14+ = treated as adult.

1825 — House of Refuge, NYC: First institution specifically for delinquent and neglected youth. Reformatory movement begins.

1899 — Cook County, IL: First juvenile court in the US. Spread nationally within 25 years. Founded on parens patriae, informal, no due process protections.

1960s-70s — Due Process Revolution: Supreme Court found the informal system was failing kids — treating them like adults in terms of confinement but giving them fewer rights. Series of cases imposed adult-style protections.

1990s — "Get tough" era: Spike in juvenile violence (myth of "superpredators"). States lowered transfer ages, increased adult court waivers, harsh sentencing.

2005-present — Developmental approach: Brain science (prefrontal cortex not mature until ~25) drives reforms. Supreme Court bans death penalty + life-without-parole for juveniles. NC raises age of jurisdiction.

03The landmark cases (memorize these)

Kent v. United States (1966)
Procedural rights at waiver/transfer hearings: hearing, counsel, access to records, written statement of reasons. First major Supreme Court juvenile case.
In re Gault (1967)
The most important juvenile case. 15-year-old Gerald Gault sentenced to ~6 years in industrial school for an obscene phone call (adult would've gotten max $50 fine or 2 months). Supreme Court applied four due process rights to juvenile delinquency hearings: notice of charges, right to counsel, right to confront/cross-examine witnesses, privilege against self-incrimination.
In re Winship (1970)
Standard of proof in juvenile adjudication = beyond a reasonable doubt (same as adult criminal). Raised from "preponderance of evidence."
McKeiver v. Pennsylvania (1971)
Juveniles do NOT have a constitutional right to jury trial in juvenile court. (Some states grant it by statute; NC does not for juvenile delinquency.)
Breed v. Jones (1975)
Double jeopardy protection applies — can't be adjudicated delinquent in juvenile court, then waived to adult court for the same offense.
Schall v. Martin (1984)
Preventive detention of juveniles before adjudication is constitutional if proper procedures are followed.
Roper v. Simmons (2005)
Eighth Amendment bans the death penalty for offenses committed under age 18.
Graham v. Florida (2010)
Eighth Amendment bans life without parole for juveniles convicted of non-homicide offenses.
Miller v. Alabama (2012)
Eighth Amendment bans mandatory life without parole for juveniles, even in homicide cases. Discretionary LWOP still allowed but rare.
JDB v. North Carolina (2011)
NC case. Age is relevant when determining whether Miranda warnings are required — a reasonable child may feel "in custody" when an adult wouldn't.

05Categories of juvenile offenses

Delinquent acts — would be crimes if committed by an adult. Theft, assault, drug offenses, etc.

Status offenses — only illegal because of age. Truancy, running away, curfew violations, underage drinking, "ungovernable" behavior. Unique to juvenile system.

Undisciplined juveniles (NC term) — youth who are habitually disobedient, truant, or runaways. Handled differently than delinquents in NC.

Abuse, neglect, dependency — victims, not offenders. Different court track (DSS-led) but related system.

06NC's Juvenile Justice System

NC Raise the Age (effective Dec 1, 2019)

Before 2019, NC was one of only TWO states that prosecuted 16- and 17-year-olds as adults automatically. The Juvenile Justice Reinvestment Act raised the age of juvenile jurisdiction from 15 to 17 for most offenses. Key details:

  • Covers offenses committed at age 16-17, with some exceptions
  • Most Class A-G felonies and some misdemeanors involving violence still get transferred to adult court
  • Class A felonies (first-degree murder) are required to be transferred
  • Below age 16 — almost always juvenile jurisdiction (with rare transfer)

Minimum age

NC sets juvenile court jurisdiction starting at age 6 (raised from 7 in 2021). Below 6 → no juvenile court intake.

Stages of NC juvenile process

  1. Intake — court counselor evaluates whether to divert, file petition, or close. Diversion is preferred for first-time/low-level cases.
  2. Secure custody decision — like adult bail, but extra-protective standards
  3. First appearance — for felonies, like adult initial appearance
  4. Probable cause hearing — for felonies allegedly committed at 13+
  5. Transfer hearing — for cases where adult court waiver is sought
  6. Adjudicatory hearing — bench trial, beyond reasonable doubt
  7. Dispositional hearing — judge imposes outcome from disposition options
  8. Review hearings — ongoing

Disposition levels in NC

NC uses a structured disposition grid — like adult Structured Sentencing. Three levels based on offense severity + delinquency history:

  • Level 1 — Community: Probation up to 12 months, community service, restitution, counseling, electronic monitoring
  • Level 2 — Intermediate: Intensive supervision, day treatment, multisystemic therapy, short-term YDC stay possible
  • Level 3 — Commitment: Indefinite commitment to a Youth Development Center (YDC)

YDCs (formerly "training schools") are run by the NC Department of Public Safety, Division of Juvenile Justice.

07Waiver / transfer to adult court

Three mechanisms by which a juvenile case ends up in adult court:

  1. Judicial waiver — juvenile court judge decides to transfer (after Kent-style hearing)
  2. Statutory exclusion / mandatory transfer — certain offenses/ages excluded from juvenile court by statute (Class A felonies in NC)
  3. Prosecutorial discretion / "direct file" — prosecutor chooses where to file. NC does not use direct file.

08Key theories applied to juveniles

You'll see CJC-112 theories applied in CJC-113. The strongest fits for delinquency:

  • Social bond theory (Hirschi) — kids with weak attachments, low school commitment, lots of unstructured time, and weak belief in laws are at higher risk
  • Differential association / social learning — peer influence is the single strongest predictor of teen delinquency
  • Labeling theory — formal CJ contact in adolescence damages identity and outcomes; basis for diversion programs
  • Strain theory — school failure, family problems, lost opportunities = strain → coping via delinquency
  • Developmental / life-course criminology (Moffitt) — distinguishes adolescence-limited offenders (most teens, age-out) from life-course persistent offenders (small group, lifelong)

09Major treatment and prevention programs

Diversion programs — keep youth out of formal system. Teen courts, restorative justice circles, mediation.

Multisystemic Therapy (MST) — evidence-based intensive family- and community-based therapy for serious delinquents. Reduces recidivism.

Functional Family Therapy (FFT) — short-term family therapy. Evidence-based.

Drug courts / mental health courts — specialty court alternatives focused on treatment.

Boot camps and Scared Straight — do not work. Multiple meta-analyses show no effect or worse outcomes. Politically popular, scientifically discredited. Know this — common exam trap.
