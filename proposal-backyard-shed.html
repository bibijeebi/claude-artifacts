Proposal #015

Backyard Shed Build

A hand-built 12x12 stick-frame shed, sized to swallow the ride mower, push mower, welder and long tools. Built from the ground up for the experience of building it.

Total Cost $2,000–$4,500
Difficulty 4/5
Time Several weekends
Category Home
Status Proposed

Bottom Line Up Front

A 12x12x12 footprint keeps the build under Wake County's building-permit threshold, so no structural permit, plan review, or inspection. But it is not permit-free: a Land Use Permit from Wake Planning & Zoning still likely applies. This is a storage-only shed, mowers, tools, and the kayaks. Welding stays in the garage or out on the brick patio, so the foundation lands on a gravel pad with pressure-treated skids and a framed wood floor: cheaper, faster, fully DIY, and exactly right for storage. DIY materials land around $2,000–$3,200.

The Goal

The garage is over capacity and also needs cleaning out. A dedicated shed pulls the bulky outdoor gear off the garage floor and gives the welder a real home. The build itself is half the point. This is stick-framed by hand, not a plastic kit and not a flat-pack panel shed, because the experience of framing, sheathing, and roofing a real structure is the deliverable as much as the shed is.

What goes in it

Ride-on mower (the space driver, needs a wide door and a ramp), push mower, the welder and a welding bench, and long-handled tools like the weed eater on wall hangers or in an overhead loft. The existing deck box stays separate and keeps holding what it already holds. Some overflow can still go to the garage once the garage gets cleaned out, but the shed should stand on its own for the mowers and the welder.

Permits & Code (Wake County)

Accessory buildings with any dimension greater than 12 feet require a building permit, and buildings that do not exceed 12 feet in any dimension may still require a Land Use Permit from Wake County Planning and Zoning. So the 12x12x12 plan is correct on the building-permit front, that is the right number to design around, but it is not a free pass.

Three things still apply even at 12x12x12:
  • Land Use Permit from Wake P&Z. Cheap and quick, but get it before you build, not after.
  • Electrical permit the moment you run power. Electrical, plumbing, or mechanical work requires separate permits in addition to any shed permit. A welder circuit absolutely counts.
  • Setbacks. Wake County requires sheds to sit a minimum of 5 feet from side and rear property lines, and a minimum of 5 feet from all parts of a septic system. If the build site is near the drain field, the placement has to clear it.

Also worth a five-minute check before you start: the Stamey's Walk HOA, if there is an active one, may have its own rules on shed size, siding, and placement, and those can be stricter than the county. A site plan showing lot lines, existing and proposed structures, and impervious surfaces is part of the county process, and a recent mortgage or refinance survey works fine as the base for that.

The Build (Phased)

Each phase is a clean stopping point. You can spread this over several weekends and the shell is weather-tight well before the finish work.

Phase 1: Foundation $550–$1,100

Per the decision above. Slab: form a 12x12 pad, gravel base, wire mesh or rebar, ~1.8 yards of concrete, screed and float it. Gravel pad: excavate, compact crushed stone, set pressure-treated 4x6 skids dead level. This phase sets the tone, take the time to get it square and level because every later phase inherits its mistakes.

Phase 2: Floor Platform $0–$500

Slab build skips this entirely, the slab is the floor. Gravel/pier build: frame the floor with pressure-treated 2x6 joists at 16 inches on center and deck it with 3/4 inch pressure-treated plywood. This becomes your work platform for framing the walls.

Phase 3: Wall Framing & Sheathing $350–$550

Four stud walls, 2x4 at 16 inches on center, 7 to 8 foot wall height. Frame each wall flat on the deck or slab, stand it, brace it, tie them together. Build the wide door opening into the front wall now with a proper header. Sheath the walls. This is the most satisfying phase, the box becomes a building in an afternoon.

Phase 4: Roof $450–$750

Gable roof to make use of the full 12 foot height allowance and give you headroom plus a possible loft. Stick-frame the rafters with a ridge board or order simple trusses, sheath with OSB, then felt and either architectural shingles or ribbed metal panels. Metal is faster and lasts longer, shingles are cheaper and easier to learn on.

Loft option: the gable gives you free vertical space. A partial loft across the back, framed from 2x6, holds the weed eater and long-handled tools overhead and keeps the floor clear for the mowers. Costs almost nothing in extra lumber.
Phase 5: Siding, Trim & Paint $350–$650

T1-11 plywood siding is the classic DIY shed skin, cheap, fast, and you paint it to match the house. LP SmartSide is a step up in durability for more money. Trim the corners and openings, caulk every seam, prime and paint. Paint is not optional, it is what keeps water out of the sheathing.

Phase 6: Doors & Ramp $150–$400

The ride mower sets the door spec. A single 3 foot man door will not pass it. Build a double barn-style door for a 5 to 6 foot clear opening, or hang a roll-up. Build a ramp from pressure-treated lumber so the mower rolls straight in. Verify the opening against the actual mower deck width before you frame it, not after.

Kayak storage: two 10-foot Lifetime Tamarack Anglers, roughly 57 lbs each, store cleanly against one 12-foot side wall. Mount them on edge in wall cradles, or on padded horizontal arms, stacked one above the other and lagged into the studs. That packs about 20 feet of boat into a 3-foot strip of wall and keeps the floor clear for the mowers and the welder. Budget $30–$60 for a set of heavy-duty wall hooks, or build the arms from scrap 2x4 for near nothing. Frame this into the wall layout from the start so a stud lands where you need the lags.
Optional Phase 7: Electrical $300–$1,500

The welder needs power, and depending on the unit that may be a 240V circuit. Run conduit or direct-burial cable from the house or garage subpanel, set a small panel or circuit in the shed, add outlets and overhead lighting. This requires its own electrical permit. Doable as DIY at the low end of the range, but the connection at the source panel and the permit inspection are where a licensed electrician earns the higher end. This phase can be deferred, build and use the shed first, power it later.

Recommended Spec

For a shed that stores the mowers, tools, and kayaks: 12x12x12 stick-built, gravel pad foundation with pressure-treated 4x6 skids and a framed wood floor, gable roof with a partial rear loft, T1-11 siding painted to match the house, double barn doors with a pressure-treated ramp, and a stacked two-kayak wall rack on one side wall. No electrical needed, welding stays in the garage or on the patio. If a real workshop gets built back there later, that is its own proposal with its own slab.

If the slab pour is more than you want to take on for a first build, drop to a gravel pad and wood floor, and lay a steel plate or cement-board welding station inside as the fire-safe compromise. Everything else in the spec stays the same.
