What Is This?

The Dog Moms is a DogTV show where professional dog trainers Chrissy Joy and Amber Akar spin a "Pet Roulette" wheel, land on random dog-care topics, and have just four minutes to share their best advice, personal stories, and real-life experiences. Filmed at a cabin getaway with all seven of their dogs roaming around, it's part expert knowledge, part unfiltered dog-mom chaos.

This guide distills every actionable piece of advice from all 8 episodes of Season 1 into a searchable reference you can actually use.

Chrissy Joy

Dogs: Beasley, Darby, Fuji

Former equestrian turned dog trainer. Specializes in trick training, precision behaviors, and TV/film animal work. Has a six-year-old daughter and balances the dog-mom and human-mom life daily. Passionate about puzzle games and teaching dogs complex tricks like Jenga and ring stacking.

Amber Akar

Dogs: Oakley, Whiskey, Tucker (+ 2 cats)

Specializes in behavioral work, scent work, and reactive dogs. Oakley is her service dog. Has a dedicated dog van with custom crate setup (100K+ miles in under 2 years). Former exotic animal handler. Avid hiker and paddleboarder with her dogs.

Traveling with Your Dog

Ep 1

Both hosts travel constantly with their dogs, putting 100,000+ miles on their vehicles in under two years. Amber converted a van with a bed on top and crate slots underneath. The core principle: safety first, comfort second, but never skip either.

Crate your dogs in the car. Amber uses crash-tested Ruffland crates secured under a bed platform in her van. If you can't fit a crate, use a crash-tested harness that buckles into the seatbelt (Amber uses one for Oakley, who's too tall for his crate).
Build a travel kit. Always bring: first aid kit (ask your vet to help you build one), medicine bag with any meds they might need, collapsible water bowls, a big jug of water from home, extra leashes, slip leads, and their own dog beds.
Memory foam hack. Amber buys memory foam online and cuts it to size for the van. Long road trips are hard on joints, so give them something decent to lie on, not just a blanket.
Favorite dog-friendly destinations: Amber recommends the parks surrounding Mount Rainier. Chrissy loves Acadia National Park in Maine, calling it one of the most beautiful, dog-friendly places she's ever visited.

Food, Diet & Supplements

Ep 1

Both hosts emphasize that their dogs do heavy physical activity (sports, hiking, film work), so joint protection through supplementation is a priority. The food itself matters, but what you add to it can make a real difference for active dogs.

Joint supplements are non-negotiable for active dogs. Look for glucosamine, chondroitin, and MSM. These three ingredients together support cartilage health and reduce inflammation. Amber adds these to every dog's meal.
Fish oil / salmon oil for coat health, brain function, and anti-inflammatory benefits. Both hosts use it regularly. Chrissy also uses coconut oil topically and sometimes in food.
Feeding doesn't have to be boring. Scatter food in the grass for a simple sniff-and-forage session. Use puzzle toys when the dog didn't get enough physical exercise that day. Save bowl feeding for days they were physically active and just need calories.
Always separate dogs at mealtimes. With 3-4 dogs each, both hosts gate and separate every dog during meals and puzzle toy sessions to prevent resource guarding and food stealing.

Exercise & Physical Activity

Ep 6

Exercise isn't just about tiring your dog out. It's about conditioning their body for longevity, building different muscle groups, and giving them freedom of movement. The hosts are big proponents of hiking because it lets dogs move naturally at their own pace.

Hiking is king. Chrissy uses off-leash trails to give her dogs freedom of natural movement without being restricted by a harness. If off-leash isn't safe in your area, use a long line (15-30 ft) to give more freedom.
With multiple age dogs, adjust. Chrissy used to hike twice: once with older dogs at their pace, once with younger dogs. If you have senior and young dogs, don't force everyone to walk at the same speed.
Fetch is overrated for exercise. 30 minutes of fetch involves a lot of sudden stopping and direction changes that can blow ACLs and damage joints. It's fine in a pinch, but it's not great as a primary exercise routine, especially for dogs that are reckless about stopping.
Condition before competing. Don't let your dog sit around all week then enter a Frisbee competition. Build up with hill work, varied terrain, swimming (works different muscles), and progressive distance increases.
Swimming is excellent low-impact exercise that works different muscles than walking/running. Amber prioritizes it in warm months. Great for older dogs or dogs recovering from injury.

Mental Stimulation & Enrichment

Ep 1, 3

Both hosts agree: mental work can tire a dog out more than physical exercise. A dog that comes back from an hour of scent work sleeps harder than one that did an hour of fetch. And enrichment isn't just food puzzles.

Scent work is a game-changer. Amber's favorite activity. You don't need formal training. Just hide a favorite toy or treats around the house and let your dog hunt for them. It's low physical demand, high mental output, and dogs sleep like the dead afterward.
DIY enrichment ideas: Egg cartons with treats inside (poke holes, tape shut, let them destroy it). Milk jugs or Gatorade bottles with kibble inside for noise and rolling challenges. Kongs with peanut butter at bedtime. Scatter feeding in the grass.
Build an enrichment garden. One Instagram creator built a backyard with pet-safe plants, a sand digging pit, and a small pond where dogs could "fish" for things. Perfect for apartment dwellers or people without trail access.
Trick training is mental enrichment. Chrissy teaches precision tricks (Jenga, ring stacking on posts) that require the dog to think through multi-step problems. This exhausts them mentally and builds confidence in their own abilities.
Enrichment isn't only food. Bring novel smells home from places you visit (sticks, leaves, fabric) for a dog recovering from surgery or one that can't walk. Novel scents alone can be enriching.

Grooming & Hygiene

Ep 2

Short-haired dogs often stink more and need more frequent baths than long-haired dogs (counterintuitive, but both hosts confirmed it). Long-haired dogs may shed more but seem to trap dirt differently. Bathing frequency depends on coat type, not a universal schedule.

Bathing frequency: Once a month to once every two months for most dogs. Washing too often strips natural oils from the coat. Some people bathe weekly, and that's fine, but it's not necessary for most dogs.
Trim the "Grinch feet." Dogs with long hair between their toes (Border Collies, Goldens, Aussies) can slip on hard floors and lose traction during activity. Use small shears or a beard trimmer to keep paw hair trimmed. Chrissy admits she lets her dogs go full Grinch and only recently got better about this.
Nail care: Dremel > clippers for dogs with black nails where you can't see the quick. If you do nick the quick, have styptic powder on hand. It'll bleed dramatically but isn't dangerous.
Use an undercoat rake, not just a slicker brush. Slicker brushes only get the top coat. An undercoat rake pulls out all the dead hair from underneath. Use it during or after a bath with a force dryer (the high-powered ones that blow hair everywhere) for best results.

Skin & Coat Care

Ep 2

Amber shared her "industry secrets" for coat care. The main theme: what you put on the coat matters as much as diet and supplements.

Show Sheen (yes, the horse product). Amber's top secret. It makes coats incredibly shiny and slick. Do NOT use it indoors because it makes floors ice-rink slippery. Use it outside or in a tub, then let the dog dry before coming in.
Coconut oil applied topically to dry or flaky skin. Works on noses, paw pads, and coat. Some dogs may try to lick it off, which is fine in small amounts.
Fish oil and salmon oil in food for coat health from the inside out. Both hosts consider this a daily staple, not occasional.
Medicated shampoos for dogs with chronic skin issues. Both hosts recommend working with your vet to find the right formula rather than guessing with over-the-counter products.

Behavioral Issues & Reactivity

Ep 2, 3, 4
"If your dog is giving you a hard time, they're not trying to. Your dog is having a hard time." — Amber's guiding philosophy

Amber has extensive behavioral experience and emphasizes that even professional trainers have difficult dogs. Having a reactive or aggressive dog doesn't mean you failed. It means you have a different kind of journey with that dog.

Reactivity has multiple causes. A dog barking on leash could be excited, scared, frustrated, or overstimulated. The motivation matters because the approach is different for each. Don't assume your dog is "aggressive" when they might just be overwhelmed.
Walking isn't always the answer. If every walk is your dog pulling and exploding at triggers, the walk isn't enriching for either of you. Try burning energy with a ball in the backyard first, then attempt a calmer walk. Or skip the walk entirely and do mental enrichment instead.
Give space, don't flood. Don't throw a reactive dog into the situations that stress them out. Build distance, work under threshold, and gradually increase exposure. Removing them from overwhelming environments is not "giving up," it's management.
Muzzle training is a safety tool, not a punishment. Amber strongly advocates teaching your dog to wear a muzzle happily before they ever need one. It gives peace of mind to the handler, protects others, and vets often need to use them anyway. Teach them to enjoy it so it's not traumatic when needed.
Aggression requires professional help. If your dog is showing aggression, work with a qualified behaviorist. It's not just about the dog; the owner's stress and anxiety are layers that need addressing too. When it's serious, consult your vet about medical support options.

Socialization

Ep 5

Chrissy is in the thick of socializing her puppy Fuji and reports that her previous three dogs took to socialization easily, but Fuji is a different breed with different challenges. Some puppies are naturally confident; others need more patience.

Socialization isn't just meeting other dogs. It's about exposure to sounds, surfaces, environments, people of all types, objects, car rides, and novel experiences. The goal is creating a dog that can handle the unexpected without falling apart.
Fear periods are real. Puppies go through developmental stages where things they were fine with suddenly become scary. This is normal. Don't push through it; instead, create positive associations at the dog's comfort level.
Let the dog control the experience. Amber found that letting puppies create scary sounds themselves (popping balloons, banging things on the floor) builds confidence faster than having scary things happen to them. The dog having agency over the stimulus changes their emotional response.
It's not just for puppies. Even adult dogs need ongoing socialization maintenance. And older dogs can develop new sensitivities (Amber's Oakley developed thunder phobia at age 6-7 despite having none before).

Body Language

Ep 5

Both hosts consider body language literacy one of the most important skills a dog owner can develop. Amber specifically talked about learning to respect what her older dog was telling her through subtle signals she used to ignore.

Know your breed's baseline. Border Collies carry their tails low naturally, which can look "sad" to someone used to Labs. Whiskey's ears fold forward, so "ears back" looks different on her than on a German Shepherd. Learn your specific dog's neutral state before reading deviations.
Stress signals to watch for: Lip licking, yawning (not tired), whale eye (showing whites), tucked tail, turning away, refusing food they normally love, excessive panting when it's not hot. These are your dog communicating discomfort.
"Opting in" vs. being forced. Amber learned this with her older dog Tucker. She used to push him into activities and ignore his subtle resistance. Now she respects when he opts out, and he actually participates more willingly because it's on his terms. If your dog is telling you they don't want to participate, listen.
A wagging tail doesn't always mean happy. Speed, height, and stiffness of the wag all matter. A low, slow wag is different from a high, stiff wag. Context matters enormously.

Dogs & Kids

Ep 4

Chrissy has a six-year-old daughter and four dogs. Amber was pregnant during filming and was actively preparing her dogs. Their joint perspective: it's not just about teaching your dog to tolerate kids. You also have to teach kids to respect dogs.

Kids are weird to dogs. They're unpredictable, small (at eye level), make loud noises, move erratically, and sometimes pretend to be dogs. Amber's dog Whiskey got scared of kids after one child pretended to resource guard a water bowl from her, growling at the dog. That single experience made Whiskey nervous around children for a long time.
Teach kids the rules: Don't take things from a dog's mouth. Don't approach a sleeping dog. Don't jump into play-fighting between dogs. Don't put your face near a dog's face. These seem obvious, but kids at floor level with dogs don't intuitively know this.
Gates are essential. Both hosts use baby gates extensively to manage interactions between dogs and children. Separation during feeding, during unsupervised moments, and when the child has food (dogs see toddlers holding Cheerios as perfect-height Pez dispensers).
Show kids what they CAN do. Don't just make it all "don't do this, don't do that." Teach them to help with training, cheer the dog on during potty training, participate in feeding routines. Build the bond positively so the kid sees the dog as a teammate, not a restriction.

Weight Management

Ep 4

The majority of pet dogs are actually overweight, and many owners don't realize it. Fluffy dogs hide their body condition under all that coat, and different breeds carry weight completely differently.

Know your dog's body type. A Bulldog's build is nothing like a Border Collie's. Bathe your fluffy dog and actually look at their body underneath the hair. You might be surprised how different they look wet.
Your dog motivates YOU. Both hosts admit there are days they don't want to go outside. But the dogs need it, so they go. And they always feel better afterward. Having a dog is a built-in accountability partner for your own fitness.
Treats add up fast. If you're training with high-value treats all day, reduce meal portions accordingly. Chrissy saves a portion of her dogs' daily food for training so the total caloric intake stays the same.
Early neutering can affect weight. Chrissy notes that Beasley was neutered early and tends to carry weight more easily as a result. Something to discuss with your vet when making timing decisions.

Joint Health & Arthritis

Ep 6

Chrissy's Beasley has osteoarthritis, and both hosts have senior dogs requiring joint management. The message: don't wait for it to become a problem. Start conditioning and supplementing early.

Watch for subtle signs. Chrissy noticed Beasley was limping on and off for a while. She kept dismissing it ("he probably slept wrong"). When it became consistent, she got him checked. Early intervention makes a huge difference.
Adequan injections were a game-changer for Beasley. It's a series of injections over about a month that support joint health. Talk to your vet about whether this might help your dog.
Laser therapy and rehab. Beasley now has a prescribed rehabilitation schedule with specific walks and exercises to strengthen the areas around his arthritic joints. Professional rehab guidance matters.
Fitness equipment for dogs exists. Balance boards, wobble cushions, and specific conditioning exercises can be done at home to build strength around joints. This is especially useful for dogs transitioning from high-impact sports to lower-impact activities.
Supplement early. Glucosamine, chondroitin, and MSM in daily meals, plus fish oil for inflammation. Don't wait until your dog is already arthritic.

Firework & Thunder Phobia

Ep 3, 4

Amber's Oakley developed thunder phobia at age 6-7 despite having no history of noise sensitivity. He would literally lock himself in the bathroom and slam the door shut. This is a topic both hosts have personal, emotional experience with.

Comforting your scared dog will NOT make them more scared. This is a myth. Amber taught Oakley that he could come to them when scared, and it created a better association than him hiding in the bathroom. Let your dog seek comfort from you.
Desensitize proactively. For puppies: play 8-hour thunderstorm sounds on YouTube at moderate volume throughout the day as background noise. Make it boring. DogTV also has desensitization programs.
Bubble wrap to fireworks, gradually. Chrissy starts with popping bubble wrap + treats, then graduates to popping balloons + treats. The goal is to pair loud sudden sounds with good things in a controlled, incremental way. Let the dog be the one making the sound when possible.
Create a safe space. Could be a crate, a closet, under the bed, on the couch with you. Whatever your dog gravitates to when scared, make it accessible and comfortable. Play white noise or music loudly to drown out the external sounds.
Already-phobic dogs need different help. If your dog is already over-threshold and panicking, desensitization games won't cut it in the moment. Talk to your vet about medical support options. The #1 day dogs go missing is July 4th.

Sleep & Nighttime Routine

Ep 5

Neither host has major sleep issues with their dogs, and they attribute it to consistent routines. The dogs know when it's bedtime and go to their spots without drama.

Routine is everything. Amber literally says "okay, it's bedtime" and all her dogs go to their respective places. Chrissy uses Kongs with peanut butter at bedtime as a positive end-of-day ritual. The dogs associate crate time with something good.
For puppies: beat them to the punch. Chrissy sets her alarm to take the puppy out before it cries, so the puppy doesn't learn that crying = getting let out. She knows Fuji's bladder window and preempts the wake-up. As the puppy ages, she stretches the intervals.
Old dogs may need more bathroom breaks. If your senior dog suddenly has "sleep problems," it might just be a bladder issue. Make sure the last thing before bed is a bathroom trip.
Sudden sleep changes in older dogs can indicate canine cognitive dysfunction (similar to Alzheimer's). If your dog's sleep-wake cycle reverses or they seem confused at night, see your vet.

Water Safety & Emergency Prep

Ep 7, 8

Amber shared an extremely emotional story: her dog fell through ice on a frozen pond. He went under and didn't come back up. She smashed through the ice herself to pull him out. Her body was black and blue. This was the moment that drove her to learn canine CPR.

Never assume your dog can handle water. Even dogs who swim well can get into trouble with currents, cold water, ice, or exhaustion. Amber now keeps life jackets for her dogs when doing water activities.
Learn canine CPR. After her ice incident, Amber trained in it. Many vet clinics and pet organizations offer courses. You may never need it, but if you do, you'll be glad you know.
Car emergency kit: Extra leash/slip lead (strays turn up often), large jug of water + foldable bowl, first aid kit (have your vet help you build one and teach you basic wound wrapping), dog's medical info and emergency contacts.
Ask your vet for a first aid lesson. Amber scheduled an appointment specifically for this. Her vet showed her how to wrap injuries, what to keep on hand, and basic triage. This is invaluable for hikers and travelers.

Vet Care, Vaccines & Prevention

Ep 4, 7, 8

Amber worked in a veterinary hospital for years. Both hosts are passionate about preventative care and trusting your gut when something feels off with your dog.

Trust your gut. Amber felt a hard lump on Tucker's stomach. She thought it was another fatty lump, but something felt wrong. Ultrasound revealed a splenic tumor. Within a month of discovery, he had an emergency situation that would have killed him if she hadn't already been monitoring it.
Vaccines and titers. Core vaccines are essential. For senior dogs, Amber does titer testing (blood draw to check antibody levels) instead of automatically re-vaccinating. Titers show whether the dog's immune system still has adequate protection without adding unnecessary vaccines.
Know which vaccines you need. Core: rabies, distemper. Common adds: Bordetella, lepto (important if your dog drinks from natural water sources), Lyme (may not be needed if on proper tick preventative, discuss with vet), canine influenza.
Pet insurance. Multiple mentions across episodes. Both hosts support it, especially for dogs that do sports or heavy outdoor activity. Even if you don't have insurance, don't skip the vet visit.
Dental health matters. Stinky breath isn't just annoying; it's often a sign of dental disease. Get your dog's teeth cleaned. Chrissy notes the waitlist at her vet is months long, so schedule early.
Parasites. Flea, tick, and heartworm prevention year-round. Watch for scooting (worms), diarrhea, and poor coat quality. Get fecal tests at the vet regularly. Some parasites live in soil and can reinfect your dog from their own backyard.

Getting a New Dog

Ep 3

Both hosts are pro-rescue AND pro-responsible breeders. The key is doing your research and being honest about your lifestyle before bringing a dog home.

Research the breed. Don't get a Border Collie because they're cute and then be shocked when it needs 3 hours of stimulation daily. Understand energy levels, grooming needs, health predispositions, and whether the breed matches your actual lifestyle, not the lifestyle you wish you had.
If going through a breeder: Health test the parents. Ask about lineage. Visit in person. A reputable breeder will welcome questions and have documentation. Avoid puppy mills and backyard breeders.
If rescuing: Rescue dogs can be incredible, but ask the shelter about the dog's temperament, energy level, and history. Are they good with kids? Other animals? What's their activity level? Shelter staff spend time with these dogs and can give you real feedback.
Foster to adopt. Many rescues offer this option. You take the dog home on a trial basis before committing. Chrissy warns she's a "foster fail" (fostered a cat, kept it), but it's genuinely a great way to see if the dog fits your home before it's permanent.
Don't impulsively get a dog. Both hosts stress this repeatedly. A dog is a 10-15+ year commitment that will fundamentally change your daily routine, travel plans, finances, and relationships. Take your time.

Dog Mom Guilt & Being "Perfect"

Ep 4
"We're full of imperfections. Every day we're challenged with: are we doing enough? And the answer is that doing your best IS enough." — Chrissy

This was one of the most personal segments. Both hosts talk about dog mom guilt weekly. Social media amplifies it because you see everyone else's highlight reel and compare it to your behind-the-scenes reality.

Your dog doesn't need to hike every day. It's okay for your dog to have a lazy day. It's okay for YOU to have a lazy day. Dogs adapt. One slow Tuesday doesn't ruin their quality of life.
Stop comparing to social media. The person posting their dog's agility course completion also had three days that week where they just watched TV with their dog on the couch. Nobody posts the boring days.
Having a hard day doesn't make you a bad dog parent. Both hosts, who are literally professional dog trainers, have days where they feel like they're not doing enough. If the pros feel this way, it's okay for you to feel it too.
Having a supportive friend helps. Amber and Chrissy talk to each other about this regularly. Having someone who understands the dog-parent guilt and can reality-check you is genuinely valuable.

Toys & Play

Ep 2

Every dog has preferences. Oakley is obsessed with sloth toys (Amber has sewn his original one together 20+ times). Knowing what your dog loves and using it strategically makes training and bonding more effective.

Save high-value toys for training. Chrissy keeps Frisbees, squeaker toys, and balls strictly for training sessions. This keeps the toys valuable and exciting. The dog doesn't get bored of them because access is limited.
For durability: no stuffing. Kong makes rope-and-cloth combo toys that last much longer than stuffed toys. Less mess, longer play sessions.
Playology scented toys are rubber toys infused with scent (chicken, beef, etc.). Both hosts noted these keep dogs engaged longer than regular rubber toys because the smell keeps them interested even when the novelty of the shape wears off.
Puzzle toys for rehab. Amber used puzzle toys to help a dog rehab a shoulder injury. The dog had to use its body to manipulate the toy, getting light physical therapy while being mentally engaged. Two-for-one.

Dogs & Cats Together

Ep 8

Amber lives with three dogs and two cats (one was a foster fail). Her main insight: most conflicts happen because people don't give both species adequate space to escape each other.

Give cats vertical escape routes. Cat trees, shelves, high perches. Cats need to get up and away from dogs when they want space. This single change prevents most dog-cat conflicts.
Cat doors and peepholes. Amber has small openings in doors that cats can pass through but dogs can't. This gives cats access to dog-free zones in the house at all times.
Learn cat body language. A wagging tail on a cat means the opposite of what it means on a dog (annoyance/tension, not happiness). If you're only fluent in dog body language, you'll misread your cat constantly.
Do things together. Amber has all her animals sit together and receive treats by name. This builds positive associations between the species and creates shared calm experiences rather than only stressful interactions.

Info for Pet Sitters

Ep 2

A viewer asked what to leave with a pet sitter, and the hosts covered both the practical and the unexpectedly important.

Leave written emergency permission. Amber learned this from a vet tech: write out explicit permission for the sitter to spend up to X amount on emergency care, or to make life-and-death decisions if you're unreachable. Give this to the sitter in writing, and make sure the vet clinic has it on file too.
Standard info: Vet name and number, emergency vet location, feeding schedule with exact amounts, medication schedule and dosages, supplement routine, behavioral notes (e.g., "don't leave out shoes, he eats them").
Household-specific warnings. Amber always writes in big letters: "Be aware the cats will try to escape when you open the door." Every house has its quirks. Think about what someone who doesn't live there needs to know about your setup. Gates, doors that don't latch, dogs that can jump fences, etc.

🎤 Fan Q&A Highlights

Every episode ends with viewer questions. Here are the best answers from across the season.

I recently had a baby and can't exercise my dog like I used to. Help?
Chrissy lived this. Her advice: everything is temporary. Your dog's routine will change, but that doesn't mean you're doing less for them. Try enrichment toys, scatter feeding, pair walks with baby nap time (the walking rocks the baby to sleep), and find activities that include both baby and dog. Also give yourself grace. This phase passes faster than you think.
How do I calm my dog during thunderstorms?
Let them come to you for comfort (this does NOT reinforce fear). Play white noise or music loudly. Provide a safe enclosed space. For prevention, play thunderstorm sounds at low-moderate volume during puppyhood to normalize the sound. For severe cases, talk to your vet about medical options.
My dog is super reactive on walks. How do I keep her calm?
First, figure out WHY. Is she excited? Scared? Frustrated? The approach differs for each. Don't force walks if they're miserable for both of you. Try: exercise before the walk (fetch in the yard), increase distance from triggers, give the dog space to decompress, and consider whether the walk is even necessary if you can provide enrichment other ways.
How do I keep older dogs engaged?
Switch from physical to mental challenges. Scent work, trick training, puzzle toys, and novel smells to explore. Adjust hike distances (shorter, flatter terrain). Keep them using their brains; dogs that stop being mentally stimulated can become depressed, and some research suggests it may shorten their lives. Respect their pace, but don't stop giving them things to do.
How do I keep my house clean with dogs?
Vacuum daily (or close to it). Wash carpets weekly with a Bissell carpet cleaner. Check and replace your HVAC air filters frequently (they clog fast with pet dander). Use air purifiers. Washable rugs are a lifesaver. Keep towels near water bowls. Clean with white vinegar or pet-safe products like Nature's Miracle. Use pet-safe essential oils for smell.
How do I balance dog-mom life with my spouse and kids feeling jealous?
Time management. Handle dog training and exercise when kids are at school and spouse is at work. When the family is together, include them in dog activities rather than choosing one over the other. Have an honest conversation with your partner about it. And remember: your family is #1, even if the dogs are your passion. Show your kids and partner they matter by carving out dog-free time with them too.
Tips for taking great photos of your dog?
Focus on the eyes, not the nose. Phones auto-focus on whatever sticks out most (the nose), making the face blurry. Tap the eye on your phone screen. Teach your dog a "catch" cue and throw treats to get alert ears and engaged expressions. Amber uses her Apple Watch as a remote shutter with a tripod, which is perfect for getting in the photo yourself.
