Proposal #019

Two-Flag Display: Old Glory + USMC

Two wall-mounted spinning flagpoles flanking the front entry. The American flag on the honor side, the Marine Corps flag opposite. Done correctly by the U.S. Flag Code, sized right for a house, anchored properly into brick.

Total Cost$70–$140

Bottom Line Up Front

Do not stack both flags on one 6 ft house pole. A short wall pole physically fits one flag; the lower flag would drag the bracket. The right answer is two separate 6 ft tangle-free spinning poles, one on each side of the front entry. The US flag goes on the left as you face the house (the position of honor), the USMC flag on the right. Two poles also let you half-staff the US flag independently, which a stacked pole can't do. Both 3x5 embroidered, brackets set to fly the flags forward toward the street. About $70–$140 all-in, one afternoon, and the only real skill is drilling clean holes in brick.

Why Two Poles, Not One

The instinct is one pole, US flag up top, USMC flag clipped in the slot below. It does not work on a house wall pole. Those poles are 5 to 6 ft and are built to fly a single flag. A 3x5 flag is 5 ft along the pole; put a second one under it and the lower flag rides down into the mounting bracket and the siding. Flag retailers are blunt about this, most residential wall poles are rated for one flag, and two flags belong on a tall in-ground or commercial pole.

Where It Goes

Primary: Flank the Front Entry Recommended

One pole on the flat brick to the left of the entry, one on the brick pier to the right (between the entry and the big arched window). This is the position of honor, it is symmetric, it frames the door, and it reads cleanly from the street. The flags fly out over the front walk and shrub bed, nothing in the flight path.

  • US flag, left side of the entry as you face the house. Left is the honor position for the national flag on equal-height poles.
  • USMC flag, right side of the entry.
  • Set both brackets to the angle that flies the flags forward toward the street, not splayed outward, so the USMC flag never crosses the arched window glass.
  • Mount high, roughly 7.5 to 8 ft off the ground, so a 3x5 flag clears the shrubs and walkway at rest.
Alternate: Garage-Side Brick Panel Backup

If drilling either side of the entry feels too busy, both poles go on the flat brick panel left of the left garage door, 3 to 4 ft apart, flying forward as a tight pair. It is off-center and less of a front-door frame, but it is one clean uninterrupted brick wall with no windows in the way. Same flag order: US on the left, USMC on the right.

Flag Etiquette You're Now Bound By

Flying the Marine Corps flag next to Old Glory comes with rules. They are short and they are not optional.

Position. On two separate poles of equal height, the US flag goes to its own right, which is the observer's left when facing the display. So: US flag on the left pole, USMC on the right pole.

Size. The USMC flag must never be larger than the US flag. Both at 3x5 is correct. Do not run a 4x6 service flag next to a 3x5 national flag.

Height. No flag flies above the US flag. Equal-height poles keep this automatic; just don't mount the USMC bracket higher than the US one.

Material and topper. Embroidered nylon for both, outdoor-rated. Use a ball topper on each pole, not an eagle, an eagle topper is conventionally reserved for poles flying only the US flag, and matching ball toppers keep the pair looking deliberate.

HOA reality check: Federal law (the Freedom to Display the American Flag Act of 2005) stops an HOA from banning the US flag, though it can still set reasonable rules on pole size and placement. That protection does not cover the USMC flag. Stamey's Walk has covenants, so confirm the service flag and the mount locations are allowed before drilling. A two-minute covenant check now beats a take-it-down letter later.

Mounting Into Brick, The Right Way

The whole job is anchoring two brackets into a brick wall so they hold a flag through a thunderstorm gust. Done correctly it is genuinely a one-afternoon, difficulty-2 task.

Drill the brick, not the mortar

Set the anchors into the face of a brick, not the mortar joints. The solid brick gives far better pull-out resistance, and wind load on a flag is mostly a pulling force on the top anchors. Mortar is softer and crumbles. Brick face it is.

Use exterior-rated fasteners

Stainless or hot-dipped galvanized only. Plain steel anchors will rust-stain the brick and weaken within a couple of seasons. The standard pick is 1/4 in masonry anchors, either Tapcon-style concrete screws or sleeve anchors, roughly 1-3/4 in long, with at least 1 in of embedment into the brick.

Brick veneer note: a brick house wall is usually a veneer with an air gap behind it. You are anchoring into the brick itself, you do not need to reach the framing behind it. A 1/4 in anchor seated solidly in a brick face holds a 6 ft flagpole fine.

Mind what's behind and around the wall

Before picking the exact spot, make sure you are not drilling near the gas meter, an electrical run, a hose bib, or a dryer vent. Keep clear of window and door trim. The marked locations on the photo already account for this; just eyeball it once more in person.

Install Steps

  1. Confirm HOA covenants allow a service flag and wall-mount poles. Do this first.
  2. Pick the two spots from the photo. Hold a bracket up at each, around 7.5 to 8 ft, and check there is clear airspace for a 3x5 flag to fly forward without hitting trim, a window, or shrubs.
  3. Mark the anchor holes with a pencil, dead on a brick face, not a mortar joint. Use a level so the bracket sits plumb.
  4. Drill each hole with the hammer drill and 1/4 in masonry bit. Drill slightly deeper than the anchor length so debris has somewhere to go.
  5. Blow or brush the brick dust out of every hole. Dust left in the hole stops the anchor from seating fully.
  6. Mount both brackets with the stainless/galv anchors, snug and plumb. Set the bracket angle so each flag flies forward toward the street.
  7. Assemble both spinning poles, attach the ball toppers, clip the flags to the tangle-free rings. US flag on the left pole, USMC on the right.
  8. Slide each pole into its bracket and lock it. Step back to the street and confirm the flags hang even and fly clean.

Reality Checks

Take them down in storms. These poles are not wind-rated. NC summer thunderstorms and any hurricane remnants will snap a pole or shred a flag. Pull them in.

Flags wear out. A daily-flown outdoor flag lasts 6 to 12 months. The scarlet USMC flag fades faster than the US flag, red dye goes first. Budget for replacements, and a spare set means you are never flying a faded rag.

Light them or lower them. Flag code says the US flag flown at night should be lit. Either add a small solar spotlight or take both flags down at dusk and re-fly at dawn.

Half-staff. The two-pole setup pays off here. On the days that call for it you can lower just the US flag to half-staff, which a single stacked pole physically cannot do.

Recommendation

Run the primary plan: two 6 ft tangle-free spinning poles flanking the front entry, US flag on the left, USMC on the right, both 3x5 embroidered nylon, ball toppers, anchored into the brick face with stainless hardware. It is the position of honor, it frames the door, it satisfies the flag code without thinking about it, and it keeps half-staff on the table. Spend the upcharge on made-in-USA flags, buy a spare set, confirm the HOA covenants first, and keep them out of the weather. One afternoon of clean drilling and the front of the house flies right.
