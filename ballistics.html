Rimfire vs Centerfire

Rimfire

Primer compound is spun into the rim of the cartridge base. The firing pin strikes the rim edge, crushing it to ignite the primer.

  • .22 LR, .22 Short, .22 WMR, .17 HMR
  • Cases cannot be reloaded (rim is destroyed)
  • Cheaper to manufacture
  • Limited to lower pressures

Centerfire

Separate primer seated in the center of the case head. Firing pin strikes the center.

  • Everything else: 9mm, .223, .308, .45 ACP, etc.
  • Cases can be reloaded
  • Handles higher pressures
  • More consistent ignition

Cartridge Anatomy

Bullet (Projectile)

The part that leaves the barrel. This is what hits the target.

Case / Brass

The container holding everything together. Made of brass, steel, or aluminum.

Powder (Propellant)

Burns rapidly to create expanding gas that pushes the bullet out.

Primer

Impact-sensitive compound that ignites the powder when struck by the firing pin.

Case Mouth

Crimped to hold the bullet in place until firing.

Rim / Base

Where the extractor grips to remove the spent case after firing.

Nomenclature

Cartridge / Round: The complete unit (interchangeable terms)

Magazine: Container feeding rounds into action. NOT a "clip."

Chamber: Where the cartridge sits before firing

Caliber & Naming Conventions

The numbers in cartridge names do mean something, but inconsistently across naming systems.

American System

Often nominal bore diameter in decimal inches

  • .22 = 0.22" bore (actually ~.223")
  • .30-30 = .30 cal, originally 30gr powder
  • .30-06 = .30 cal, adopted 1906
  • .45-70 = .45 cal, 70gr powder

⚠ Often nominal, not exact measurements

Metric System

Bullet diameter × case length in mm

  • 9×19mm = 9mm bullet, 19mm case
  • 7.62×51mm = 7.62mm bullet, 51mm case
  • 5.56×45mm = 5.56mm bullet, 45mm case

✓ Most logical system - tells you exactly what you have

Marketing Names

Numbers are vibes, not specs

  • .357 Magnum = actually .38 caliber
  • .38 Special = fires .357" bullets
  • .380 ACP = fires .355" bullets
  • .44 Magnum = actually .429"

⚠ Always verify actual dimensions

Common Equivalents

≈ means dimensionally similar, but specs may differ (military vs civilian pressure)

.308 Win ≈ 7.62×51mm NATO .223 Rem ≈ 5.56×45mm NATO 9mm = .355" = 9×19mm = 9mm Luger = 9mm Parabellum

Grains: Bullet Weight

1 grain = 1/7000 lb ≈ 0.065 grams

Used for two measurements: bullet weight ("40gr .22 LR") and powder charge (reloading context)

36gr
Light
  • Faster velocity
  • Flatter trajectory initially
  • More affected by wind
  • Sheds velocity quicker
40gr
Standard
  • Balanced performance
  • Most common .22 LR weight
  • Good all-around choice
55gr
Heavy
  • More momentum
  • Slower initial velocity
  • Better wind resistance
  • Better energy retention at distance

Bullet Types & Terminal Ballistics

Full Metal Jacket (FMJ)

Copper jacket fully encases lead core. Penetrates with minimal expansion. May overpenetrate.

Use: Target shooting, plinking

Hollow Point (HP)

Cavity in nose fills with fluid on impact, causing hydraulic expansion to 1.5-2× diameter.

Use: Hunting, self-defense

Soft Point (SP)

Exposed lead tip mushrooms on impact. Controlled expansion, middle ground between FMJ and HP.

Use: Hunting

Lead Round Nose (LRN)

Pure lead, no jacket. Common in .22 LR. Some deformation on impact.

Use: Target, small game

Hollow Point Expansion

Before Impact

Original diameter (~.355" for 9mm)

Expanding

Cavity fills, petals forming

Mushroomed

Final diameter .60-.70"

External ballistics (flight): Hollow points fly essentially the same as comparable solids. Terminal ballistics (impact): Completely different behavior.

Trajectory & Drop Calculation

What Affects Trajectory?

Muzzle Velocity

Faster = less time in flight = less drop. Your 700 FPS shorts spend much longer getting to target than 1070 FPS standard velocity.

Ballistic Coefficient (BC)

Measures air drag resistance. Higher BC = bullet slows down less. Heavier, more aerodynamic bullets have better BC.

Gravity

Constant 32.2 ft/s². The longer the bullet is in the air, the more it drops. This is why velocity matters so much.

Approximate Drop from Line of Bore

Round Velocity BC 50yd 75yd 100yd
.22 LR High Velocity 1280 FPS ~0.14 2.5" 6" 12"
.22 LR Standard Velocity 1070 FPS ~0.13 3" 8" 14"
.22 Short Subsonic 700 FPS ~0.09 7" 18" 35"+

The 700 FPS shorts drop almost 2.5× as much at the same distance.

Ballistic Calculators

Use these to calculate exact trajectories for your specific loads:

  • Strelok - Phone app, excellent and free
  • JBM Ballistics - Web-based, free
  • Applied Ballistics - Premium, very accurate

Input: velocity, bullet weight, BC, sight height. Output: drop tables for any distance.

Subsonic vs Supersonic

Sound barrier: ~1125 FPS at sea level. Bullets transitioning through it can destabilize briefly.

  • Your 700 FPS shorts: Stay subsonic entire flight (quieter, stable, but slow)
  • Standard .22 LR: Starts supersonic, goes subsonic around 75-100 yards (accuracy can suffer at transition)

Pro Tips

  • Point of impact shifts with ammo: Different ammo = different POI, even same caliber. Always verify zero with the ammo you'll actually use.
  • For squirrel hunting: Hollow points like CCI Mini-Mag HP expand well on small game. Solids may pass through without much damage.
  • Twist rate: Your .22 likely has 1:16 twist, optimized for 40gr bullets.
