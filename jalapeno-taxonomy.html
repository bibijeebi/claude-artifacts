A Definitive Guide

The Complete Taxonomy of Stuffed Jalapeño Peppers

From deep-fried poppers to competition ABTs, armadillo eggs, and Texas Twinkies — every named preparation, where it came from, and how to tell them apart.

Every bacon-wrapped, cheese-stuffed jalapeño you've ever eaten has a proper name — and you've probably been calling it the wrong one. The stuffed jalapeño family tree spans at least five distinct preparations, each defined by a specific structural difference: where the sausage goes (inside vs. outside), whether it's breaded or wrapped, and how it's cooked.

The tradition stretches back over a century to Mexican chile rellenos, passed through Texas Tex-Mex culture in the mid-20th century, got trademarked by a Wisconsin frozen food company in 1992, and then exploded across BBQ competition forums in the 2000s into the sprawling taxonomy below.

01 — The OriginalThe Jalapeño Popper Started as a Frozen Food Trademark

The classic: breaded, fried, no bacon in sight

The jalapeño popper — the original, the one most people know — is specifically a breaded and deep-fried stuffed jalapeño. Halved, seeded, filled with cream cheese (sometimes mixed with cheddar), coated in breadcrumbs or batter, and dropped in a fryer until golden. No bacon. No smoker. No sausage.

Its ancestry runs directly to the Mexican chile relleno: roasted pepper stuffed with cheese or meat, battered, and fried. Stuffed jalapeños arrived in the United States through canned Mexican imports as early as 1926, when Don Clement Jacques, a French entrepreneur based in Mexico City, shipped cans of jalapeños stuffed with tuna, cheese, and shrimp into Texas. By the 1960s, Texas newspapers were running homemade stuffed jalapeño recipes. Food historian Lisa Fain found evidence of stuffed jalapeños at a 1972 armadillo festival in Victoria, Texas.

The name "Jalapeño Poppers" became official on August 17, 1993, when Anchor Food Products of Appleton, Wisconsin registered U.S. Trademark No. 1,788,303. The trademark was later cancelled on May 22, 2004 when Anchor failed to file renewal. By then, H.J. Heinz had acquired the retail brand and TGI Friday's licensing rights, while McCain Foods took Anchor's food-service operations — a business doing $500 million in annual sales.

Before the trademark, Leon's Texas Cuisine of Dallas launched "Jalitos" in 1985 — cheese-stuffed, breaded, fried jalapeños they claim were the first nationally distributed commercial version. By 1995, both Applebee's and TGI Friday's had poppers on their menus, and the frozen grocery store version followed shortly after. Today, "jalapeño popper" has become a genericized catch-all that people apply to virtually any stuffed jalapeño — which drives BBQ purists to distraction.

02 — The BBQ LegendThe Atomic Buffalo Turd Puts a Smokie Inside

The defining feature: a cocktail sausage nestled inside the cream cheese

The ABT — Atomic Buffalo Turd — is the BBQ competition world's contribution to the stuffed jalapeño canon, and its defining characteristic is widely misunderstood. A traditional ABT is a halved jalapeño filled with cream cheese, topped with a cocktail sausage (Lit'l Smokie), wrapped in bacon, and smoked at 225–250°F for about two hours. The cocktail sausage is what makes it an ABT. Without it, you just have a bacon-wrapped jalapeño.

The evidence for the smokie as the defining ingredient is strong. Scott Thomas of GrillinFools lists peppers, little smokies, cream cheese, and bacon as the four essential ingredients. The Black Peppercorn states plainly that the smokie is what sets ABTs apart from standard poppers. The influential early recipe from a user called "AlienBBQ" on The Smoke Ring specifically calls for cheddar Lit'l Smokies and describes the layered flavor profile from outside in: smoked bacon, then jalapeño, then cream cheese and onion, finishing with the cheddar-flavored smoked sausage. The name itself is a clue — the cocktail sausage poking out of cream cheese in a bacon wrapper looks the part.

Nobody knows who coined the name. The earliest documented use appears on the Big Green Egg forum in March 2005. By October 2006, new forum members were already asking "What is an ABT?" Etymologist Barry Popik researched the term in 2007 and found roughly 2,360 Google hits. "Atomic" references the heat. "Turds" references the shape. It spread through BBQ Brethren, SmokingMeatForums, and eventually mainstream BBQ sites.

Over time, usage has drifted. Many people now call any smoked, bacon-wrapped stuffed jalapeño an ABT, even without the smokie. Both Jeff Phillips and AmazingRibs.com present ABT recipes that omit the sausage entirely. But among early-2000s forum veterans, the smokie is the turd — it's right there in the name.

03 — The Scotch Egg CousinArmadillo Eggs Wrap Sausage Around the Outside

The inverse of an ABT: sausage goes outside, not inside

The armadillo egg is structurally the inverse of the ABT, and this is the single clearest distinction in the entire taxonomy. In an ABT, the sausage goes inside the pepper. In an armadillo egg, sausage meat goes outside, completely encasing the stuffed jalapeño like a Scotch egg wraps a hard-boiled egg. The result is a large, oval, egg-shaped object — hence the name.

The construction works from inside out: a whole jalapeño (seeded and cored), filled with cream cheese and cheddar, then wrapped entirely in raw ground sausage (typically breakfast sausage like Jimmy Dean), molded into an egg shape, and often then wrapped in bacon over the sausage layer. They're cooked by smoking at 250°F for about two hours, grilling over indirect heat, or baking at 375–400°F.

The armadillo egg is older than both the ABT and the trademarked popper. Lisa Fain of Homesick Texan, a James Beard Award-winning writer and seventh-generation Texan, traced the first mention to 1972 in the Victoria, Texas Advocate, in an article about an armadillo festival. She found evidence suggesting the variant was invented in Lubbock at Texas Tech. The BBQ Brethren forum draws the line sharply: armadillo eggs are pepper wrapped in sausage; ABTs are pepper stuffed with cheese and wrapped in bacon. Two different things.

04 — The Identity CrisisThe Bacon-Wrapped Jalapeño Lives in a Naming Gap

The most common stuffed jalapeño people actually make at home — halved jalapeño, cream cheese, wrapped in bacon, baked or grilled, no sausage anywhere — does not have its own universally recognized name. This is the preparation that creates the most confusion.

Most recipe websites call it a "bacon-wrapped jalapeño popper." Many BBQ enthusiasts call it an ABT, even though it lacks the cocktail sausage that traditionalists consider essential. Others call it an armadillo egg, despite having no sausage encasement. The SmokingMeatForums captured the casual user's perspective bluntly: "ABTs, Armadillo Eggs & Jalapeno Poppers are just different names for the same thing." BBQ purists would disagree vehemently, but the statement reflects how most home cooks actually use these terms.

05 — The Extended FamilyTexas Twinkies, Rattlesnake Eggs, and the Wider Universe

The Texas Twinkie: brisket-stuffed and bacon-wrapped

Texas Twinkies

Invented in 2014 by Hutchins BBQ in McKinney, Texas, these stuff a large whole jalapeño with cream cheese and chopped smoked brisket, wrap it in thick-cut bacon, and smoke it. The dish was created to use leftover brisket and became their best-selling item — they sell 8,000 to 10,000 per week and ship nationwide at $95 per dozen. October 16 is National Texas Twinkie Day.

Rattlesnake Eggs

A whole jalapeño stuffed with cream cheese and a piece of cheese stick, encased in a Bisquick-and-ground-sausage dough, dipped in egg, rolled in seasoned coating, and baked at 350°F. The breaded dough coating makes them more of a baked snack than a smoked BBQ item.

The Orbiting Appetizers

Several related BBQ appetizers orbit the stuffed jalapeño universe without actually being stuffed jalapeños. Pig shots use a slice of smoked sausage as the base, with bacon wrapped around it to form a cup, filled with cream cheese and diced jalapeños — the jalapeño is an ingredient, not the vessel. Shotgun shells substitute manicotti pasta for the jalapeño entirely. Rattlesnake bites (the Texas Roadhouse kind) are deep-fried balls of pepper jack and diced jalapeño — no whole pepper involved.

07 — The Quick TestIdentify What You're Eating

Ask yourself:
Is it breaded and fried?

That's a classic popper.

Ask yourself:
Is there a little sausage inside?

That's a traditional ABT.

Ask yourself:
Is the whole pepper wrapped in sausage meat?

That's an armadillo egg.

Ask yourself:
Is it stuffed with brisket?

That's a Texas Twinkie.

No BBQ sanctioning body formally defines any of these categories. The taxonomy exists entirely as folk knowledge, enforced by forum arguments and regional tradition rather than any official authority. The terms emerged organically from Texas BBQ culture (armadillo eggs, 1970s), frozen food marketing (poppers, 1992), and internet BBQ forums (ABTs, mid-2000s), and they continue to shift as each new generation of backyard pitmasters learns one name and applies it to whatever they happen to cook.
