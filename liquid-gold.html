Liquid Gold

Stop throwing away your cooking fats.

That jar of bacon drippings isn't waste, it's free cooking fuel that your grandparents knew to save. Here's everything you need to know about collecting, storing, and cooking with rendered animal fats.

↓

🥓 The Bottom Line

Yes, save that bacon grease. It's not technically lard (that's rendered pork leaf fat), but it's flavorful cooking fat that works beautifully for savory dishes. Strain it, jar it, refrigerate it. You'll significantly reduce your cooking oil purchases and your food will taste better. That's not nostalgia talking, that's thermodynamics and flavor chemistry.

Know Your Fats

Bacon Drippings

What You Have

Rendered fat from cooked bacon with smoky, salty, savory flavor profile. Contains fond (those tasty brown bits). Best for savory cooking where you want that bacon essence. Not neutral enough for baking.

Leaf Lard

Premium Pork Fat

Rendered from the fat around pig kidneys. Nearly neutral flavor, high smoke point. The gold standard for pie crusts and pastries. This is what serious bakers seek out, often hard to find retail.

Beef Tallow

Rendered Beef Fat

Rendered beef fat with beefy flavor and very high smoke point. Historically used for frying (McDonald's original fries). Extremely shelf-stable. Great for high-heat cooking and cast iron seasoning.

What To Cook With It

Fried Eggs

The classic. Crack eggs into a spoonful of warm bacon fat for crispy edges and rich flavor. The eggs essentially baste in rendered pork essence. Once you try this, plain butter feels like a downgrade.

Pro Tip: Medium heat, let the fat shimmer before adding eggs. Tilt pan to baste the tops.

Fried Potatoes

Hash browns, home fries, roasted potatoes. Animal fat + starch + heat = crispy golden perfection. The Maillard reaction works overtime when you're frying in bacon drippings. Restaurant-quality results at home.

Pro Tip: Par-boil cubed potatoes first, then fry in hot fat for maximum crisp.

Cornbread

This is the secret weapon. Grease your cast iron with bacon fat, get it smoking hot, pour in the batter. You get a crispy, savory crust that butter simply cannot achieve. Southern grandmothers have known this forever.

Pro Tip: Preheat your greased skillet in a 425°F oven before adding batter.

Sautéed Greens

Collards, kale, chard, cabbage. Start with bacon fat, add garlic, wilt your greens. The fat carries flavor and helps absorb fat-soluble vitamins. This is how greens were meant to be eaten.

Pro Tip: Add a splash of vinegar at the end to brighten the richness.

Refried Beans

Traditional refried beans are made with lard, and bacon fat works beautifully. Mash cooked pintos into warm fat, season heavily. Creamy, rich, and deeply savory. Vegetable oil versions don't compare.

Pro Tip: Save bean cooking liquid to adjust consistency while mashing.

Popcorn

Pop kernels in bacon fat instead of oil. The result is savory, slightly smoky, and doesn't need butter. Movie night elevated. Fair warning: regular popcorn will taste boring afterward.

Pro Tip: Use the 3-kernel test, when they pop, add the rest and shake constantly.

Cast Iron Seasoning

Animal fats polymerize beautifully when heated, creating that legendary non-stick surface. Thin coat, high heat, repeat. Your great-grandmother's skillet was seasoned this way and it still works.

Pro Tip: Wipe until you think there's no fat left, then wipe again. Too much = sticky.

Storage Guide

1-2 weeks

Counter Storage

Keep a jar by the stove for daily use. Strain through fine mesh to remove solids (they accelerate rancidity). Works if you're using it regularly. The fat should stay solid at room temp; if your kitchen runs hot, move to fridge.

3-6 months

Refrigerator

Sealed jar in the fridge. This is your main storage method. Fat solidifies completely, scoops out easily. Label with date if you're organized. Smell it before using after long storage, rancid fat has an obvious off odor.

∞ frozen

Freezer

Pour into ice cube trays, freeze, transfer to bags. Each cube is roughly a tablespoon. Basically indefinite storage. Perfect for bulk saving when you cook a lot of bacon at once. Thaws quickly at room temp or goes straight into a hot pan.

Clarifying Your Fat

Want cleaner fat with longer shelf life? Remove the salt, smoke particulates, and fond with this water-rendering method.

1

Combine

Melt your collected drippings with equal parts water in a pot

2

Simmer

Low heat for 10-15 minutes. Impurities migrate to the water layer

3

Chill

Refrigerate until fat solidifies into a disk on top of the water

4

Separate

Pop off the fat disk, scrape any gunk off the bottom. Repeat if needed

The Reality Check

🔥 What You Can Replace

  • Vegetable oil for most savory frying
  • Shortening in savory applications
  • Butter for cooking (not finishing)
  • Purchased lard for non-baking uses
  • Commercial cooking sprays

🛒 What You'll Still Buy

  • Butter (different flavor, dairy fat, finishing)
  • Olive oil (cold applications, salads)
  • Neutral oil (high-heat, neutral flavor needs)
  • Leaf lard (serious pie crusts, pastry)
  • Coconut oil (if that's your thing)

Your Practical Setup

The System That Actually Works

Keep a "working jar" by the stove. Every time you cook bacon, sausage, or fatty pork, strain the fat through a fine mesh strainer into this jar. Use from it daily.

Maintain a "storage jar" in the fridge. When your working jar gets full or you haven't used it in a week, transfer to the fridge jar. This is your backup supply.

Freeze the overflow. If you're cooking a lot of bacon (meal prep, big breakfast, whatever), freeze the excess in ice cube trays. Long-term storage, no waste.

Separate your fats if you're fancy. Keep beef tallow and pork fat in different containers. They have different smoke points and flavor profiles. Or don't. Mixed fat is fine for general cooking.
