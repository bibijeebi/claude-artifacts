Protein Efficiency

Not all protein sources are equal when you're trying to hit a target. The key metric is what percentage of calories come from protein. Pepperoni gives you 8g protein for 177 calories (18% protein calories). Chicken breast gives you 31g for 165 calories (75% protein calories). That's a 4x difference in efficiency.

When you're trying to hit 150-180g protein without blowing past your calorie target, efficiency matters enormously.

Practical Move

Keep a bag of frozen shrimp and a carton of egg whites. When you're behind on protein but near your calorie limit, these are your emergency levers.

02

Protein Quality

Is whey better than chicken? For practical purposes, they're roughly equivalent, with whey having a slight edge. Here's what actually matters:

Amino Acid Completeness

All animal proteins (whey, chicken, beef, eggs, fish) contain all 9 essential amino acids. Plant proteins often lack one or more. If you're eating animal protein, this is a non-issue.

Leucine Content

Leucine is the amino acid that triggers muscle protein synthesis (mTOR pathway). Whey is king at ~11% leucine. Chicken is ~7.5%. The threshold to max out MPS is ~2.5-3g leucine per meal.[1]

Absorption Speed

Whey peaks in 1-2 hours, whole foods take 3-4+. Fast isn't necessarily better. Post-workout, whey's speed is slightly advantageous. Otherwise, doesn't matter much.[2]

Bioavailability

Whey isolate is ~100% digestible. Chicken is ~95%. Beans are 70-80%. Animal sources are all in the same ballpark.[3]

Bottom Line

If you're eating 150-180g protein/day from mixed animal sources, you're fine. The "eat complete proteins and hit your number" advice is 95% of the battle.

03

Satiety Mechanisms

Protein is genuinely the most satiating macro, and insulin is way overhyped in the satiety conversation. Here are the actual mechanisms:

Stomach Stretch

Mechanoreceptors in your stomach wall signal fullness based on volume. 400 cal of spinach fills you up; 400 cal of olive oil doesn't. Volume matters independent of calories.

Satiety Hormones

Protein stimulates PYY and GLP-1 (the hormone Ozempic mimics) more than carbs or fat. It also suppresses ghrelin (hunger hormone) longer.[4]

CCK Release

Fat and protein both trigger cholecystokinin, which slows gastric emptying and signals fullness. Pure carbs don't do this as much.

Fiber

Slows digestion, adds bulk, feeds gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids (which also signal satiety). Highly satiating per calorie.

The Insulin Myth

Insulin doesn't make you hungry. The blood sugar crash after a spike can trigger hunger, but protein spikes insulin too and nobody's getting rebound hunger from chicken breast. The "insulin bad" crowd oversimplifies this.[5]

Satiety Hierarchy: Protein + Fiber + Volume > Protein + Fat > Fat > Refined Carbs
04

Muscle Protein Synthesis

Your body is constantly breaking down and rebuilding muscle. Gains happen when synthesis exceeds breakdown. Protein and resistance training both spike MPS, and they stack. Training a muscle elevates MPS sensitivity for 24-48 hours, which is why protein timing around workouts has some merit—but total daily intake matters way more.[6]

The "30g Per Meal" Myth

Old bro science said "30g max per meal, the rest is wasted." Mostly wrong. Your body will use it, just over a longer window. That said, spreading protein across 3-5 meals (30-50g each) may optimize MPS vs. one giant 150g meal. Practical difference? Maybe 5-10%. Don't stress it.[7]

05

Calorie Surplus

The conventional wisdom is 250-500 cal surplus for lean gains. Bigger surplus = faster gains but more fat. Smaller surplus = slower but cleaner.

Your body can only synthesize ~0.5 lb muscle/week max under ideal conditions (newer lifters, good genetics, perfect training). Excess calories beyond that just become fat.[8]

Max Muscle Gain ≈ 0.5 lb/week = 2 lb/month = 24 lb/year (ideal conditions)

For most experienced lifters, realistic expectations are closer to 0.25-0.5 lb muscle per month. The massive gains you see in transformation photos often include glycogen, water, and yes, some fat.

06

Sleep

Growth hormone peaks during deep sleep. Sleep deprivation tanks testosterone and spikes cortisol (catabolic). 7-9 hours isn't optional if you're serious about gaining.[9]

One bad night won't kill you. Chronic 5-6 hour sleep will cap your progress hard. Studies show sleep-deprived individuals lose significantly more muscle mass during a cut compared to well-rested subjects eating identical calories.[10]

07

Progressive Overload

Muscle grows in response to increasing demands. If you lift the same weight forever, you plateau. Add weight, reps, sets, or slow down tempo over time. Track your lifts.

The Only Question: "Did I do more than last time?"
Underrated Factors

Creatine: 5g/day, every day. Cheap, safe, well-studied, works.[11]
Walking: Low-intensity cardio improves nutrient partitioning without spiking cortisol.
Stress management: Chronic stress = chronic cortisol = muscle breakdown + fat storage.

08

Cardio While Bulking

The "interference effect" is real but overstated. Research shows concurrent endurance + strength training can blunt muscle gains compared to strength-only—but the magnitude matters. It's like a 10-20% reduction in hypertrophy in studies using high volume cardio (40+ miles/week).[12]

A few 30-minute runs per week? Not enough to meaningfully interfere.

The Real Issue: Calories

Running burns 80-120 cal/mile. If you run 3 miles and don't eat those 300 cal back, you erased part of your surplus. Simple fix: eat more.

Recovery Competition

Hard running (sprints, tempo) taxes your CNS and legs. Heavy squats the next day will suffer. Easy/moderate running has minimal recovery impact.

Glycogen Competition

Both lifting and running use muscle glycogen. If doing both in a day, carb intake matters more. Insufficient carbs = flat workouts.

Practical Approach

Keep running if you enjoy it—cardiovascular health is valuable. Eat back the calories. Separate hard runs from leg days by 24-48 hours. If gains stall, reduce running volume before cutting it entirely.
