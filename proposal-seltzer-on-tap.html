Seltzer on Tap

Skip the SodaStream entirely. Bulk CO2 tank + carbonation cap + any 2L soda bottle = unlimited sparkling anything for pennies per liter. Carbonate water, juice, cocktails, whatever you want.

The Problem

We go through roughly a 12-pack of store-brand seltzer water per week from Food Lion at about $5–6 per pack. That's $260–$312 per year on cans of water with bubbles in it. The cans pile up in recycling, take up fridge space, and we run out mid-week when we're mixing drinks at the bar. It's a consumable that never stops costing money.

Seltzer water is literally just water + CO2. There's no reason to keep buying it pre-packaged in aluminum cans shipped from a bottling plant when CO2 is one of the cheapest industrial gases on earth.

The Solution

A carbonation cap is a stainless steel fitting that screws onto any standard 2L PET soda bottle and has a ball-lock post on top, the same quick-connect fitting used in homebrew kegging. A gas line runs from the cap to a CO2 regulator mounted on a 5lb bulk tank. Set the regulator to 30–40 PSI, connect the cap, shake for 30 seconds, done. Carbonated whatever-you-want.

No SodaStream machine. No proprietary bottles or cartridges. No adapter hose hacks. No drilling holes in anything. Just a tank, a regulator, a hose, a cap, and any PET bottle you already have from buying soda.

How It Works

  1. Fill a 2L PET bottle with cold liquid (water, juice, tea, whatever), leaving 2–3 inches of headspace
  2. Screw on the carbonation cap
  3. Squeeze out excess air from the bottle, then snap on the ball-lock disconnect
  4. Open CO2 tank valve. Regulator feeds gas at 30–40 PSI into the bottle.
  5. Shake the bottle vigorously for 30–60 seconds
  6. Disconnect, unscrew cap, pour. Sparkling.

Why Not a SodaStream?

The SodaStream is just a spring-loaded valve in a plastic housing. It pushes CO2 into a proprietary bottle when you press a button. That's it. The carbonation cap does the exact same thing, but better in several ways.

SodaStream

The only advantage of a SodaStream is push-button convenience, you don't have to shake a bottle. But you're paying for that convenience with proprietary lock-in, limited functionality, and counter space. The carbonation cap is functionally superior.

Setup Instructions

  • Get a filled 5lb CO2 tank. Buy new (VEVOR on Amazon, ~$60) or find used on Marketplace. Get it filled at Machine & Welding Supply in Garner if empty.
  • Mount the dual-gauge CO2 regulator onto the tank's CGA320 valve. Wrap threads with plumber's tape first. Hand-tighten, then snug with a wrench. The regulator has two gauges: one shows tank pressure (~800 PSI when full), the other shows your output pressure.
  • Connect the gas line to the regulator's barbed output fitting. Secure with a hose clamp. Attach the ball-lock disconnect to the other end of the gas line. Secure with a hose clamp.
  • Set the regulator output to 30–40 PSI. Higher = more carbonation. Start at 35 PSI and adjust to taste.
  • Fill a 2L PET bottle with very cold liquid (colder = better carbonation). Leave 2–3 inches of headspace at the top.
  • Screw the carbonation cap onto the bottle. Squeeze the bottle slightly to push out excess air, then snap the ball-lock disconnect onto the cap's post.
  • Open the CO2 tank valve. You'll hear gas flow into the bottle. Shake vigorously for 30–60 seconds. More shaking = more fizz.
  • Close CO2 tank valve. Disconnect the ball-lock. Slowly unscrew the cap (it will hiss). Pour and enjoy.
Safety notes: Never use a PET bottle that's damaged, cracked, or has been through the dishwasher (heat weakens PET). Don't exceed 40 PSI on PET bottles. Always close the tank valve when not in use to prevent slow leaks through the regulator. Store the tank upright.
Pro tip: Keep 2–3 bottles of water in the fridge at all times. When you want seltzer, grab a cold bottle, cap it, carbonate, done in under a minute. Colder water absorbs CO2 much better than room temperature.

Why This Matters for the Bar

Half the cocktails on the drinks bar use seltzer, tonic, or sparkling water as a component. Running out of cans mid-session means a trip to Food Lion or a flat drink. With the carbonation cap setup, there's always carbonated water on demand. Even better, you can carbonate things directly: sparkling lemonade, carbonated sweet tea, fizzy fruit juice for mocktails, or even carbonated cocktail batches. Try that with a SodaStream.

Making homemade tonic water is also on the table now. Cinchona bark, citrus peel, lemongrass, sweetener, water, carbonate. Proper G&Ts with house tonic for about $0.50 a bottle.
