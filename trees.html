Tree Profiles

Every species that could be on or near your property. Sorted by likelihood — confirmed first, then common Piedmont species you'll encounter around Willow Spring.

Oak (Multiple Species)

Quercus spp.
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Highly variable by species. White oak: light gray, shallow furrows, flaky plates. Water oak: dark gray-black, smooth when young, rough ridges when mature. Willow oak: gray-brown, relatively smooth with narrow ridges. Southern red oak: dark, deeply furrowed with flat ridges.

Leaves (Spring)
White: rounded lobes. Water: spatula-shaped. Willow: narrow like willow. Red: pointed lobes with bristle tips.
Acorn Size
Water/Willow: small, <1/2". White: 3/4-1", warty cap. Red: 1/2-1", flat scaly cap.

60-100 ft mature. Massive canopy spread.
Acorn Pain Index
Moderate to high. White oak acorns are the worst barefoot.
To narrow down which oak: check acorn shape right now. Small and round = water or willow oak (most common in Wake County yards). Larger with a deep bumpy cap = white oak. Look at the branching pattern too: willow oaks have a clean, upright crown; water oaks spread wide and messy.

Pecan

Carya illinoinensis
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Gray to gray-brown with shallow irregular furrows and flattened ridges. On mature trees, bark develops interlocking ridges that can look like a tight diamond pattern. Looks similar to ash and hickory. Less "shaggy" than shagbark hickory, but chunkier than ash.
Pecan Bark
Pecan Leaves & Nuts
Pecan Husks Opening
Shelled Pecans
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 12-20" long with 9-17 lance-shaped leaflets. Leaflets are slightly curved. Come in late (May).
Nuts
Oblong, 1-2" long, thin shell. Enclosed in a husk that splits into 4 sections when ripe (Oct-Nov).
Size
70-100 ft tall, can reach 150 ft. Massive trunk up to 6 ft diameter. Largest hickory species.
Crown Shape
Broadly oval, symmetrical. Lower branches sweep wide on open-grown trees.
Pecans need cross-pollination. If you have one pecan producing well, there's likely another pecan (or hickory) nearby acting as a pollinator. Your two mystery trees might include a second pecan. Check the ground under them for old husks.
Free pecans every fall. Beat the squirrels by harvesting as soon as husks start splitting (usually October in Wake County). Shake branches or wait for windy days. Store in-shell pecans in the freezer, they'll keep for 2+ years.

Catalpa ("Tobacco Tree")

Catalpa bignonioides / C. speciosa
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Gray-brown, breaking into irregular scaly plates. Relatively thin bark compared to oaks. Twigs are stout with large circular leaf scars. The dead-giveaway is the long cigar-like seed pods hanging from branches all winter.
Catalpa Bark
Catalpa Leaf
Catalpa Flowers
Catalpa Seed Pods
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
HUGE heart-shaped leaves, 6-12" long. Smell slightly unpleasant when crushed. This is why people call it "tobacco" — big leaves.
Seed Pods
10-20" long, thin, cylindrical. Look like green beans or cigars. Turn brown and persist through winter, splitting to release winged seeds.
Flowers (May-June)
Showy white clusters with purple/yellow spots. Actually really pretty. Orchid-like.
Size
30-60 ft. Irregular, spreading crown. Often looks messy/wild.
Catalpa is NOT tobacco. Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) is an annual herb that grows 3-6 ft tall, not a tree. People confuse them because of the large leaves. Also known as "Indian bean tree," "cigar tree," and "catawba" (corruption of the same name). Fishermen love catalpas because catalpa worms (sphinx moth caterpillars) are premium fishing bait.

Loblolly Pine

Pinus taeda
Confirmed
Bark ID (Year-Round)
Young trees: dark, furrowed bark. Mature trees: thick, reddish-brown to gray plates separated by deep furrows. Plates are roughly rectangular. Very distinctive once you know what to look for. Smells like pine resin if you scratch it.
Loblolly Pine Bark
Loblolly Pine Cones
Loblolly Pines
Old Growth Loblolly
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Needles
6-9" long, in bundles of 3 (occasionally 2). Evergreen. This is the #1 pine in NC.
Cones
3-6" long, oval, with small prickly spines on each scale. Persist on ground.
Size
60-90 ft (up to 115 ft). Fast-growing. Can add 2-3 ft/year when young.
Fun Fact
Makes up ~50% of all standing pine timber in the Southeast. "Loblolly" = mud puddle in old Southern dialect.
If you see a pine with needles in bundles of 3 in Wake County, it's loblolly 95% of the time. The other 5% is longleaf pine (needles 8-18", much longer). Shortleaf pine has shorter needles (3-5") in bundles of 2-3. Virginia pine has short needles (1.5-3") in bundles of 2 and is scrubby.

Hickory (Mockernut / Pignut)

Carya tomentosa / C. glabra
Possible - Mystery Trees
Bark ID (Winter)
Mockernut: tight, interlocking ridges forming a rough diamond/net pattern. Gray. Very similar to pecan but slightly tighter pattern. Pignut: tighter, more finely ridged bark. Both lack the dramatic peeling strips of shagbark hickory.
Mockernut Bark
Hickory Leaves
Hickory Nuts
Mockernut Hickory
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 8-15" with 5-9 leaflets (fewer than pecan's 9-17). This is the key difference from pecan.
Nuts
Rounder than pecans, hard thick shell. Mockernut shell is very hard to crack. Pignut tastes bitter.
Pecan vs Hickory
Pecan: 9-17 leaflets, oblong thin-shell nut. Hickory: 5-9 leaflets, round thick-shell nut.
Common in Area?
Very. Mockernut and pignut are the two most common hickories in the NC Piedmont.
Hickory wood is excellent for smoking meat. If you ever need to take one down, save the wood. Also makes fantastic firewood - highest BTU of any common hardwood.

Sweetgum

Liquidambar styraciflua
Possible - Mystery Trees
Bark ID (Winter)
Deeply furrowed, grayish-brown with narrow, rounded ridges. Older trees develop corky wings/ridges on branches (very distinctive if present). Bark looks more "rough and rough" than oak's plate-like texture.
Sweetgum Bark

Leaves (Spring)
Star-shaped with 5-7 pointed lobes. Glossy green, turning brilliant red/orange/purple in fall. Smell sweet when crushed.
The Gumballs
Spiky spherical seed balls, 1-1.5" across. The bane of barefoot walkers everywhere. Persist on ground through winter.

Look on the ground. If there are spiky balls, it's sweetgum. Period. No other common tree drops anything like them.
Size
60-80 ft. Straight trunk, pyramidal to oval crown.
If you DON'T see gumballs under the mystery trees, cross sweetgum off the list. They're impossible to miss and persist well into winter.

Tulip Poplar (Tulip Tree)

Liriodendron tulipifera
Possible - Fat Tree in Treeline
Bark ID (Winter)
Very straight trunk (more vertical than oaks). Young trees have smooth, greenish-gray bark. Mature trees develop deep, interlocking diamond-shaped furrows with flat-topped ridges. The furrows are very regular and uniform compared to the messier patterns of oak.

Leaves (Spring)
Totally unique shape: 4 lobes with a flat or notched top. Looks like someone cut the tip off a maple leaf. 4-8" across. Unmistakable.
Flowers (April-May)
Tulip-shaped, yellow-green with orange band. Beautiful but high up in the canopy, hard to see.
Fruit
Cone-like cluster of winged seeds, 2-3". Sit upright on branches like little candles. Persist through winter.
Size
80-120 ft. One of the tallest eastern hardwoods. Very straight trunk. "Fat" trunks are common on mature specimens.
If the "one fat tree" in your treeline has a very straight, tall trunk that goes up without much branching low down, it's probably tulip poplar. They're the skyscrapers of the NC Piedmont forest and extremely common. Not actually related to poplars or tulips.

Red Maple

Acer rubrum
Possible - Double Trunk / Mystery
Bark ID (Winter)
Young trees: smooth, light gray (easily mistaken for beech). Mature trees: develops darker, scaly plates that peel up at edges. Not deeply furrowed. The bark transition from smooth to platy is distinctive.
Red Maple Bark
Red Maple Leaves
Red Maple Flowers
Maple Samaras
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Classic maple shape: 3-5 lobes with shallow sinuses (notches). 2-5" across. Brilliant red fall color.
Seeds
Paired helicopter samaras, red when young (spring). Ripen and fall before most other trees even leaf out.
Why Double-Trunk?
Red maples commonly fork into multiple stems, especially if topped or damaged young. Very common multi-trunk form.
Blooming
One of the FIRST trees to bloom in spring. Tiny red flower clusters appear in late Feb / early March. Look for red fuzz on branches soon.
If your double-trunk front yard tree starts showing tiny red flowers in the next few weeks, it's red maple. They're the earliest bloomers and the red clusters are visible from a distance. Most common hardwood in the eastern US.

Black Walnut

Juglans nigra
Less Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
Very dark brown to black, deeply furrowed with rough diamond-shaped ridges. The darkest bark of any common tree in the area. Deeply textured.
Black Walnut Bark
Walnut Leaves
Walnut in Husk
Black Walnut Tree
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 12-24" with 15-23 leaflets. Similar to pecan but leaflets are more finely toothed and aromatic.
Nuts
Round, 1.5-2.5" green husks that turn black and STAIN EVERYTHING. Hard corrugated shell inside. Meat is rich and distinctive.
Killer Feature
Produces juglone, a chemical that kills tomatoes, peppers, azaleas, and many other plants within the root zone.
Confirm Now
Look for round green/black husks on the ground. They'll have stained the soil dark brown/black underneath.
If you have black walnut, be aware of juglone toxicity. Don't plant a garden within 50-80 ft of the trunk. The roots extend far. Tomatoes, peppers, blueberries, azaleas, and rhododendrons are all sensitive.

White Ash

Fraxinus americana
Less Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
The classic tight diamond/lattice pattern. Deep, interlocking ridges forming distinct diamond shapes. Gray. This pattern is so regular it almost looks man-made. Very similar to mature pecan and tulip poplar, but ash diamonds are typically the tightest and most uniform.
White Ash Bark
Ash Leaves
Ash Samaras
White Ash Tree
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 8-12" with 5-9 leaflets. Leaflets are opposite (not alternating like hickory/pecan). Dark green above, pale below.
Seeds
Single-winged paddle-shaped samaras in clusters. Not paired like maple. Hang in dense clusters through fall.
Branches
OPPOSITE branching pattern. Branches come in pairs from the trunk. This is a key ID feature shared with maple and dogwood (MAD = Maple, Ash, Dogwood).
Size
50-80 ft. Straight trunk, oval crown.
Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has been confirmed in Wake County. Signs: D-shaped exit holes (1/8"), bark splitting revealing S-shaped larval galleries, canopy dieback starting at the top, excessive woodpecker activity. If you have ash, consider preventive treatment (trunk injections of emamectin benzoate). Untreated ash have a ~99% mortality rate once EAB arrives in the area.

Sassafras

Sassafras albidum
Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
Young trees: green-brown, smooth. Mature: thick, reddish-brown with deep, irregular furrows forming flat-topped ridges. Looks corky and chunky. Scratch a young twig and it smells like root beer/fruit loops — that's the dead giveaway any time of year.
Sassafras Bark
Sassafras Leaves
Sassafras Berries
Sassafras Flowers
Sassafras Tree
Photos via Go Botany
Leaves (Spring)
The famous 3 shapes on ONE tree: unlobed (oval), 2-lobed (mitten), and 3-lobed (trident). Bright green, aromatic when crushed. Brilliant orange-red fall color.
Fruit
Small dark blue berries on bright red stalks (Sep-Oct). Birds love them. Each berry sits in a little red cup.
Size
30-60 ft typically, can reach 80 ft. Often grows in thickets via root suckers. Irregular, flat-topped crown.
Root Beer Test
Scratch any twig or root — instant root beer smell. Works year-round, even in winter. This is the only tree that does this.
Sassafras is everywhere in Wake County piedmont. It colonizes edges, fence lines, and disturbed areas aggressively via root suckers, so if you find one, there are probably more nearby. The three leaf shapes on a single tree make it one of the easiest native trees to ID once leaves are out. In winter, look for the root beer smell test and the green twigs.

Eastern Red Cedar

Juniperus virginiana
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
Thin, reddish-brown to gray, fibrous and shreddy — peels off in long vertical strips. Very distinctive. On old trees it gets more deeply furrowed but keeps that stringy, fibrous quality. Trunk often has a twisted appearance. Evergreen year-round, so foliage is always present for ID.
Shreddy Bark
Blue Berry-Cones
Full Tree Form
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Foliage
Evergreen. Scale-like foliage (mature) or needle-like (juvenile). Both forms can appear on the same tree. Dark green to blue-green. Aromatic when crushed — cedar chest smell.
Fruit
Small waxy blue "berries" (actually tiny cones), 1/4" diameter. Present on female trees fall through winter. Birds love them — this is how the tree spreads everywhere.
Size
30-50 ft typically, sometimes taller. Dense pyramidal or columnar shape when young, often irregular and ragged when old. Very variable form.
Habitat
THE pioneer species. First tree to colonize abandoned fields, fence rows, highway medians, and disturbed land. Grows anywhere, even in terrible soil. Extremely common along every road in Wake County.
Despite the name, this is actually a juniper, not a true cedar. It's the most widely distributed conifer in eastern North America. If you see a scraggly dark-green evergreen tree in an old field or along a fence line, it's almost certainly this. The wood is naturally rot-resistant and aromatic — that's what cedar closets and cedar chests are made from.

Flowering Dogwood

Cornus florida
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
Very distinctive: dark gray to black, broken into small square or rectangular blocky plates — often described as "alligator skin" or a checkerboard pattern. This bark pattern is unique among common Piedmont trees and is a reliable winter ID. Small tree, typically in the understory.
Blocky "Alligator" Bark
Spring Blooms
Red Fall Berries
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Flowers (Spring)
The iconic white (sometimes pink) "flowers" are actually four large bracts surrounding a tiny cluster of yellow-green true flowers. Bloom in April before leaves fully emerge. NC's state flower.
Fruit
Clusters of bright glossy red drupes (Sep-Oct). Each berry is oval, about 1/2" long. Important food source for birds and wildlife.
Size
15-30 ft. Understory tree. Horizontal branching pattern creates distinctive layered look. Rarely a dominant canopy tree.
Winter Buds
Look for the rounded flower buds at branch tips right now — they look like tiny gray-green buttons or onion domes. Leaf buds are narrow and pointed.
Dogwood anthracnose has killed a lot of native dogwoods in the mountains but is less severe in the Piedmont. If you have healthy dogwoods on your property, they're worth protecting. The wood is extremely hard and dense — historically used for textile shuttle spools, tool handles, and golf club heads. Look for the flower buds at branch tips right now to confirm ID before spring bloom.

American Beech

Fagus grandifolia
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
The easiest bark in the forest: smooth, light silver-gray, and stays smooth even on very old trees. No ridges, no furrows, no peeling. Looks almost like elephant skin. This is why people carve initials into beeches — that smooth surface is irresistible (but don't do it, it damages the tree and invites disease).
Smooth Gray Bark
Fall Bronze Leaves

Leaves
Simple, elliptical, 3-5" long with prominent parallel veins ending in pointed teeth. Brilliant golden-bronze in fall. Dead leaves often persist through winter (marcescence) — papery tan leaves hanging on = beech.
Fruit
Small triangular nuts in spiny husks. Husks split open into 4 sections in fall. Edible (sweet, nutty flavor) but small. Squirrels, deer, and bears love them.
Size
60-80 ft tall with a wide spreading crown. Can dominate an area because it casts such deep shade that few other trees can grow beneath it.
Winter Clue
Buds are very long, narrow, and sharply pointed — like tiny bronze-brown cigars. Combined with smooth bark + hanging dead leaves, this tree is easy to nail in winter.
If you see a tree with smooth gray bark and dead papery leaves still hanging on the branches in February, it's beech. That leaf-holding habit (marcescence) is one of the most reliable winter ID features. Beech leaf disease has been spreading in the northeast but hasn't hit NC hard yet. Common in any wooded area in Wake County.

Black Cherry

Prunus serotina
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
Changes dramatically with age. Young trees: smooth, reddish-brown with horizontal lenticels (looks like birch almost). Mature trees: very dark, thick, breaking into rough irregular scaly plates that curl outward — often described as "burnt cornflakes" or "potato chips." Peel a small piece and sniff: distinct bitter almond smell.
"Burnt Cornflake" Bark
Ripe Cherries
Spring Flower Racemes
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Flowers (Spring)
Long drooping clusters (racemes) of small white flowers, 4-6" long. Bloom in April-May after leaves emerge. Fragrant.
Fruit
Small dark purple-black cherries, 1/3" diameter, in drooping clusters. Ripe Jun-Aug. Edible but bitter raw. Used for jelly, wine, and flavoring (cherry bounce).
Size
50-80 ft. Can get quite large in the open. Straight trunk with narrow crown. One of the most valuable native hardwoods — cherry lumber is prized for furniture.
Smell Test
Scratch a twig or inner bark — bitter almond/cherry cough syrup smell. Works year-round. This is the most reliable single ID feature. Warning: leaves and bark contain cyanide compounds (toxic to livestock, not a concern for you).
This was one of the original candidates for your bark photos in the backyard. The "burnt cornflake" bark on mature trees is unlike anything else. Black cherry is one of the most common trees in Wake County — it grows in every forest, field edge, and fence line. The wood is extremely valuable (cherry furniture, cabinets). One of those bark photos from the original conversation might still turn out to be this tree.

American Persimmon

Diospyros virginiana
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
One of the most distinctive barks: very thick, dark gray to black, broken into small square or rectangular blocks in a regular pattern. Often described as "charcoal briquettes" or "alligator hide." The blocks are deeply separated by narrow furrows. This pattern is highly distinctive and one of the best winter IDs in the Piedmont.
"Charcoal Block" Bark
Ripe Fruit
Summer Foliage
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Fruit
Round orange-yellow fruits, 1-2" diameter. Astringently bitter until fully ripe (usually after first frost). Ripe fruit is incredibly sweet and custardy — one of the best wild fruits in North America. Persists into late fall/winter.
Leaves
Simple, oval, 4-6" long, glossy dark green above, paler below. Smooth edges (no teeth). Turn yellow to reddish-purple in fall.
Size
35-60 ft. Often grows in thickets via root suckers (like sassafras). Tends to colonize edges, old fields, and disturbed areas. Trunk straight with a rounded crown.
Foraging Note
DO NOT eat unripe fruit — it's intensely astringent (mouth-puckering). Wait until soft, wrinkly, and almost falling off the tree. After a hard frost they're perfect. Makes incredible pudding, bread, and homebrew.
If you find a tree with that chunky block bark pattern AND old orange fruit still hanging or on the ground beneath it in winter, you've got persimmon. The wood (also called "American ebony") is extremely hard and dense — historically used for golf club heads and billiard cues. Look for it along field edges and disturbed areas in Wake County. The fruit is incredible once you learn when to pick it.

Virginia Pine

Pinus virginiana
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
Thinner and more scaly than loblolly. Reddish-brown to dark gray with thin, flat, scaly plates. On younger trees, bark is relatively smooth with small scales. Doesn't develop the thick, platy ridges that mature loblolly gets. The tree itself is usually scraggly and irregular, which helps distinguish it from the straighter loblolly.
Thin Scaly Bark
Short Twisted Needles
Prickly Persistent Cones
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Needles
Short (1.5-3"), in bundles of 2, often twisted or slightly spiraled. This is the key distinction from loblolly (which has 6-9" needles in bundles of 3). Yellow-green, sometimes looking sparse or thin.
Cones
Small (1.5-3"), egg-shaped, with sharp prickly spines on each scale. Cones persist on the tree for years — you'll see old gray cones mixed with newer brown ones. The prickles will stab you.
Size
30-50 ft typically (much shorter than loblolly). Scraggly, irregular crown. Often looks "rough" or unkempt compared to loblolly's straighter form. Dead lower branches persist.
Quick ID vs Loblolly
Virginia pine: short needles (bundles of 2), scraggly form, smaller tree, prickly persistent cones. Loblolly: long needles (bundles of 3), straighter/taller, larger cones, dominant canopy tree.
Virginia pine is the "scrub pine" of the Piedmont — it colonizes poor, dry soils and abandoned fields where even loblolly struggles. It's a pioneer species (like red cedar) that eventually gets shaded out as larger trees grow in. Common on road cuts, poor soils, and dry ridges throughout Wake County. Not commercially valuable like loblolly, but popular as Christmas trees.

American Sycamore

Platanus occidentalis
Common Nearby
Bark ID (Winter)
Possibly the easiest tree to spot from a distance: upper trunk and branches are bright white to cream-colored where outer bark flakes off in irregular puzzle-piece patches. Lower trunk is darker with gray-brown rougher bark. The jigsaw pattern of white, cream, tan, and olive-green patches is unmistakable. You can spot a sycamore from hundreds of yards away by the white bark glowing against a winter forest.
White Puzzle Bark
Maple-like Leaves
Full Tree by Water
AI-generated botanical illustrations
Leaves
Huge (6-10" wide), star-shaped with 3-5 lobes, similar to maple but much bigger and coarser. Bright green. The largest leaves of any native tree in the eastern US.
Fruit
Round bristly seed balls ("buttonballs"), about 1" diameter, hanging on long stems. Persist through winter. Each ball is a cluster of tiny seeds with fuzzy tufts. One seed ball per stem (London plane has 2-3).
Size
75-100 ft, can exceed 130 ft. The largest deciduous tree in North America by trunk diameter. Massive spreading crown. Typically grows near water.
Habitat
Bottomlands, stream banks, floodplains. Loves wet feet. If you see a huge white-barked tree near a creek or low-lying area, it's sycamore. Also planted as a shade tree in yards and parks.
Sycamore is the "white giant" of NC waterways. Drive along any creek or river in Wake County and you'll see the white bark shining through the bare winter canopy. The biggest eastern deciduous tree — some sycamores have trunks over 10 feet in diameter. Even if you don't have one on your property, you'll see them along Middle Creek and other streams near Willow Spring.

Seasonal ID Calendar

When to look for what. Your best ID windows throughout the year.

❄️

Winter (Now)

  • Check ground for nuts, pods, gumballs, seed balls
  • Examine bark closely — blocky plates = dogwood or persimmon
  • Note branching pattern (opposite vs alternate)
  • Magnolia and red cedar still have leaves/foliage
  • Catalpa pods still hanging
  • Beech: look for tan dead leaves still clinging to branches
  • Sycamore: white bark glows from across the forest
  • Scratch sassafras/cherry twigs for smell test
🌱

Spring (Mar-May)

  • Red maple blooms first (Feb-Mar, red fuzz)
  • Dogwood blooms (April) — white bracts are unmistakable
  • Leaf shapes nail the ID
  • Sassafras: 3 leaf shapes on one tree (mitten, trident, oval)
  • Catalpa gets huge heart-shaped leaves
  • Tulip poplar: unique 4-lobe flat-top leaf
  • Count pecan vs hickory leaflets
  • Black cherry: long drooping white flower clusters
