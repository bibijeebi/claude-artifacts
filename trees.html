Winter Ground-Clue Cheat Sheet

Right now (February), leaves are gone. The fastest way to ID deciduous trees is to check what's on the ground underneath them.

What's on the Ground Tree Key Detail
Small round acorns (<1") Water Oak or Willow Oak Most common yard oaks in Wake County
Larger acorns with bumpy cap White Oak or Red Oak White oak caps are warty; red oak caps are flat/scaly
Oblong nuts in 4-part husks Pecan Husks split into 4 segments when dry
Round nuts, hard shell Hickory Rounder than pecans; mockernut and pignut common here
Spiky gumballs Sweetgum Unmistakable. Step on barefoot once, you'll know.
Tennis-ball-sized green nuts Black Walnut Stain everything black/brown. Smell like citrus when crushed.
Long bean/cigar seed pods (10-20") Catalpa Pods persist through winter. This is your "tobacco tree."
Cone-like aggregate fruit Tulip Poplar Small (2-3"), look like upright candle holders on branches
Red fuzzy cone with seeds Southern Magnolia Seeds are bright red/orange. Tree keeps leaves year-round.
Pine cones + long needles in bundles of 3 Loblolly Pine 6-9" needles. Most common pine in NC Piedmont.
Helicopter seeds (samaras) Red Maple or Ash Maple = paired wings; Ash = single paddle-shaped
Root beer smell from scratched twig Sassafras Green twigs, mitten-shaped leaves in fall litter. Only tree with 3 leaf shapes.

Tree Profiles

Every species that could be on your property. Sorted by likelihood.

Oak (Multiple Species)

Quercus spp.
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Highly variable by species. White oak: light gray, shallow furrows, flaky plates. Water oak: dark gray-black, smooth when young, rough ridges when mature. Willow oak: gray-brown, relatively smooth with narrow ridges. Southern red oak: dark, deeply furrowed with flat ridges.

White Oak Leaves
White Oak Acorns
Willow Oak Leaf
Willow Oak Bark
Red Oak Bark
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
White: rounded lobes. Water: spatula-shaped. Willow: narrow like willow. Red: pointed lobes with bristle tips.
Acorn Size
Water/Willow: small, <1/2". White: 3/4-1", warty cap. Red: 1/2-1", flat scaly cap.
Height
60-100 ft mature. Massive canopy spread.
Acorn Pain Index
Moderate to high. White oak acorns are the worst barefoot.
To narrow down which oak: check acorn shape right now. Small and round = water or willow oak (most common in Wake County yards). Larger with a deep bumpy cap = white oak. Look at the branching pattern too: willow oaks have a clean, upright crown; water oaks spread wide and messy.
🥜

Pecan

Carya illinoinensis
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Gray to gray-brown with shallow irregular furrows and flattened ridges. On mature trees, bark develops interlocking ridges that can look like a tight diamond pattern. Looks similar to ash and hickory. Less "shaggy" than shagbark hickory, but chunkier than ash.
Pecan Bark
Pecan Leaves & Nuts
Pecan Husks Opening
Shelled Pecans
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 12-20" long with 9-17 lance-shaped leaflets. Leaflets are slightly curved. Come in late (May).
Nuts
Oblong, 1-2" long, thin shell. Enclosed in a husk that splits into 4 sections when ripe (Oct-Nov).
Size
70-100 ft tall, can reach 150 ft. Massive trunk up to 6 ft diameter. Largest hickory species.
Crown Shape
Broadly oval, symmetrical. Lower branches sweep wide on open-grown trees.
Pecans need cross-pollination. If you have one pecan producing well, there's likely another pecan (or hickory) nearby acting as a pollinator. Your two mystery trees might include a second pecan. Check the ground under them for old husks.
Free pecans every fall. Beat the squirrels by harvesting as soon as husks start splitting (usually October in Wake County). Shake branches or wait for windy days. Store in-shell pecans in the freezer, they'll keep for 2+ years.

Catalpa ("Tobacco Tree")

Catalpa bignonioides / C. speciosa
Confirmed
Bark ID (Winter)
Gray-brown, breaking into irregular scaly plates. Relatively thin bark compared to oaks. Twigs are stout with large circular leaf scars. The dead-giveaway is the long cigar-like seed pods hanging from branches all winter.
Catalpa Bark
Catalpa Leaf
Catalpa Flowers
Catalpa Seed Pods
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
HUGE heart-shaped leaves, 6-12" long. Smell slightly unpleasant when crushed. This is why people call it "tobacco" — big leaves.
Seed Pods
10-20" long, thin, cylindrical. Look like green beans or cigars. Turn brown and persist through winter, splitting to release winged seeds.
Flowers (May-June)
Showy white clusters with purple/yellow spots. Actually really pretty. Orchid-like.
Size
30-60 ft. Irregular, spreading crown. Often looks messy/wild.
Catalpa is NOT tobacco. Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) is an annual herb that grows 3-6 ft tall, not a tree. People confuse them because of the large leaves. Also known as "Indian bean tree," "cigar tree," and "catawba" (corruption of the same name). Fishermen love catalpas because catalpa worms (sphinx moth caterpillars) are premium fishing bait.
🌲

Loblolly Pine

Pinus taeda
Confirmed
Bark ID (Year-Round)
Young trees: dark, furrowed bark. Mature trees: thick, reddish-brown to gray plates separated by deep furrows. Plates are roughly rectangular. Very distinctive once you know what to look for. Smells like pine resin if you scratch it.
Loblolly Pine Bark
Loblolly Pine Cones
Loblolly Pines
Old Growth Loblolly
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Needles
6-9" long, in bundles of 3 (occasionally 2). Evergreen. This is the #1 pine in NC.
Cones
3-6" long, oval, with small prickly spines on each scale. Persist on ground.
Size
60-90 ft (up to 115 ft). Fast-growing. Can add 2-3 ft/year when young.
Fun Fact
Makes up ~50% of all standing pine timber in the Southeast. "Loblolly" = mud puddle in old Southern dialect.
If you see a pine with needles in bundles of 3 in Wake County, it's loblolly 95% of the time. The other 5% is longleaf pine (needles 8-18", much longer). Shortleaf pine has shorter needles (3-5") in bundles of 2-3. Virginia pine has short needles (1.5-3") in bundles of 2 and is scrubby.

Southern Magnolia

Magnolia grandiflora
Probable
Bark ID (Year-Round)
Smooth, gray to gray-brown. Stays relatively smooth even on old trees (thin, tight bark). Not deeply furrowed like oaks. Can develop some shallow furrows with age.
Magnolia Flower
Magnolia Leaves
Magnolia Seed Pod
Southern Magnolia
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Year-Round)
EVERGREEN. Thick, leathery, glossy dark green on top, fuzzy brown/rust underneath. 5-10" long. This is the dead giveaway.
Flowers (May-July)
Huge white fragrant flowers, 8-12" across. Iconic Southern tree flower.
Fruit
Cone-like aggregate, 3-5" long, fuzzy red-brown. Seeds are bright red/orange, dangling on threads.
Confirm Now
Walk up to it. If it has big glossy green leaves RIGHT NOW in February, it's magnolia.

Hickory (Mockernut / Pignut)

Carya tomentosa / C. glabra
Possible - Mystery Trees
Bark ID (Winter)
Mockernut: tight, interlocking ridges forming a rough diamond/net pattern. Gray. Very similar to pecan but slightly tighter pattern. Pignut: tighter, more finely ridged bark. Both lack the dramatic peeling strips of shagbark hickory.
Mockernut Bark
Hickory Leaves
Hickory Nuts
Mockernut Hickory
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 8-15" with 5-9 leaflets (fewer than pecan's 9-17). This is the key difference from pecan.
Nuts
Rounder than pecans, hard thick shell. Mockernut shell is very hard to crack. Pignut tastes bitter.
Pecan vs Hickory
Pecan: 9-17 leaflets, oblong thin-shell nut. Hickory: 5-9 leaflets, round thick-shell nut.
Common in Area?
Very. Mockernut and pignut are the two most common hickories in the NC Piedmont.
Hickory wood is excellent for smoking meat. If you ever need to take one down, save the wood. Also makes fantastic firewood - highest BTU of any common hardwood.
🦔

Sweetgum

Liquidambar styraciflua
Possible - Mystery Trees
Bark ID (Winter)
Deeply furrowed, grayish-brown with narrow, rounded ridges. Older trees develop corky wings/ridges on branches (very distinctive if present). Bark looks more "rough and rough" than oak's plate-like texture.
Sweetgum Bark
Sweetgum Leaf
Sweetgum Ball
Sweetgum Tree
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Star-shaped with 5-7 pointed lobes. Glossy green, turning brilliant red/orange/purple in fall. Smell sweet when crushed.
The Gumballs
Spiky spherical seed balls, 1-1.5" across. The bane of barefoot walkers everywhere. Persist on ground through winter.
Confirm Now
Look on the ground. If there are spiky balls, it's sweetgum. Period. No other common tree drops anything like them.
Size
60-80 ft. Straight trunk, pyramidal to oval crown.
If you DON'T see gumballs under the mystery trees, cross sweetgum off the list. They're impossible to miss and persist well into winter.

Tulip Poplar (Tulip Tree)

Liriodendron tulipifera
Possible - Fat Tree in Treeline
Bark ID (Winter)
Very straight trunk (more vertical than oaks). Young trees have smooth, greenish-gray bark. Mature trees develop deep, interlocking diamond-shaped furrows with flat-topped ridges. The furrows are very regular and uniform compared to the messier patterns of oak.
Tulip Poplar Bark
Tulip Poplar Leaf
Tulip Tree Flower
Seed Aggregate
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Totally unique shape: 4 lobes with a flat or notched top. Looks like someone cut the tip off a maple leaf. 4-8" across. Unmistakable.
Flowers (April-May)
Tulip-shaped, yellow-green with orange band. Beautiful but high up in the canopy, hard to see.
Fruit
Cone-like cluster of winged seeds, 2-3". Sit upright on branches like little candles. Persist through winter.
Size
80-120 ft. One of the tallest eastern hardwoods. Very straight trunk. "Fat" trunks are common on mature specimens.
If the "one fat tree" in your treeline has a very straight, tall trunk that goes up without much branching low down, it's probably tulip poplar. They're the skyscrapers of the NC Piedmont forest and extremely common. Not actually related to poplars or tulips.

Red Maple

Acer rubrum
Possible - Double Trunk / Mystery
Bark ID (Winter)
Young trees: smooth, light gray (easily mistaken for beech). Mature trees: develops darker, scaly plates that peel up at edges. Not deeply furrowed. The bark transition from smooth to platy is distinctive.
Red Maple Bark
Red Maple Leaves
Red Maple Flowers
Maple Samaras
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Classic maple shape: 3-5 lobes with shallow sinuses (notches). 2-5" across. Brilliant red fall color.
Seeds
Paired helicopter samaras, red when young (spring). Ripen and fall before most other trees even leaf out.
Why Double-Trunk?
Red maples commonly fork into multiple stems, especially if topped or damaged young. Very common multi-trunk form.
Blooming
One of the FIRST trees to bloom in spring. Tiny red flower clusters appear in late Feb / early March. Look for red fuzz on branches soon.
If your double-trunk front yard tree starts showing tiny red flowers in the next few weeks, it's red maple. They're the earliest bloomers and the red clusters are visible from a distance. Most common hardwood in the eastern US.
🟤

Black Walnut

Juglans nigra
Less Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
Very dark brown to black, deeply furrowed with rough diamond-shaped ridges. The darkest bark of any common tree in the area. Deeply textured.
Black Walnut Bark
Walnut Leaves
Walnut in Husk
Black Walnut Tree
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 12-24" with 15-23 leaflets. Similar to pecan but leaflets are more finely toothed and aromatic.
Nuts
Round, 1.5-2.5" green husks that turn black and STAIN EVERYTHING. Hard corrugated shell inside. Meat is rich and distinctive.
Killer Feature
Produces juglone, a chemical that kills tomatoes, peppers, azaleas, and many other plants within the root zone.
Confirm Now
Look for round green/black husks on the ground. They'll have stained the soil dark brown/black underneath.
If you have black walnut, be aware of juglone toxicity. Don't plant a garden within 50-80 ft of the trunk. The roots extend far. Tomatoes, peppers, blueberries, azaleas, and rhododendrons are all sensitive.

White Ash

Fraxinus americana
Less Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
The classic tight diamond/lattice pattern. Deep, interlocking ridges forming distinct diamond shapes. Gray. This pattern is so regular it almost looks man-made. Very similar to mature pecan and tulip poplar, but ash diamonds are typically the tightest and most uniform.
White Ash Bark
Ash Leaves
Ash Samaras
White Ash Tree
Photos via Go Botany / Wikimedia Commons
Leaves (Spring)
Compound, 8-12" with 5-9 leaflets. Leaflets are opposite (not alternating like hickory/pecan). Dark green above, pale below.
Seeds
Single-winged paddle-shaped samaras in clusters. Not paired like maple. Hang in dense clusters through fall.
Branches
OPPOSITE branching pattern. Branches come in pairs from the trunk. This is a key ID feature shared with maple and dogwood (MAD = Maple, Ash, Dogwood).
Size
50-80 ft. Straight trunk, oval crown.
Emerald Ash Borer (EAB) has been confirmed in Wake County. Signs: D-shaped exit holes (1/8"), bark splitting revealing S-shaped larval galleries, canopy dieback starting at the top, excessive woodpecker activity. If you have ash, consider preventive treatment (trunk injections of emamectin benzoate). Untreated ash have a ~99% mortality rate once EAB arrives in the area.

Sassafras

Sassafras albidum
Likely
Bark ID (Winter)
Young trees: green-brown, smooth. Mature: thick, reddish-brown with deep, irregular furrows forming flat-topped ridges. Looks corky and chunky. Scratch a young twig and it smells like root beer/fruit loops — that's the dead giveaway any time of year.
Sassafras Bark
Sassafras Leaves
Sassafras Berries
Sassafras Flowers
Sassafras Tree
Photos via Go Botany
Leaves (Spring)
The famous 3 shapes on ONE tree: unlobed (oval), 2-lobed (mitten), and 3-lobed (trident). Bright green, aromatic when crushed. Brilliant orange-red fall color.
Fruit
Small dark blue berries on bright red stalks (Sep-Oct). Birds love them. Each berry sits in a little red cup.
Size
30-60 ft typically, can reach 80 ft. Often grows in thickets via root suckers. Irregular, flat-topped crown.
Root Beer Test
Scratch any twig or root — instant root beer smell. Works year-round, even in winter. This is the only tree that does this.
Sassafras is everywhere in Wake County piedmont. It colonizes edges, fence lines, and disturbed areas aggressively via root suckers, so if you find one, there are probably more nearby. The three leaf shapes on a single tree make it one of the easiest native trees to ID once leaves are out. In winter, look for the root beer smell test and the green twigs.

Seasonal ID Calendar

When to look for what. Your best ID windows throughout the year.

❄️

Winter (Now)

  • Check ground for nuts, pods, gumballs
  • Examine bark closely
  • Note branching pattern (opposite vs alternate)
  • Magnolia still has leaves
  • Catalpa pods still hanging
🌱

Spring (Mar-May)

  • Red maple blooms first (Feb-Mar, red fuzz)
  • Leaf shapes nail the ID
  • Catalpa gets huge heart-shaped leaves
  • Tulip poplar: unique 4-lobe flat-top leaf
  • Count pecan vs hickory leaflets
☀️

Summer (Jun-Aug)

  • Catalpa flowers (white + purple)
  • Magnolia flowers (huge, white, fragrant)
  • Green pecans/walnuts developing
  • Full canopy comparison
🍂

Fall (Sep-Nov)

  • Pecan harvest (Oct-Nov)
  • Acorn drop for oak ID
  • Sweetgum gumballs dropping
  • Walnut husks turning black
  • Maple helicopter seeds
