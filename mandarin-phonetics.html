A visual guide to the sounds that make Mandarin tick. Three tongue positions, zero voicing, and why pinyin letters don't mean what you think.
音

Mandarin has three sets of hissing/hushing consonants where English has roughly one. The difference between them is tongue position, and the pairs within each set differ by aspiration (a puff of air), not voicing. Mandarin has basically zero voiced obstruents.

The Three Tongue Positions

This is the whole framework. Put your tongue in three places and you've got Mandarin's sibilant system. Everything else is details.

Alveolar (z c s)
Retroflex (zh ch sh r)
Palatal (j q x)

Series 1: Alveolar

Tongue tip at tooth ridge
Sharp, hissy. Like English "s" sounds. Your tongue tip touches or approaches the bumpy ridge right behind your top front teeth.
z [ts] c [tsʰ] s [s]

Series 2: Retroflex

Tongue tip curled way back
Darker, thicker. Curl your tongue tip up and back so it points at the roof of your mouth behind the ridge. Sounds more "buzzy."
zh [ʈʂ] ch [ʈʂʰ] sh [ʂ] r [ɻ]

Series 3: Alveolo-palatal

Tongue blade flat on hard palate
Bright, "squeezed." Flatten the broad part of your tongue against the hard palate. Almost like saying "she" while smiling wide.
j [tɕ] q [tɕʰ] x [ɕ]

⚠ The Big Misconception

English speakers see "zh" and think "voiced sh." It's not.

Nothing in Mandarin uses English-style voicing contrasts. The pairs zh/ch, j/q, z/c are all distinguished by aspiration: hold your hand in front of your mouth. The aspirated one (ch, q, c) gives you a puff of air. The unaspirated one (zh, j, z) doesn't. Both are technically voiceless.

This is the single biggest thing English speakers get wrong about Mandarin consonants. English "b vs p" = voiced vs voiceless. Mandarin "b vs p" = unaspirated vs aspirated. Completely different system.

Aspiration, Not Voicing

English distinguishes b from p by whether your vocal cords vibrate (voicing). Mandarin doesn't care about that. Instead it distinguishes by whether a burst of air comes out (aspiration). This is why Mandarin "b" is actually IPA [p] — it's voiceless, just without the puff.

The hand test: Hold your palm 2 inches from your mouth. Say "pin" — you feel a puff. Say "spin" — no puff. English "p" in "spin" is actually closer to Mandarin "b" than English "b" is.

Other Sounds That Trip People Up

ü[y]Rounded front vowel. Say "ee" with your lips rounded like "oo." Same as French "u" or German "ü"
e[ɤ]Back unrounded. Start saying "uh" but keep your tongue pulled back. Not like any English vowel

💡 The "Spin" Test

Here's the fastest way to internalize aspiration vs. voicing:

Say "spin" in English. That "p" has no puff (unaspirated). That's Mandarin b [p].

Now say "pin." Big puff. That's Mandarin p [pʰ].

Do the same with "skit" (Mandarin g) vs. "kit" (Mandarin k), and "star" (Mandarin d) vs. "tar" (Mandarin t).

The Bottom Line

Mandarin's consonant system runs on two things: where your tongue goes and whether air puffs out. Not voicing. Nail these three tongue positions and the aspiration distinction and you're 90% of the way there.
