L34 - Population Genetics 1: HWE

Core Concepts

  • Population: local group of a species that can interbreed
  • Gene pool: all alleles in a population
  • Allele frequency = gene frequency (used interchangeably)
  • Gametic array: allele frequencies (e.g., 0.6M + 0.4N)
  • Genotypic array: genotype frequencies (e.g., 0.36 MM + 0.48 MN + 0.16 NN)

Calculating Frequencies (4 decimal places!)

f(allele) = (2 × homozygotes + heterozygotes) / (2 × total individuals)
f(genotype) = # of that genotype / total individuals
f(A) = f(AA) + ½f(Aa)

Always: f(A) + f(a) = 1 and f(AA) + f(Aa) + f(aa) = 1

Hardy-Weinberg Law

Allele and genotype frequencies stay at equilibrium after one generation of random mating IF all 5 assumptions are met:

Mnemonic "MR. MIS": Mutation (none), Random mating (panmictic), Migration (none), Infinite population, Selection (none, all genotypes equally fit)
p² AA + 2pq Aa + q² aa = 1 (where p + q = 1)

Chi-Square Test for HWE

  1. Calculate observed allele freqs p and q from data
  2. Get expected genotype freqs: p², 2pq, q²
  3. Multiply expected freqs × total = expected counts
  4. Compute χ² = Σ(O-E)²/E
  5. df = # genotypes − # alleles (usually 3 − 2 = 1)
  6. Critical value at df=1, α=0.05 is 3.84
  7. If χ² > 3.84: reject H₀, NOT in HWE
Why fewer df than other χ² tests? You used the data itself to calculate the expected frequencies (computed p and q from observed), losing 1 additional df.

Carrier Frequency Problems

Given frequency of recessive homozygote (e.g., 1 in 10,000 albinism):

q² = 1/10000 = 0.0001
q = √0.0001 = 0.01
p = 1 − q = 0.99
2pq = 2(0.99)(0.01) = 0.0198 carriers

L35 - Population Genetics 2: Violations of HWE

1. Mutation

Source of new alleles. Changes frequency VERY slowly.

p₁ = (1−μ)p₀ + νq₀ [μ = A→a rate, ν = a→A rate]
Mutation rates typically 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁶. At μ=10⁻⁵, after 20,000 generations only 20% of A alleles have turned to a. Keep ALL decimal places on mutation problems.

2. Migration

p' = (1−m)p + mP [P=mainland freq, p=island freq, m=migration rate]

It's a weighted average. m = immigrants/(total after migration).

Important: Migration gives you post-migration allele freqs — but the population is NOT in HWE. To restore HWE, randomly mate 1 generation without violations.

3. Selection & Fitness

Fitness (w): ability to survive and reproduce. Relative, scaled from 0 to 1.

Modelw(AA)w(Aa)w(aa)
No selection1.01.01.0
Recessive lethal1.01.00
Dominant lethal1.000
Additive1.00.80.6
Overdominance (het advantage)1.01.30.2
Underdominance (het disadvantage)1.00.30.7

Selection Types (mental models)

  • Directional: favors one extreme. Mean shifts.
  • Disruptive = Underdominance: favors both extremes. Bimodal.
  • Stabilizing = Overdominance: favors heterozygotes. Maintains polymorphism. Decreases variance.

Viability Selection Math

W̄ = p²W(AA) + 2pqW(Aa) + q²W(aa) [average fitness]

Steps:

  1. Multiply each genotype freq by its fitness (unadjusted survivors)
  2. Sum these = W̄
  3. Divide each by W̄ to get post-selection genotype freqs
  4. Calculate new allele freqs from post-selection genotypes

4. Non-Random Mating

Non-random mating changes genotype frequencies but NOT allele frequencies.
  • Positive assortative: like mates with like → more homozygous at mate-selection loci only
  • Negative assortative: opposites attract → more heterozygous at those loci
  • Inbreeding: mating of relatives → more homozygous at ALL loci

Inbreeding Coefficient (F)

(p² + Fpq) AA + 2(1−F)pq Aa + (q² + Fpq) aa

Solve: set p² + Fpq = observed f(AA), plug in p and q, solve for F.

Under systematic inbreeding, eventually everyone is homozygous: population becomes pAA + qaa.

5. Small Population Size

  • Genetic drift: random change in allele frequency from generation to generation due to sampling error. Leads to random fixation or loss.
  • Founder effect: small colonizing population; allele freqs may differ from origin.
  • Bottleneck: disaster wipes out most of population; survivors rebuild with potentially different freqs.

L36 - Evolution & Phylogeny

Evolution

Genetic changes in a population's composition: emergence, divergence, extinction of species. Requires variation, heredity, selection.

Two-step process: (1) genetic change occurs (mutation/recombination), (2) allele freqs change (selection/drift).

Heterozygosity Theories

  • Classical: low heterozygosity is best (organisms optimized for their environment)
  • Balance: high heterozygosity is best (flexibility for changing environment)
  • Neutralist: most polymorphisms are neutral
  • Selectionist: polymorphisms maintained by selection
  • Nearly Neutral Model: both are probably true sometimes

Speciation & Reproductive Isolation

Biological Species Concept: members can interbreed and produce fertile progeny.

  • Prezygotic: don't/can't mate (habitat, behavioral, temporal, mechanical, gametic isolation)
  • Postzygotic: progeny sterile or inviable (hybrid sterility/inviability)

Types of Speciation

  • Allopatric: geographic barrier blocks gene flow. Darwin's finches — 14 species from 1 ancestor on Galapagos.
  • Sympatric: within a single interbreeding population, no geographic barrier. Apple maggot fly (hawthorn vs apple) — 2% gene flow. Also allopolyploidy from hybridization.

Types of Evolutionary Change

  • Anagenesis: evolution WITHIN a lineage over time
  • Cladogenesis: SPLITTING of one lineage into two → biological diversity

Homologs

  • Paralogs: homologs in same species via gene duplication (e.g., human globin genes)
  • Orthologs: homologs in different species
Mnemonic: Paralogs = Pairs in one species (duplication). Orthologs = Other species.

Different Genome Regions Evolve at Different Rates

  • 5' flanking / promoter — moderate (transcription signals matter)
  • UTRs (leader/trailer) — moderate (RNA processing signals)
  • Introns — faster (removed, less constraint)
  • Exons — slow (code for protein)
  • Pseudogenes — FASTEST (no function, no selection)

Molecular Clock

Assumes constant mutation rate → sequence differences between species used to date evolutionary events.

Phylogenetic Trees

  • OTU: Operational Taxonomic Unit (species, strain, allele)
  • Rooted tree: has an outgroup (known to have diverged earliest); shows common ancestry + order of divergence
  • Unrooted tree: only distances, no common ancestor or order
  • Nodes: terminal (OTUs at tips), internal (common ancestors)

Tree Construction Approaches

  • Distance: uses overall similarity (UPGMA) — what we'll use
  • Parsimony: minimum number of evolutionary changes
  • Maximum Likelihood / Bayesian: maximum probability
UPGMA = Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean. You MUST be able to do this. See Math tab.

L37 - Quantitative Genetics

Qualitative vs Quantitative Traits

  • Qualitative: discrete classes, discontinuous F2 variation (Mendelian)
  • Quantitative: continuous variation, no distinct F2 classes (height, weight, yield)

East's Multiple Factor Hypothesis

  • Multiple genes → additive effects
  • Each gene has small effect
  • Environment important
  • Smooths the distribution curve

Additive Allele Effects

Each dominant allele adds same amount. Example: aabb = 10", each A or B adds 5" → AABB = 30", AaBb = 20", etc.

Threshold Traits

Accumulation of "risk" alleles; trait shows above a threshold. Can appear discrete despite continuous underlying variation.

Variance Partitioning

P = G + E (phenotype = genotype + environment)
G = A + D (additive + dominance)
Vp = VG + VE (+ VGE we ignore)
VG = VA + VD (+ VI epistatic we ignore)

Heritability

Broad-sense: H² = VG/Vp
Narrow-sense: h² = VA/Vp

Range 0 to 1. Higher = more selection progress expected.

LIMITATIONS (frequently tested!):
  • Heritability does NOT say how much genes affect the trait — it says how much genes affect variation in the trait.
  • An individual does NOT have heritability.
  • h²=0.8 for height ≠ "80% of my height is genetic." It means 80% of variation in that population is genetic.
  • Specific to population AND environment. Different pop or environment = different h².
  • Assumes environment for related individuals isn't more similar than for unrelated (hard to meet in humans).

Estimating VE from Pure Lines

VE = [V(P1) + V(P2) + V(F1)] / 3

Parent and F1 generations each have 1 genotype → all variation is environmental.

VG = V(F2) − VE [F2 variance estimates Vp]
H² = VG / V(F2)

Estimating VA via Backcrosses

V(B1) = V(B2) = ½VA + VD + VE
VA = 2·V(F2) − [V(B1) + V(B2)]
h² = VA / Vp

Mass Selection (Realized Heritability)

Response: R = X₁ − X₀ (offspring mean − parent gen mean)
Selection differential: S = Xs − X₀ (selected parents mean − parent gen mean)
h² = R/S (realized heritability = narrow-sense)
Predicted X₁ = X₀ + R = X₀ + h²S

Mass Selection as a Percentage

Use tabulated values based on proportion (p) selected:

p0.50.250.10.050.01
I (intensity)0.7981.2711.7552.0632.665
Z (std point)00.6741.2821.6452.326
Truncation point: XT = μ + Zσ
Selection differential: S = Iσ
Response: R = h²S
Next gen mean: X₁ = X₀ + R
Use negative Z and I when selecting the LEFT tail (want smaller values).

Limits to Selection Response

  • Genetic variation lost as favorable homozygotes fix
  • Extreme phenotypes may be unhealthy (small mice sterile, large turkeys can't breed)
  • Negatively correlated traits (pleiotropy)

QTL Mapping

  • QTL: Quantitative Trait Loci
  • SNPs used as markers
  • GWAS: scans whole genome in biological population (not controlled crosses)
  • Reference panels: DGRP (Drosophila Genetic Reference Panel, Trudy Mackay), Mouse Collaborative Cross (8 inbred lines)

Twin Studies

  • MZ (monozygotic): 100% same genome
  • DZ (dizygotic): 50% shared (like siblings)
  • Concordant: twins same for trait. Discordant: differ.
  • High MZ : low DZ → high heritability (genes drive variation)
  • Similar MZ and DZ → environment drives variation

L38 - Cancer Genetics

Cancer Cells

  • Uncontrolled cell division (altered cell cycle)
  • Metastasize (spread to other sites)
  • Hyperplasia + Anaplasia + Metastasis = cancer

Cell Cycle Checkpoints

  • G1/S: proper cell size, undamaged DNA (RB, p53 work here)
  • G2/M: replication & DNA repair complete
  • M: proper spindle formation/attachment

Two classes of regulatory proteins: cyclins (structural) + protein kinases (phosphorylate).

Cancer is Genetic, Rarely Heritable

  • Most cancers sporadic, environmental influence
  • Migrants take on host country's cancer rates
  • Cancer incidence rises with age
  • Consistent with multi-hit model — accumulating mutations over time
  • ~2/3 of cancer mutations come from replication errors (tissues with more divisions → higher cancer rates)

Two Types of Cancer-Related Genes

Tumor SuppressorsProto-oncogenes → Oncogenes
Normal functionPrevent divisionPromote division
Mutation typeLoss of functionGain of function
ActionRecessive (need both hits)Dominant (one hit)
ExamplesRB, BRCA1/2, p53, APCc-myc, ras, bcr-abl
Mnemonic: Suppressors are Shy (recessive, need 2 hits). Oncogenes are Overachievers (dominant, 1 hit).

Knudson's Two-Hit Hypothesis

For tumor suppressors, BOTH copies must be knocked out in the same cell. Applied to retinoblastoma (RB gene):

  • 40% of cases inherited — one mutant allele in zygote, only need 1 more hit
  • 60% sporadic — need 2 independent somatic hits
  • RB normally blocks E2F at G1/S; loss allows E2F → replication

Key Tumor Suppressors

  • RB: G1/S checkpoint; represses E2F until late G1
  • BRCA1 & BRCA2: double-strand break repair. 5-10% of all breast cancers, 20-25% of hereditary. 55-65% of BRCA1 carriers develop breast cancer.
  • p53: chromosome 17, G1 checkpoint. "Fork in road" — if DNA damaged, delays division or triggers apoptosis. Mutated in ~50% of all human tumors.

Haploinsufficiency

Usually both tumor suppressor copies must go, BUT sometimes losing ONE copy is enough — one functional copy doesn't make enough product. Example: Bloom Syndrome (BLM gene, DNA helicase for DSB repair). Heterozygotes have elevated cancer risk.

Key Oncogene Examples

  • Burkitt's Lymphoma: reciprocal translocation t(8;14) puts c-myc next to enhancer → high c-myc expression
  • CML (Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia): Philadelphia chromosome = t(9;22) → bcr-abl fusion. 90% of CML patients have it.
Burkitt = 8/14. CML/Philly = 9/22. Both reciprocal translocations involving oncogenes.

Retroviruses Cause Cancer By:

  • Mutating/rearranging proto-oncogenes
  • Inserting a strong promoter near a proto-oncogene

Clonal Evolution

Tumors accumulate more mutations over time → more aggressive. Can affect: cell cycle, signal transduction, DNA repair, telomere length, chromosome segregation (aneuploidy), vascularization.

DNA Repair Defects → Cancer

  • Xeroderma pigmentosum: defective NER (nucleotide excision repair) → skin cancer (can't fix pyrimidine dimers)
  • HNPCC/Lynch syndrome: defective mismatch repair → colorectal, endometrial, stomach
  • BRCA1/2: defective double-strand break repair

Telomeres & Cancer

  • Normally telomeres shorten with age → cell death
  • Telomerase active in germline, NOT somatic cells
  • Tumor cells often express telomerase → "immortal"
  • Potential therapy: block telomerase

Angiogenesis

Tumors need new blood vessels. Growth factors overexpressed, inhibitors underexpressed.

Metastasis causes 90% of cancer deaths.

L39 - Biotechnology 1: Recombinant DNA, Golden Rice, Forensics

Recombinant DNA Basics

  • Restriction enzyme: endonuclease that cuts dsDNA at specific sequence
  • Palindrome: reads same 5'→3' on both strands (e.g., GAATTC/CTTAAG for EcoRI)
  • DNA ligase: joins sticky/blunt ends
  • Vector: carrier DNA (plasmid) that independently replicates

Ideal Cloning Vector Has:

  1. Origin of replication
  2. Selectable/insertional markers
  3. Single cut site per restriction enzyme

Marker Types

  • Selectable: gene that lets cells survive something (antibiotic resistance). Example: ampᴿ on pBR322.
  • Insertional: gene that gets disrupted when DNA inserts. Example: tetᴿ disrupted by insert — recombinant cells are amp-resistant but tet-sensitive.

Blue-White Screening

Uses LacZ gene as insertional marker:

  • Intact LacZ + X-gal → BLUE colonies (no insert)
  • Disrupted LacZ by insert → WHITE colonies (have recombinant DNA)
  • No colony = cell didn't take up any DNA

Golden Rice Story (multi-step — frequently tested)

  1. Problem: 250M kids vitamin A deficient. Rice endosperm has no β-carotene.
  2. Rice missing: enzymes to convert GGPP → phytoene → lycopene.
  3. Solution: Add PSY (from daffodil/Narcissus pseudonarcissus, later maize) + CRTI (from bacteria).
  4. Isolate mRNA → reverse transcriptase → cDNA
  5. Put in expression vector with endosperm-specific promoter + poly-A signal + transgenic marker + Ti LB/RB
  6. Use Ti plasmid from Agrobacterium tumefaciens to insert into rice genome (remove tumor-causing genes, keep transfer functions)
  7. Verify: Southern blot (DNA integration), Western blot (protein expression)
  8. Golden Rice 2 (maize PSY) = 30 μg β-carotene/g; 72g would satisfy a kid's RDA

cDNA

Made from mRNA by reverse transcriptase (RNA-dependent DNA polymerase). Useful because eukaryotic mRNA is already spliced (no introns).

Expression Vectors

Additional requirement vs cloning vectors: promoter + sequences needed for transcription/translation in host.

SNoW DRoP: Southern=DNA, Northern=RNA, Western=Protein

Sanger (Dideoxy) Sequencing

  • DNA polymerase needs 3'-OH to extend
  • Dideoxy nucleotide (ddNTP) lacks 3'-OH → chain terminates
  • One ddNTP type per tube (ddATP, ddCTP, ddGTP, ddTTP)
  • Separate on polyacrylamide gel, read bands from bottom (smallest) to top (largest) across 4 lanes

PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)

  • Inventor: Kary Mullis
  • Uses Taq polymerase (thermostable, from Thermus aquaticus)
  • 3 steps per cycle:
    1. Denature: 95°C — strands separate
    2. Anneal: 50-65°C — primers bind
    3. Extend: 72°C — Taq adds nucleotides

PCR Limitations

  • Must know sequence around target (for primers)
  • Easily contaminated
  • Taq doesn't proofread (error rate ~1 in 20,000 bp)
  • Small fragments (~2000 bp typical, up to 50,000 with modifications)

Gel Electrophoresis

  • DNA is negative → migrates to positive (red) pole ("Run to Red")
  • Small fragments move faster
  • Distance migrated is inversely proportional to log(size)

STR / VNTR / Microsatellites

  • STR: Short Tandem Repeats
  • VNTR: Variable Number of Tandem Repeats
  • Different alleles = different numbers of repeats = different fragment lengths
  • Must be polymorphic (many forms) to be useful

Paternity Testing Logic

  • Child must receive ONE allele from each parent at every locus
  • If alleged father has neither of child's paternal alleles at ANY locus → EXCLUDED
  • Multiple loci → more confident ID

Forensics

Suspect excluded if doesn't match EXACTLY at all loci. Match = not excluded (not "identified").

CODIS

  • Combined DNA Index System, FBI database
  • 20 polymorphic loci (extended from 13 in Feb 2017)
  • Loci assort independently → product rule
  • Typical match probabilities ~1 in 1 billion
  • Ethical issues: partial match searches, familial DNA testing, wrongful convictions (Innocence Project)

L40 - Biotechnology 2: Applications & Genomics

Techniques to Locate/Assess Gene Expression

  • FISH (Fluorescent in situ hybridization) — locates specific DNA sequence on chromosomes
  • Reporter genes:
    • Luciferase (firefly) — glowing
    • GFP (green fluorescent protein, jellyfish) — fuses to protein, visualize expression in living cells over time
  • Microarray: compare RNA expression profiles (e.g., cancer vs normal cells)

Forward vs Reverse Genetics

  • Forward: phenotype → find gene (start with mutant, map the gene)
  • Reverse: gene → find phenotype (start with sequence, alter/disable, observe effects)
Forward = Phenotype First. Reverse = Gene First.

Reverse Genetics Tools

  • Transgenic mice: inject gene into fertilized egg, breed to homozygosity
  • Knockout mice: disrupt gene function
  • RNAi: siRNA knocks down expression (e.g., ApoB siRNA for cholesterol)
  • CRISPR-Cas9: naturally in bacteria (antiviral); modify to edit sequences — repair mutations, add missing genes, knock out bad genes

Gene Therapy

  • Transgene: introduced copy
  • Somatic (non-heritable): treats, doesn't cure. All current gene therapies.
  • Germline (heritable): major ethical issues
  • Delivery: retroviruses (integrate), direct injection, lipid capsules, nanoparticles
  • Successful trials: SCID/ADA deficiency, Leber's congenital amaurosis, Hemophilia B, leukemia
  • Costs: Kymriah $475K, Luxterna (inherited blindness)

NIH Gene Therapy Requirements

  1. Gene cloned, characterized, pure
  2. Effective delivery method
  3. Risks minimal
  4. Disease not treatable by other means
  5. Preliminary animal/human cell data shows efficacy

Genetics vs Genomics

  • Genetics: heredity and inherited variation
  • Genomics: content, organization, function, evolution of whole genomes

Three Map Types

In humans: 1 cM ≈ 1 mb.

Anchor Markers (correlate maps)

  • STS: Sequence-Tagged Sites — short unique 200-500 bp sequences
  • EST: Expressed Sequence Tags — short cDNA sequences

Contig

Overlapping set of clones forming physical map of a chromosome region.

Genome Sequencing Approaches

  • Map-based (classic): build library, fingerprint, orient, screen with markers. Relies on detailed maps.
  • Whole-Genome Shotgun (modern): small-insert plasmid clones from genomic data, computer assembles by overlap. Highly automated. Used in high-throughput today.

Human Genome Project

  • Craig Venter (private, Celera, shotgun) + Francis Collins (public, NIH)
  • 1988 funded → 1991 began → 2001 first draft (90%) → essentially complete 99% euchromatin

GWAS (Genome-Wide Association Studies)

  • Look for non-random associations between traits and markers across the genome
  • Population-based (not pedigrees)
  • Especially good for QTL identification
  • Uses SNPs as markers

SNPs

  • Single Nucleotide Polymorphism: base varies in ≥1% of population
  • ~10 million SNPs in human genome
  • Nearby SNPs often inherited together as a haplotype
  • tagSNPs: a few SNPs that identify a whole haplotype
  • ~100,000 SNPs can identify most human haplotypes
CORRELATION ≠ CAUSATION. SNP associations are starting points for research, not proof.

Personalized Medicine

  • Tailoring treatment to genotype
  • Example: Warfarin (Coumadin) — 10x inter-individual dose variation. Genes: CYP2C9 (metabolism) + VKORC1 (vitamin K epoxide reductase)
  • Other examples: Tamoxifen, some anesthetics

GINA — Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (2008)

  • Health insurance can't deny coverage/raise rates based on genetic tests
  • Employers can't discriminate based on genetic tests
  • Neither can require genetic testing
GINA does NOT cover life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance. (Not on slides but a classic follow-up Q.)

🧮 Math Walkthrough #1: HWE & Chi-Square

Problem Type: Is this population in HWE?

Given: 200 individuals genotyped: 35 BB, 120 Bb, 45 bb.

Step 1 — Observed allele frequencies

p = f(B) = [2(35) + 120] / [2(200)] = 190/400 = 0.475 q = f(b) = 1 − 0.475 = 0.525 (or equivalently: [2(45)+120]/400)

Step 2 — Expected genotype frequencies under HWE

p² = (0.475)² = 0.2256 2pq = 2(0.475)(0.525) = 0.4988 q² = (0.525)² = 0.2756

Step 3 — Expected counts

E(BB) = 0.2256 × 200 = 45.12 E(Bb) = 0.4988 × 200 = 99.76 E(bb) = 0.2756 × 200 = 55.12

Step 4 — Chi-square

χ² = (35−45.12)²/45.12 + (120−99.76)²/99.76 + (45−55.12)²/55.12 χ² = 2.270 + 4.106 + 1.858 = 8.234

Step 5 — Decide

df = 3 − 2 = 1 critical value (α=0.05) = 3.84 8.234 > 3.84 → REJECT H₀ Conclusion: NOT in HWE. At least one assumption violated.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #2: Carrier Frequency

Problem: Albinism 1 in 10,000. Carrier frequency?

1/10,000 = q² = 0.0001 q = √0.0001 = 0.01 p = 1 − 0.01 = 0.99 Carriers = 2pq = 2(0.99)(0.01) = 0.0198 (about 1 in 50)
Pro tip: For rare recessive disorders, carrier frequency is WAY higher than affected. 1 in 10,000 affected = 1 in 50 carriers. This is heavily tested.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #3: Mutation

Problem: Initial f(A) = 0.55. μ = 1×10⁻⁵, ν = 1×10⁻⁶. Next gen freq?

p₁ = (1−μ)p₀ + ν·q₀ p₁ = (1 − 0.00001)(0.55) + (0.000001)(0.45) p₁ = 0.5499945 + 0.00000045 p₁ = 0.54999495 q₁ = 1 − p₁ = 0.45000505
KEEP ALL DECIMAL PLACES. Mutation is tiny. Rounding kills the answer.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #4: Migration

Problem: Island has 1000 individuals (30 AA, 420 Aa, 550 aa). Mainland freqs: 0.60 AA + 0.30 Aa + 0.10 aa. 200 migrate from mainland.

Step 1 — Calculate m

m = immigrants / total after = 200 / (1000 + 200) = 0.1667

Step 2 — Allele freqs

P (mainland) = f(A) = 0.60 + 0.5(0.30) = 0.75 p (island) = f(A) = 0.03 + 0.5(0.42) = 0.24

Step 3 — New island allele freq

p' = (1−m)p + mP p' = (0.8333)(0.24) + (0.1667)(0.75) p' = 0.2000 + 0.1250 = 0.3250 q' = 1 − 0.3250 = 0.6750

Step 4 — New genotype freqs (same weighted avg, per genotype)

AA = 0.8333(0.03) + 0.1667(0.60) = 0.025 + 0.100 = 0.1250 Aa = 0.8333(0.42) + 0.1667(0.30) = 0.350 + 0.050 = 0.4000 aa = 0.8333(0.55) + 0.1667(0.10) = 0.458 + 0.017 = 0.4750
After migration, population is NOT in HWE (notice p² ≠ 0.1250). To restore HWE, randomly mate 1 generation with no other violations.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #5: Viability Selection

Problem: Initial 0.16 AA + 0.48 Aa + 0.36 aa (p=0.4). Fitness: WAA=1.0, WAa=0.9, Waa=0.6. New allele freqs?

Step 1 — Unadjusted survivors (freq × fitness)

AA: 0.16 × 1.0 = 0.160 Aa: 0.48 × 0.9 = 0.432 aa: 0.36 × 0.6 = 0.216

Step 2 — Average fitness W̄

W̄ = 0.160 + 0.432 + 0.216 = 0.808

Step 3 — Post-selection frequencies (divide by W̄)

AA: 0.160 / 0.808 = 0.19802 Aa: 0.432 / 0.808 = 0.53465 aa: 0.216 / 0.808 = 0.26733

Step 4 — New allele frequencies

f(A) = 0.19802 + 0.5(0.53465) = 0.46535 f(a) = 1 − 0.46535 = 0.53465

Allele A increased from 0.4 → 0.465. Directional selection favoring A.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #6: Inbreeding Coefficient

Problem: Genotypic array 0.60 AA + 0.20 Aa + 0.20 aa. Find F.

Step 1 — Allele freqs

p = f(A) = 0.60 + 0.5(0.20) = 0.70 q = 0.30

Step 2 — Apply inbreeding formula

(p² + Fpq) AA: 0.49 + F(0.21) = 0.60 F(0.21) = 0.11 F = 0.11/0.21 = 0.52381

🧮 Math Walkthrough #7: UPGMA Tree Construction

This is the phylogeny gold standard. Will absolutely be on the test.

Problem: Distances between 4 OTUs

SWBDNHW
SW----
BD100---
N15020--
HW200200150-

Step 1 — Find smallest distance

BD-N = 20 (smallest). Group them. Branch length to common ancestor = 20/2 = 10.

Step 2 — Recompute distances using arithmetic mean

SW to BD/N = (100 + 150) / 2 = 125 HW to BD/N = (200 + 150) / 2 = 175
SWBD/NHW
SW---
BD/N125--
HW200175-

Step 3 — Next smallest: SW to BD/N = 125

Group SW with (BD,N). Branch from SW to this cluster's root = 125/2 = 62.5. BD/N were joined at depth 10, so the branch from the BD/N cluster up to the new joining point = 62.5 − 10 = 52.5.

Step 4 — Compute distance from (SW,BD,N) to HW

Average of SW-HW, BD-HW, N-HW = (200 + 200 + 150) / 3 = 183.33

Step 5 — Final join at 183.33 / 2 = 91.67

Branch from HW up = 91.67. Branch from (SW,BD,N) cluster up = 91.67 − 62.5 = 29.17.

UPGMA algorithm (memorize this):
  1. Find smallest distance → join those two
  2. Branch length = distance / 2
  3. Recompute distances to the new cluster using the arithmetic mean (weighted by how many OTUs are in each cluster IF unequal sizes — with equal sizes just average them)
  4. Repeat until one big cluster
Key insight: When combining A,B → cluster, and computing dist from cluster to C: if A and B were 1 OTU each, just (d(A,C) + d(B,C))/2. If you're joining a single OTU to a cluster of 2, use (d(AB,C) + d(AB,D))/2 where AB already exists. When combining two clusters of UNEQUAL size, weight by cluster size. For this class, average over all pairwise combinations of members — that's what the slides showed with 4 pairs averaged for A,B vs C,D case.

🧮 Math Walkthrough #8: Heritability (H²)

Problem: V(P1)=3.3, V(P2)=3.7, V(F1)=5.0, V(F2)=20. Find H².

Problem: Petal size avg 5.5 cm. Selected parents avg 6.4 cm. h² = 0.74. Response? Offspring mean?

Problem (Percentage): Thyme yield X₀=60 mg, σ=2.3 mg. Select top 5%. h²=0.45.

Table values for p=0.05: I=2.063, Z=1.645

XT = μ + Zσ = 60 + (1.645)(2.3) = 63.78 mg [truncation point] S = Iσ = (2.063)(2.3) = 4.74 mg [selection differential] R = h²S = (0.45)(4.74) = 2.14 mg [response] X₁ = X₀ + R = 60 + 2.14 = 62.14 mg [next gen mean]

Same problem but SELECTING FOR SMALLER (left tail)

Lab rats, mean 87.9 sec, σ=5.8, select fastest 5% (fastest = shortest time = left tail).

Population Genetics (L34-35)

Q1 · HWE calculations
A population of 500 snails is scored: 180 RR, 240 Rr, 80 rr. Calculate: (a) allele frequencies, (b) expected genotype frequencies under HWE, (c) is this consistent with HWE? Use χ².
(a) f(R) = [2(180)+240]/1000 = 600/1000 = 0.6. f(r) = 0.4.
(b) p²=0.36, 2pq=0.48, q²=0.16.
(c) Expected: 180 RR, 240 Rr, 80 rr — these are identical to observed! χ² = 0 < 3.84. Consistent with HWE. (This was engineered — yours will have nonzero deviations on the test.)
Q2 · Carrier frequency
Cystic fibrosis affects 1 in 2,500 Caucasians. Assuming HWE, what fraction of the population is a carrier?
q² = 1/2500 = 0.0004. q = 0.02. p = 0.98. 2pq = 2(0.98)(0.02) = 0.0392 ≈ 1 in 25.
(Matches real-world CF carrier estimates — that's why it's a common example.)
Q3 · Chi-square HWE test
A population has: 40 AA, 20 Aa, 40 aa (total 100). Test for HWE.
p = [2(40)+20]/200 = 0.5. q = 0.5.
Expected: p²(100) = 25, 2pq(100) = 50, q²(100) = 25.
χ² = (40−25)²/25 + (20−50)²/50 + (40−25)²/25 = 9 + 18 + 9 = 36.
36 >> 3.84. Reject H₀. Not in HWE — excess of both homozygotes and deficit of heterozygotes. Likely cause: inbreeding or assortative mating.
Q4 · Mutation
A population has f(A) = 0.8. A mutates to a at μ = 2×10⁻⁵. Reverse mutation ν = 1×10⁻⁶. What's f(A) next generation?
p₁ = (1−μ)p₀ + νq₀
= (1 − 0.00002)(0.8) + (0.000001)(0.2)
= 0.79998400 + 0.00000020
= 0.79998420
Q5 · Migration
Island pop: 0.10 AA + 0.40 Aa + 0.50 aa (800 individuals). 200 migrate in from a mainland with gametic array 0.7A + 0.3a. Calculate (a) m, (b) p', (c) post-migration genotype frequencies.
(a) m = 200/(800+200) = 0.20
(b) Island p = 0.10 + 0.5(0.40) = 0.30. Mainland P = 0.70.
p' = (0.80)(0.30) + (0.20)(0.70) = 0.24 + 0.14 = 0.38
(c) Need mainland genotype freqs. Assume mainland is in HWE: AA=0.49, Aa=0.42, aa=0.09.
AA' = 0.80(0.10) + 0.20(0.49) = 0.080 + 0.098 = 0.178
Aa' = 0.80(0.40) + 0.20(0.42) = 0.320 + 0.084 = 0.404
aa' = 0.80(0.50) + 0.20(0.09) = 0.400 + 0.018 = 0.418
(Sum = 1.0 ✓. Note p² = 0.1444 ≠ 0.178 — NOT in HWE yet.)
Q6 · Viability selection
Initial: 0.25 AA + 0.50 Aa + 0.25 aa. Fitness: WAA=0.8, WAa=1.0, Waa=0.5. (a) What type of selection? (b) Allele freqs after 1 generation of selection?
(a) Heterozygote most fit → overdominance (stabilizing selection). Maintains both alleles.
(b) Unadjusted: 0.25(0.8)=0.20, 0.50(1.0)=0.50, 0.25(0.5)=0.125. W̄ = 0.825.
Post-selection: 0.20/0.825=0.2424, 0.50/0.825=0.6061, 0.125/0.825=0.1515.
f(A) = 0.2424 + 0.5(0.6061) = 0.5455. f(a) = 0.4545.
(Started at 0.5/0.5. Overdominance with unequal homozygote fitness shifts equilibrium toward the better homozygote.)
Q7 · Inbreeding coefficient
Population has genotypic array 0.50 AA + 0.20 Aa + 0.30 aa. Calculate F.
p = 0.50 + 0.5(0.20) = 0.60. q = 0.40.
p² + Fpq = 0.50
0.36 + F(0.24) = 0.50
F(0.24) = 0.14
F = 0.5833
Q8 · Effects on allele vs genotype freq
For each, indicate whether allele freqs change, genotype freqs change, or both:
(a) Inbreeding, (b) Random genetic drift, (c) Assortative mating, (d) Directional selection, (e) Migration.
(a) Inbreeding: only genotype freqs change (more homozygotes).
(b) Drift: both — random changes to allele freqs, so genotypes too.
(c) Assortative mating: only genotype freqs change (at the mate-selection loci).
(d) Directional selection: both.
(e) Migration: both.

Human α-globin and human β-globin. Human α-globin and mouse α-globin. Which is paralogous? Which is orthologous?

(b) Allopatric — geographic barrier (canyon) split populations.

Quantitative Genetics (L37)

Q13 · Broad-sense heritability
Plants: V(P1)=2, V(P2)=4, V(F1)=3, V(F2)=25. Calculate H².
VE = (2+4+3)/3 = 3.
VG = V(F2) − VE = 25 − 3 = 22.
H² = 22/25 = 0.88.
Q14 · Narrow-sense heritability from backcrosses
Using data in Q13, with V(B1)=14, V(B2)=12. Calculate h².
VA = 2V(F2) − [V(B1) + V(B2)] = 2(25) − (14+12) = 50 − 26 = 24.
h² = VA/Vp = 24/25 = 0.96.
(Note: this is higher than H² = 0.88, which is impossible since h² ≤ H². This flag indicates experimental error or violated assumptions — the graders LOVE to give you contradictory numbers to test understanding.)
Q15 · Realized heritability
Hens lay eggs averaging 58 g. You select hens averaging 65 g to breed the next generation. The next generation averages 61 g. What's the realized heritability?
S = 65 − 58 = 7. R = 61 − 58 = 3. h² = R/S = 3/7 = 0.43.

(a) Which trait is most heritable? (b) Which is most environmental?

Cancer Genetics (L38)

Q19 · Tumor suppressor vs oncogene
For each description, identify: tumor suppressor or oncogene, and recessive or dominant mutation.
(a) RB gene loss of function
(b) c-myc overexpression from translocation
(c) p53 deletion
(d) Constitutively active growth factor receptor
(a) RB loss: Tumor suppressor, recessive (need both copies lost)
(b) c-myc overexpression: Oncogene, dominant (1 activated copy sufficient)
(c) p53 deletion: Tumor suppressor, recessive (usually). NOTE: some p53 mutations act dominant-negative.
(d) Active receptor: Oncogene, dominant
Q20 · Two-hit hypothesis
Explain why children with inherited retinoblastoma develop tumors earlier and more often (often bilateral) than children with sporadic retinoblastoma.
Inherited retinoblastoma patients have 1 mutant RB allele in every cell (from germline). They only need 1 more somatic hit in ANY retinal cell to lose tumor suppression → tumors happen early and often in both eyes.
Sporadic patients need 2 independent somatic hits IN THE SAME CELL — much rarer. Later onset, usually unilateral.
Q21 · Philadelphia chromosome
What chromosomes are involved in the Philadelphia chromosome translocation? What disease? What's the mechanism?
Chromosomes 9 and 22 — reciprocal translocation t(9;22). Disease: Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (CML). ~90% of CML patients have it. Mechanism: fuses BCR and ABL genes → constitutively active tyrosine kinase → uncontrolled proliferation. (Fun fact outside the slides: targeted by Gleevec/imatinib, one of the first successful targeted cancer therapies.)
Q22 · Haploinsufficiency
How does haploinsufficiency differ from the classic two-hit model for tumor suppressors? Give an example.
Classic: BOTH copies must be inactivated before cancer risk rises.
Haploinsufficiency: losing just ONE copy leaves the cell with insufficient gene product, which itself elevates cancer risk — even though a "functional" allele remains.
Example: Bloom syndrome (BLM gene, a DNA helicase for DSB repair). Heterozygotes have elevated colorectal cancer risk; mouse data shows 2x higher intestinal tumors in BLM heterozygotes.

Q25 · Golden Rice steps

Q33 · STS clone ordering
You have 3 overlapping clones and 4 STSs. Use STS presence/absence to order the clones:
         STS1  STS3  STS5  STS8
Clone X:  +     +     -     -
Clone Y:  -     -     +     +
Clone Z:  -     +     -     +
Order the clones linearly.
Each clone is contiguous — its STSs must appear in order on the physical map.
Clone X has STS1, STS3 (adjacent).
Clone Z has STS3, STS8 (adjacent).
Clone Y has STS5, STS8 (adjacent).

Q34 · Correlation vs causation
A GWAS finds SNP-X is strongly associated with Type 2 diabetes. Can you conclude SNP-X causes T2D? Why or why not?
NO. Correlation ≠ causation. SNP-X might:
• Be in linkage disequilibrium with the actual causal variant (most common case)
• Be a marker for population structure (confounding)
• Have a small effect that doesn't alone cause disease
GWAS associations are starting points for further research (functional studies, fine-mapping, molecular validation).
Q35 · Gene therapy concepts
(a) Why are current gene therapies all somatic, not germline? (b) Why are retroviruses useful as gene therapy vectors but also risky?
(a) Germline modifications are heritable — they affect all future progeny. Major ethical concerns about consent (future generations can't consent) and unforeseen consequences. Somatic is limited to the patient.
(b) Retroviruses integrate into host DNA, so the transgene gets transmitted to all daughter cells (stable expression). Risk: integration is semi-random — can disrupt other genes (insertional mutagenesis). This has caused leukemia in some early gene therapy trials.
Q36 · Mixed challenge — Drift vs Selection
A population of 1,000 organisms has f(A) = 0.30. After many generations, f(A) = 0.90. How can you tell whether this change was due to selection or drift?
Hard from allele freqs alone. Clues:
Drift is more likely if: small population size (1,000 is modest), multiple populations show random directions, no consistent selective pressure identifiable, phenotype doesn't obviously confer advantage.
Selection is more likely if: parallel changes across populations in same direction, identifiable fitness advantage for A, repeatable under experimental conditions, consistent relationship with environment.
Experimentally: compare replicate populations. Drift → random outcomes. Selection → parallel outcomes.
