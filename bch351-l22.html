Nucleotide Structure

Every nucleotide — the monomer unit of DNA and RNA — has three components:

1. Pentose sugar — a 5-carbon sugar (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA)
2. Nitrogenous base — attached at the 1' carbon
3. Phosphate group — attached at the 5' carbon

Numbering convention: Sugar carbons get primes (1', 2', 3', 5'). Base atoms use regular numbers (1, 2, 3…).

Nucleoside vs. Nucleotide

A nucleoside is just the base + sugar (no phosphate). A nucleotide is all three components together. For example: adenine + ribose = adenosine (nucleoside). Add a phosphate at 5' and you get AMP — adenosine 5'-monophosphate (nucleotide).

RNA vs. DNA: One Hydroxyl Changes Everything

The only structural difference is at the 2' carbon of the sugar. RNA has a hydroxyl group (—OH); DNA has just hydrogen (—H). This tiny change has major consequences:

🧬 DNA

No 2'—OH means DNA readily forms the classic B-form double helix — a very stable, low-energy structure.

Cannot act as a catalyst because it lacks the nucleophilic —OH.

🔬 RNA

The 2'—OH sterically interferes with B-form helix formation, making RNA structurally different.

The —OH can be deprotonated and act as a nucleophile, enabling some RNAs to function as enzymes (ribozymes).

The Four Bases

Both DNA and RNA use four bases, split into two structural families based on ring structure:

Purines (2 rings)

Adenine (A): amino group at C6

Guanine (G): amino group at C2, carbonyl at C6

Connect to sugar via N9 → C1'

Pyrimidines (1 ring)

Cytosine (C): amino group at C4. Can be methylated at C5 (epigenetics).

Thymine (T): DNA only. Methyl group at C5.

Uracil (U): RNA only. Like thymine but no methyl group.

Connect to sugar via N1 → C1'

All bases are aromatic and hydrophobic, which is why they stack inward in the double helix while the hydrophilic sugar-phosphate backbone faces the water.

Abbreviations in sequences: DNA uses A, G, C, T. RNA uses A, G, C, U.

pKa & Charge

Each nucleotide has two relevant pKa values (see Table 4.1, p. 77):

pK₁ comes from the phosphate group (acidic, low pKa). pK₂ comes from the base (basic, higher pKa). The overall pKa is roughly the average of the two. At physiological pH, AMP, GMP, and CMP are overall acidic, while TMP is basic.

Under physiological conditions, certain nitrogen atoms on the bases can gain or lose protons, which affects base-pairing behavior and catalytic potential.

Base Pairing

Watson-Crick base pairing follows a strict pattern, with different hydrogen bond counts:

PairH-BondsStrengthConsequence
G ≡ C3StrongerHigher melting temperature (Tₘ)
A = T / A = U2WeakerDenatures at lower temperature
Practical takeaway: GC-rich DNA sequences are harder to denature (higher Tₘ). AT-rich regions melt first. This matters for PCR primer design, hybridization experiments, and understanding genome stability.

Why Triphosphates for Biosynthesis?

Polymerases use nucleoside triphosphates (NTPs/dNTPs) as substrates, not monophosphates. The reason is pure thermodynamics — polymerization is energetically unfavorable on its own.

The cell solves this by coupling unfavorable reactions with favorable ones:

Step 1: Cleave the high-energy anhydride bond between the α and β phosphates → releases energy to drive nucleotide addition to the growing chain.

Step 2: The released pyrophosphate (PPi) is hydrolyzed into 2 Pi → another highly favorable reaction that pulls equilibrium further toward polymerization.

This two-step energy release makes DNA/RNA synthesis essentially irreversible under cellular conditions.

ATP as Energy Currency

ATP is the most common nucleoside triphosphate used for energy coupling throughout the cell. It contains two high-energy anhydride bonds (γ–β and β–α). Hydrolysis of either releases substantial free energy to drive otherwise unfavorable reactions. GTP serves a similar role in certain processes but is less prevalent than ATP.

Bottom line: The presence or absence of a single hydroxyl group at the 2' carbon is the fundamental distinction between RNA and DNA, determining their drastically different structural stability and catalytic potential. Nucleotide polymerization is driven thermodynamically by phosphoanhydride bond hydrolysis.
