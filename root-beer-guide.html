The FDA Killed Real Root Beer in 1960

Root beer was America's first soft drink. Before Coca-Cola, before Dr. Pepper, colonists were brewing small beers from local roots, primarily sassafras. The distinctive flavor comes from safrole, an aromatic compound in sassafras root bark.

In 1960, the FDA banned safrole after rat studies showed liver cancer at absurdly high doses. The human equivalent would be drinking 32+ bottles of root beer daily for years. Critics point out that safrole also exists in black pepper, nutmeg, and basil, none of which are banned.

🔬 The Safrole Situation

What's banned: Commercial sale of safrole-containing sassafras for food/beverage use. Also, safrole is a DEA List I controlled precursor because it can be used to synthesize MDMA.

What's NOT banned: Foraging sassafras for personal use. Selling sassafras bark labeled "not for human consumption." Making filé powder from leaves (no safrole). Safrole-free sassafras extract.

Reality: People in Appalachia, Louisiana, and the rural South have been making sassafras tea for generations. Nobody's getting arrested. The actual health risk from occasional tea or homebrew is negligible.

Foraging Sassafras in North Carolina

📍 Willow Spring, NC

Good news: Sassafras (Sassafras albidum) is extremely abundant throughout Wake County and the NC Piedmont. You almost certainly have it on your 0.57 acres or within walking distance.

Where to Look

Sassafras thrives in disturbed areas and forest edges. In your area, check:

  • Forest edges and fence lines - Sassafras loves the boundary between woods and clearings
  • Old fields reverting to forest - Pioneer species that colonizes abandoned land
  • Roadsides and power line cuts - Disturbed soil is sassafras heaven
  • Along your property boundaries - Check where your land meets neighbors' woods
  • Near larger hardwoods - Often found under oaks, maples, and dogwoods
🌳 Colony Behavior

Sassafras spreads via root suckers, creating genetically identical colonies. If you find one tree, you'll usually find a cluster of them ranging from saplings to mature trees. The "mother tree" sends out horizontal roots that sprout new trees, so what looks like a thicket of individual trees is actually one organism.

Pro tip: Harvest from the small suckers (1-3 ft tall) rather than the mother tree. The colony will replace what you take, and the small roots are easier to dig.

State Parks & Game Lands

If you don't find sassafras on your property (unlikely), nearby public lands where it's abundant:

  • Harris Lake County Park - 15 min drive, sassafras along trails
  • Clemmons Educational State Forest - 20 min, excellent for foraging education
  • Raven Rock State Park - 30 min, abundant in Piedmont forests
⚠️ Public Land Rules

Technically, removing plants from state parks requires a permit. In practice, nobody's checking if you grab a few sassafras roots for tea. Use common sense: don't strip an area, don't take rare plants, don't dig up mature trees. Game lands are generally more permissive.

Winter Foraging: Actually the Best Time

❄️ Plot Twist

Winter is the OPTIMAL time to harvest sassafras roots. The sap is concentrated in the roots rather than running up into the branches. You'll get more flavor per root than any other season.

The Conditions

  • Ground must be thawed enough to dig - If the soil is frozen solid, you won't get anywhere
  • Best after a warm spell - Wait for temps above 40°F for a day or two
  • Moist soil is easier - After rain or snow melt, the dirt comes up easier
  • Mark trees in fall - Without leaves, ID is harder. Tag trees you've identified while leaves are still on

January in Wake County

You mentioned it's snowy. Here's the reality:

  • NC Piedmont soil rarely freezes deeper than a few inches
  • Snow actually insulates the ground, keeping it warmer
  • Wait for snow to melt and a day or two above freezing
  • The top inch might be crusty, but dig past it and you're good
🛠️ Winter Harvesting Kit
  • Shovel or trowel (for frozen top layer)
  • Pruning shears (for cutting roots)
  • Bucket with water (for pre-rinsing)
  • Gloves (it's cold and dirty)
  • Knee pad (wet ground)
  • Ribbon or paint (for marking trees you've ID'd)

How to Identify Sassafras

The Famous Leaves (Spring-Fall)

Sassafras has three distinct leaf shapes on the same tree, making it one of the easiest trees to ID when leaves are present:

🧤 The Mitten

Two-lobed leaf that looks like a mitten with a thumb. Most distinctive and memorable. "Mittens on the mitten tree."

🦖 The Trident / Dino Paw

Three-lobed leaf. Kids call it the "dinosaur footprint." Less common than mittens but unmistakable.

🥚 The Oval

Simple unlobed oval leaf. Looks like many other trees, but finding it alongside mittens and tridents confirms sassafras.

Winter ID (No Leaves)

This is harder but totally doable with practice:

Bark

Mature trees: Dark reddish-brown, deeply furrowed with flat-topped ridges. Cinnamon-brown inner bark visible in cracks.

Young trees: Smooth, greenish-brown bark with visible lenticels (small dots).

Twigs

Slender, bright yellowish-green, curving upward at tips. Small green buds (1/4 inch). Young twigs display at uniform 60° angle from main stem, creating a distinctive J-shape where branches fork.

👃 The Sniff Test (Works Year-Round)

This is your secret weapon. Break a twig or scratch the bark. Sassafras has an unmistakable fruity/spicy/root beer aroma that no other tree has. Even in deep winter, that smell comes through instantly.

If you're unsure about bark ID, the sniff test is 100% confirmation. Oak and boxelder (common look-alikes) smell like nothing or just earthy wood.

Colony Pattern

Sassafras colonies are another identification clue. Look for clusters of trees of varying sizes in a roughly circular pattern, all genetically identical clones connected underground. If you see what appears to be a "thicket" of similar-looking trees with the bark characteristics above, scratch one and sniff.

Common Look-Alikes

Tree How to Tell the Difference
Boxelder Compound leaves (multiple leaflets), no aroma when scratched
Young Oak Lobed leaves are symmetrical (sassafras mittens are asymmetric), no aroma
Tulip Poplar Leaves are square-shaped with notched tips, much larger tree, different bark pattern
Spicebush Similar citrusy aroma but it's a shrub, not a tree. Leaves are simple ovals only.

DIY Root Beer Recipe

🍺 Hank Shaw's Root Beer Syrup

This is a syrup you mix with seltzer. Easy, no fermentation required, keeps for a year refrigerated.

Ingredients

  • 3 oz sassafras root bark (fresh foraged or dried)
  • 1 oz burdock root (optional, adds earthiness)
  • 1 whole clove
  • 1 star anise
  • 1 tsp coriander seeds
  • 2 tbsp molasses
  • 1/4 tsp wintergreen extract
  • 6 cups water
  • 2 cups sugar

Method

  1. Chop sassafras root bark into small pieces (1/2 inch). If using burdock, chop it too.
  2. Combine water, sassafras, burdock, clove, star anise, and coriander in a pot.
  3. Bring to boil, then simmer covered for 15 minutes.
  4. Stir in molasses. Simmer 5 more minutes.
  5. Remove from heat. Add wintergreen extract. Cover and cool 2 hours.
  6. Strain through cheesecloth.
  7. Return to pot, add sugar, simmer 5 minutes until dissolved.
  8. Cool and bottle. Keeps 1 year refrigerated.

To Serve

Mix 1 tbsp syrup per 1 pint seltzer. Adjust to taste. Add ice, enjoy.

🧪 Fermented Root Beer (Naturally Carbonated)

Traditional method using a ginger bug starter. More complex, naturally fizzy, slight probiotic benefit.

You'll Need First: Ginger Bug

Feed 2 tbsp grated ginger + 2 tbsp sugar + 2 cups water daily for 5-7 days until bubbly. This is your wild yeast starter.

Root Beer Ingredients

  • 2 oz sassafras root bark
  • 1 oz sarsaparilla root
  • 1 oz birch bark
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 2 tbsp molasses
  • 8 cups water
  • 1/2 cup active ginger bug

Method

  1. Combine water and roots/barks in pot. Boil, then simmer 20 minutes.
  2. Remove from heat. Cool to room temperature (important for yeast).
  3. Strain into gallon jar. Stir in sugar, molasses, ginger bug.
  4. Cover with cheesecloth. Stir daily for 2-3 days.
  5. Bottle in flip-top bottles with 1-2 inches headspace.
  6. Ferment at room temp 2-3 days. Burp bottles daily to prevent explosion.
  7. When carbonated, refrigerate. Drink within 2 weeks.
⚠️ Fermentation Warning: Bottles can explode if over-carbonated. Use proper flip-top bottles designed for pressure. Burp daily. When in doubt, refrigerate early.
