The FDA Ban: What Actually Happened

In 1960, the FDA banned safrole, the primary aromatic compound in sassafras oil, from use in commercially mass-produced foods and drugs. This came after studies showed that rats fed extremely high doses of safrole developed liver tumors.

Context Matters

Those rat studies used doses equivalent to a human drinking 32+ bottles of root beer daily for years. The same compound exists in black pepper, nutmeg, and basil, none of which have been banned.

The Legal Reality

Here's what the law actually says:

  • Commercial sale of safrole-containing food products is banned
  • Personal foraging is not banned
  • Home brewing for personal use exists in a gray area
  • Filé powder (from sassafras leaves) is completely legal since leaves contain no safrole

Safrole is also a DEA List I precursor chemical because it can be used to synthesize MDMA. This is why you'll see "Not for human consumption" labels on commercially sold sassafras bark, but it's a legal liability hedge, not an enforcement reality for home users.

Sassafras tea was banned briefly in 1977, then re-legalized with the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act of 1994.

Where to Forage in Willow Spring, NC

Sassafras is extremely common in Wake County's Piedmont forests. You likely have it growing on your 0.57 acres or within walking distance.

Where to Look

  • Forest edges and fence lines
  • Old fields reverting to forest
  • Roadsides and power line cuts
  • Property boundaries
  • Under oaks, maples, and dogwoods (sassafras is an understory tree)
Colony Behavior

Sassafras spreads via root suckers, creating genetically identical colonies. If you find one tree, look around for more. Harvest small saplings (1-3 ft) from colonies of 8-10+ trees, leaving the mother tree intact.

Nearby Public Lands

  • Harris Lake County Park 15 min from Willow Spring. Mixed hardwood forest with abundant sassafras.
  • Clemmons Educational State Forest 20 min. Excellent for tree ID practice with labeled specimens.
  • Raven Rock State Park 30 min. Rich forest with diverse understory including sassafras.
Permits & Ethics

State parks technically require permits for plant removal. Enforcement is minimal for small personal quantities, but be discrete. Game lands are more permissive. Never take from the same spot repeatedly.

Winter Foraging: The Best Time

Why Winter is Optimal

In winter, sap is concentrated in the roots, not running up into branches. This means stronger flavor per root than any other season. Traditional root beer makers always harvested in late fall through early spring.

Conditions for Winter Digging

  • Ground must be thawed: Wait for temps above 40°F for 1-2 days
  • Moist soil is easier: Dig after rain or snow melt
  • Snow can help: Insulates ground, prevents deep freeze
  • NC Piedmont advantage: Soil rarely freezes deeper than a few inches

The top inch might be crusty, but it's usually workable underneath. January in Willow Spring typically has plenty of diggable days between cold snaps.

Harvesting Kit

  • Small shovel or trowel
  • Pruning shears
  • Bucket with water (keeps roots fresh)
  • Gloves and knee pad
  • Ribbon or paint for marking productive spots

How to Identify Sassafras

Summer Identification (Leaves Present)

Sassafras has the most distinctive leaves of any North American tree. Three different leaf shapes grow on the same branch:

Unlobed (oval)
Bilobed (mitten)
Trilobed (trident/dino paw)

All Three on One Branch

No other tree in North America has this combination. If you see mitten-shaped and trident-shaped leaves on the same twig, it's sassafras. Period.

Winter Identification (No Leaves)

Bark

Mature trees: Dark reddish-brown, deeply furrowed with flat-topped ridges. Cinnamon-brown inner bark visible in cracks.

Young trees: Smooth, greenish-brown with raised dots (lenticels).

Twigs

Slender, bright yellowish-green (distinctive color). Curve upward at tips. Small green buds (~1/4 inch). Branch at consistent 60° angles, creating a J-shape pattern.

The Sniff Test: 100% Confirmation

Break a twig or scratch the bark. Unmistakable fruity/spicy/root beer aroma. No other tree smells like this. Works year-round and is the most reliable winter ID method.

Common Look-alikes

Tree How to Distinguish
Boxelder Compound leaves (multiple leaflets), no aroma
Young Oak Symmetrical lobes, no aroma, different bark
Tulip Poplar Square-tipped leaves, much larger tree
Spicebush Shrub only, citrus aroma but simple leaves only

DIY Root Beer Recipes

Recipe 1: Simple Sassafras Syrup (Non-Fermented)

Makes concentrate for ~20 drinks. No fermentation, no alcohol, ready same day.

Ingredients

  • 3 oz sassafras root bark (fresh or dried)
  • 1 oz burdock root (optional, adds depth)
  • 6 cups water
  • 2 cups sugar
  • 2 tbsp molasses
  • 1 whole clove
  • 1 star anise
  • 1 tsp coriander seeds
  • 1/4 tsp wintergreen extract (add at end)

Instructions

  1. Chop roots into small pieces. Bring water to boil, add roots and spices.
  2. Reduce to simmer, cover, steep 15-20 minutes. (For stronger flavor, remove from heat and steep overnight.)
  3. Strain through cheesecloth, pressing roots to extract liquid.
  4. Return to pot, add sugar and molasses. Simmer 5 min until dissolved.
  5. Cool completely, then add wintergreen extract. Store refrigerated up to 1 year.
  6. To serve: 1-2 tbsp syrup per 8 oz cold seltzer water.

Recipe 2: Fermented Root Beer (Naturally Carbonated)

Traditional method using a ginger bug starter. Produces natural carbonation and trace alcohol (~0.5%).

Ginger Bug Starter (Make 5-7 Days Ahead)

  • 2 tbsp fresh ginger, minced (with skin)
  • 2 tbsp sugar
  • 2 cups filtered water

Combine in jar, cover with cheesecloth. Feed daily with 1 tbsp ginger + 1 tbsp sugar. Ready when actively bubbly (5-7 days).

Root Beer

  • 2 oz sassafras root bark
  • 1 oz sarsaparilla root
  • 1 oz birch bark (or 1/2 tsp wintergreen extract)
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 2 tbsp molasses
  • 8 cups water
  • 1/2 cup active ginger bug

Instructions

  1. Boil roots in water 20 min. Remove from heat, steep 30 min.
  2. Strain. Cool to room temperature (critical, heat kills ginger bug).
  3. Add sugar, molasses, and ginger bug. Stir well.
  4. Cover with cheesecloth, let ferment at room temp 2-3 days, stirring daily.
  5. Bottle in flip-top bottles, leaving 1-2 inches headspace.
  6. Ferment at room temp 2-3 days. Burp daily to release pressure.
  7. Refrigerate when carbonated. Consume within 2 weeks.
Bottle Safety Warning

Fermented root beer can build serious pressure. Use flip-top bottles rated for carbonation. Burp daily. Over-carbonation can cause bottles to explode. When in doubt, refrigerate early.
