Chapter 00

What Is an "Action"?

The action is the heart of any firearm. It's the mechanism that performs the firing cycle: loading a cartridge into the chamber, locking the breech so it's safe to fire, striking the primer, then extracting and ejecting the spent case. The action type is the single most fundamental classification of any firearm.

Key Concept: The action type is separate from the feed system. A bolt-action rifle can be fed by an internal magazine, a detachable box magazine, or hand-loaded one round at a time. Don't confuse how the gun cycles with how it's fed ammunition.
Chapter 01

Manual Actions

You provide the mechanical force to cycle the action. Every round requires a deliberate physical manipulation. These are the oldest, simplest, and often the most reliable and accurate action types.

Bolt Action

aka Turn-Bolt

You lift the bolt handle (unlocking the lugs), pull it rearward (extracting and ejecting the spent case), push it forward (stripping a fresh round from the magazine and chambering it), and push the handle down (rotating the lugs into lockup). Four motions, total control.

Bolt actions are the gold standard for accuracy. The receiver is rigid and doesn't need to accommodate a reciprocating mechanism. The lockup is the same every time. There's nothing moving during the shot except the trigger, sear, and firing pin. This is why virtually every precision rifle, competition long-range gun, and serious hunting rifle is bolt-action.

Why so accurate? Consistency. The barrel is rigidly bedded to the receiver, the chamber dimensions don't change shot to shot, and there are zero moving parts at the moment of ignition beyond the firing pin. The harmonics of the barrel are predictable. Repeatable harmonics = repeatable groups.
Mauser 98 Remington 700 Ruger American Tikka T3x Savage 110 CZ 457

Lever Action

aka Lever-Throw

A lever (usually the trigger guard itself) swings downward and back. This drives the bolt rearward, extracting and ejecting the spent case, cocking the hammer, and indexing the cartridge lifter. Returning the lever chambers a fresh round and locks the bolt. Fast, intuitive, one-handed cycleable if you're feeling cowboy.

Historically fed by a tubular magazine under the barrel, which created a safety concern: pointed (spitzer) bullets stacked nose-to-primer in a tube mag could cause a chain detonation under recoil. This is why traditional lever-action cartridges use flat-nose or round-nose bullets. Hornady solved this with their LEVERevolution flex-tip polymer-tipped rounds, so now you can safely run modern ballistics through a tube mag.

Some lever actions use rotary or box magazines instead, sidestepping the tube mag issue entirely. The Browning BLR and Winchester 1895 are box-magazine lever guns.

Winchester 1873 Winchester 1894 Marlin 336 Henry Big Boy Browning BLR

Pump Action

aka Slide Action

Grip the fore-end (the handguard around the barrel/magazine tube) and rack it rearward then forward. The rearward stroke unlocks the bolt, extracts and ejects the spent shell, and cocks the hammer. The forward stroke strips a new round from the magazine and chambers it. That satisfying "chk-chk" sound is the pump action cycling.

Pump actions are mechanically the most reliable semi-manual action because you are the engine. Short-stroke a semi-auto and it jams. Short-stroke a pump and you just... rack it harder. They'll cycle anything: light birdshot, heavy slugs, less-lethal beanbags, mini-shells. Nothing to adjust, nothing to tune.

This is the dominant action for defensive and sporting shotguns and exists in some rimfire and centerfire rifles as well.

Remington 870 Mossberg 500 / 590 Benelli Nova Winchester SXP

Break Action

aka Hinge Action

The barrel(s) pivot on a hinge pin, swinging open to expose the chamber(s) directly. You drop rounds in by hand, snap it closed, and fire. Opening it again either lets you manually pull spent cases out, or the gun has ejectors that automatically kick them clear.

No magazine, no feeding mechanism, nothing to jam. The action is the simplest possible: open, load, close, fire. Break actions come as single-barrel, side-by-side (two barrels horizontal), or over/under (two barrels vertical). The lack of a magazine and the rigid barrel-to-receiver lockup makes them extremely strong for their weight.

This is the classic action for sporting clays, trap, skeet, bird hunting, and derringers. Also common in single-shot rifles, combination guns (one rifle barrel + one shotgun barrel), and signal/flare guns.

Beretta 686 Browning Citori Stevens 301 Stoeger Coach Gun Thompson/Center Contender

Falling Block

A solid breechblock drops vertically (or at an angle) into the receiver when an underlever is actuated, exposing the chamber for loading. Releasing the lever raises the block, sealing the breech. Single-shot only. The block is supported on all sides by the receiver walls, making this one of the strongest action designs in existence.

The extreme strength-to-weight ratio means falling blocks can handle pressures that would challenge other designs. This made them the choice for African dangerous-game rifles and long-range buffalo rifles in the 19th century, and they remain popular among single-shot enthusiasts today.

Ruger No. 1 Sharps 1874 Martini-Henry Farquharson

Rolling Block & Trapdoor

historical / niche

Rolling Block: The breechblock rolls backward on a central pin, pivoting out of the way to expose the chamber. A separate hammer cams behind the block when cocked, locking it shut during firing. Remington's Rolling Block rifle was one of the most widely produced firearms of the 1860s-1880s, used by dozens of militaries worldwide. Single-shot, extremely strong, simple to manufacture.

Trapdoor: A hinged breechblock flips up and forward on a hinge at the front of the receiver, like a literal trapdoor. The Springfield Model 1873 "Trapdoor Springfield" is the defining example. It was adopted because existing muzzle-loading Springfield rifle-muskets could be converted to breech-loading by replacing the breech hardware, making it the cheapest path to a modern army rifle. Single-shot, historical.

Remington Rolling Block Springfield 1873 Trapdoor
Chapter 02

Semi-Automatic Actions

The firearm harvests energy from the fired cartridge to cycle the action automatically. One trigger pull, one shot, but you don't have to manually work anything between shots. The four fundamental approaches differ in where they tap that energy and how they delay the breech opening until pressure drops to safe levels.

Gas-Operated

most common semi-auto rifle action

As the bullet travels down the barrel, it passes a small gas port drilled through the barrel wall. High-pressure gas is tapped through this port and redirected to push the bolt carrier rearward, cycling the action. The three major sub-types differ in how that gas reaches the bolt.

Direct Impingement (DI)

Gas travels through a hollow tube mounted above the barrel, directly into a cavity in the bolt carrier key. The expanding gas pushes the carrier rearward. No piston, no extra moving parts. The AR-15/M16/M4 family is the textbook DI system (technically Stoner's design is sometimes called "internal piston" or "expanding gas" rather than true DI, but the industry calls it DI).

Pros: Lighter, less reciprocating mass (better accuracy potential), simpler parts count. Cons: Gas dumps carbon fouling directly into the receiver and bolt group, so it runs dirtier and needs more frequent cleaning.

Short-Stroke Gas Piston

Gas hits a piston housed above the barrel, giving it a short, sharp kick. The piston impulse is transferred to the bolt carrier via a separate operating rod or tappet, then the piston returns to its resting position via its own spring. The piston does not travel with the bolt.

Pros: Much cleaner receiver, gas and fouling stay up at the gas block. More reliable in adverse conditions. Cons: More parts, slightly heavier, "carrier tilt" can be an issue in AR-platform conversions due to the offset piston force.

Long-Stroke Gas Piston

The piston is physically attached to (or part of) the bolt carrier and travels the full stroke with it. The entire reciprocating mass moves as one unit. The AK-47 is the most famous long-stroke piston design. Also the M1 Garand, FN FAL (which is technically a tilting-bolt short-stroke design but often grouped here), and RPK.

Pros: Extreme reliability, the heavy reciprocating mass plows through fouling and adverse conditions. The AK's legendary reliability comes largely from this. Cons: Heavier reciprocating mass means more felt recoil and a slightly slower lock time. The mass slamming back and forth degrades precision compared to DI systems.

DI (AR-15)

  • Lighter bolt carrier group
  • Better inherent accuracy
  • Simpler gas system
  • Dirtier receiver
  • More cleaning needed
VS

Piston (AK / HK416)

  • Cleaner receiver
  • More reliable dirty/wet
  • Heavier, more parts
  • Slightly less precise

Recoil-Operated

dominant pistol action

The barrel and bolt/slide are locked together at the moment of firing. Recoil energy drives both rearward together. At some point during that rearward travel, the barrel separates from the slide (via a cam, link, or locking block), and the slide continues alone to extract, eject, and then return forward under spring pressure to strip and chamber the next round.

Short Recoil

Barrel and slide travel together only a short distance (a few millimeters) before unlocking. This is how virtually every modern semi-auto pistol works. The Browning tilting-barrel design (barrel cams downward via a link or shaped cam surface to unlock from the slide) is the most common implementation. Glock, SIG P320, 1911, CZ-75, S&W M&P, Beretta 92, HK VP9, all short-recoil tilting-barrel guns.

Also used in many medium and heavy machine guns (M2 Browning .50 cal, MG42/MG3).

Long Recoil

Barrel and bolt recoil together for the entire stroke length. At full recoil, the bolt is held back while the barrel returns forward under its own spring. Once the barrel is home, the bolt is released and follows forward, stripping a new round. Uncommon today. The Browning Auto-5 shotgun is the most famous long-recoil design. The system works but is heavier and more complex than short recoil.

Why pistols use recoil, not gas: Pistol barrels are too short for an effective gas system, and the cartridge energy is relatively low. Short recoil with a tilting barrel is compact, simple, and perfectly scaled for handgun cartridges. There's no gas tube, no piston, just a barrel and a slide.
Glock 17/19 (Short) 1911 (Short) SIG P320 (Short) CZ-75 (Short) Browning Auto-5 (Long) M2 .50 Cal (Short)

Blowback

no mechanical lock at all

Here's the radical simplification: there is no locking mechanism. The bolt is just sitting against the chamber, held there by a recoil spring and its own mass. When the round fires, the case pushes rearward against the bolt face. If the bolt is heavy enough relative to the cartridge pressure, the case extracts safely after pressure drops. If it's not heavy enough, the case extracts while pressure is still too high and bad things happen.

Simple (Direct) Blowback

Works for low-pressure cartridges where the bolt's inertia and spring pressure are enough to keep things timed safely. This is how most .22 LR firearms work (Ruger 10/22, most .22 pistols), as well as .380 ACP and 9x18 Makarov pistols, and virtually all pistol-caliber carbines and submachine guns (MAC-10, UZI, Sten).

The appeal is simplicity: no locking mechanism, no cam surfaces, no rotating bolt. Fewer parts, cheaper to manufacture, easy to maintain.

Delayed Blowback

For more powerful cartridges where simple blowback would require an absurdly heavy bolt, you add a mechanism that delays the bolt's opening without fully locking it. This splits into several clever engineering solutions:

Roller-Delayed

Two rollers in the bolt head are cammed outward into recesses in the barrel extension (trunnion). To open, the bolt carrier must force the rollers inward against mechanical disadvantage. HK's signature system, used in the G3 battle rifle, MP5 submachine gun, and CETME family. Extremely smooth action, very accurate, runs dirty. Downside: violent extraction (no gas to slow things down) means it beats up brass badly, which annoys handloaders.

Lever-Delayed

A lever between the bolt head and bolt carrier creates a mechanical disadvantage that the carrier must overcome to move rearward. The FAMAS (French bullpup service rifle) and the AA-12 automatic shotgun use lever-delay systems.

Gas-Delayed

A small amount of gas bleeds from the chamber area and acts against the bolt face to slow its opening. The HK P7 pistol (famous squeeze-cocker) uses gas delay. The Steyr GB also used this system. Rare but clever.

Radial-Delayed

The bolt rotates against angled/helical surfaces that create resistance through mechanical disadvantage during opening. The CMMG Banshee MkGs (Radial Delayed Blowback) is the modern champion here, allowing 9mm AR-platform guns to run with standard-weight bolts and no gas system. It's mechanically elegant: the bolt's rotation against angled cam surfaces creates progressive resistance to opening.

Ruger 10/22 (Simple) UZI (Simple) HK MP5 (Roller) HK G3 (Roller) FAMAS (Lever) HK P7 (Gas) CMMG Banshee (Radial)

Inertia-Driven

Benelli's system

This one is beautifully counterintuitive. The bolt body has a spring between it and the bolt head (the "inertia spring"). When the gun fires, the entire gun recoils rearward, but the bolt body stays still for a moment due to inertia. This compresses the inertia spring between the moving gun and the stationary bolt. When the spring rebounds, it drives the bolt rearward to cycle the action.

The system is extremely clean because there's no gas tapped from the barrel, no piston, nothing getting fouled. It's also very light because there's minimal extra hardware. The trade-off: it needs firm resistance (your shoulder) to work. If you don't give the gun something to recoil against ("limp-wristing" for shotguns, or shooting from a rest without firm hold), the system can short-stroke. Very light loads can also cause issues because there isn't enough recoil energy.

Benelli M2 Benelli SBE3 Benelli Montefeltro Franchi Affinity Stoeger M3000
Chapter 03

Revolver Actions

Revolvers are their own category. Instead of a magazine feeding into a single chamber, the revolver has a rotating cylinder with multiple chambers. The cylinder indexes (rotates) to align each chamber with the barrel and firing pin in sequence. The action type describes what the trigger does.

Single Action (SA)

The trigger does exactly one thing: release the hammer. You must manually cock the hammer with your thumb before each shot. Cocking the hammer rotates the cylinder to align the next chamber. The trigger pull is short and light because it's only tripping a sear.

This is the classic cowboy revolver. The Colt Single Action Army ("Peacemaker"), Ruger Blackhawk, and Ruger Vaquero are all SA revolvers. The manual cocking step is slower but gives you a crisp, precise trigger, which is why SA revolvers are popular for single-action shooting sports (SASS/Cowboy Action).

Colt SAA Ruger Blackhawk Ruger Vaquero Uberti Cattleman

Double Action (DA)

The trigger does two things: cocks the hammer AND releases it, all in one long pull. You can also manually cock the hammer for a lighter, shorter single-action pull. So a DA revolver gives you both options: thumb-cock for precision shots, or pull through the long DA trigger for rapid fire.

The DA trigger pull is long and heavy (typically 10-12 lbs) because you're compressing the mainspring and rotating the cylinder with your trigger finger. The SA pull on the same gun is typically 3-5 lbs. Learning to shoot DA well is a genuine skill, and many experienced shooters consider mastering the DA trigger pull to be one of the best things you can do for your overall trigger discipline.

S&W 686 Ruger GP100 Colt Python S&W Model 29

Double Action Only (DAO)

Every shot is a long DA pull. There is no option to cock the hammer manually, either because the hammer is bobbed (spur removed), shrouded (covered by a built-in shield), or fully internal (hammerless design). Every pull is the same weight and length, making the trigger consistent but heavy.

The main advantage is snag-free carry. With no hammer spur to catch on clothing, DAO revolvers are ideal for concealed carry, especially in pockets. The S&W 642/442 "Airweight" J-frames are the quintessential DAO carry revolvers.

S&W 642 S&W 442 Ruger LCR
Chapter 04

Full Auto & Select-Fire

The mechanical action is identical to the semi-auto categories above. The only difference is in the fire control group (FCG): the internal parts (sear, disconnector, auto sear) that control when the hammer is released.

The FCG Distinction

In a semi-auto FCG, the disconnector traps the hammer after each shot and requires you to release and re-pull the trigger. In a full-auto FCG, an auto sear catches and releases the hammer each time the bolt goes into battery (fully closed), so the gun fires continuously as long as the trigger is held and ammunition is available.

Select-fire adds a selector switch that mechanically engages or disengages the auto sear. The selector has positions for SAFE, SEMI, and FULL (or BURST). The M4 carbine's selector has SEMI and BURST (3-round). The M16A1 had SEMI and AUTO.

Full-auto firearms in the US are regulated under the National Firearms Act (NFA). Civilian ownership of machine guns is legal in most states but limited to pre-1986 registered transferable guns (Hughes Amendment to FOPA 1986), which are extremely expensive ($10,000-$250,000+ depending on type). Post-86 machine guns can only be held by SOT/FFL dealers, military, and law enforcement.

Bottom line: Full auto is not a separate action type. It's a trigger group modification applied to any of the semi-auto action types. An M16 is a gas-operated, direct-impingement, rotating-bolt rifle with a select-fire FCG. An MP5 is a roller-delayed blowback SMG with a select-fire FCG. Same mechanical actions as their semi-auto counterparts, different sear arrangement.
Chapter 05

Feed Systems

The feed system is how ammunition gets into the chamber. It's a completely separate concept from the action type, though certain actions are traditionally paired with certain feed systems. A bolt-action can be magazine-fed, a semi-auto can be belt-fed. These are independent variables.

Detachable Box Magazine

The standard. A spring-loaded box (straight, curved, or double-stack) that inserts into a magwell. Rounds are stacked under follower pressure and stripped off the top by the bolt. AR-15 mags, AK mags, Glock mags, Beretta mags. Quick to reload by swapping the entire magazine.

STANAG / PMAGGlock magsAK mags

Internal Box Magazine

Same concept as a detachable box mag but fixed inside the rifle. You load rounds from the top of the action, either individually or via a stripper clip. Most traditional bolt-action hunting rifles: Mauser 98, Springfield 1903, early Remington 700s. The magazine floor plate can usually be opened for unloading.

Mauser 98M1903Mosin-Nagant

Tubular Magazine

Rounds stacked end-to-end in a tube, usually under the barrel (shotguns, lever rifles) or inside the buttstock (rare). A spring and follower push rounds toward the action. Loading gate on the side or bottom. Remember the spitzer bullet issue with lever guns, pointed bullets + tube mag + recoil = bad day.

Mossberg 500Henry LeverMarlin 60

Rotary Magazine

Rounds sit in a spool/rotor that indexes to present each round to the feed lips. More compact than a box mag of the same capacity, very smooth feeding. The Ruger 10/22's factory magazine is a 10-round rotary. Also Savage's rotary mags and the classic Mannlicher-Schoenauer.

Ruger 10/22Savage 110

Drum Magazine

A large circular housing with rounds arranged in a spiral track under spring tension. High capacity (50-100 rounds typical) but heavy, bulky, and historically less reliable than box mags due to complex spring systems and feeding geometry. The Thompson SMG's drum mag is iconic.

Thompson drumMagpul D-60KCI Glock drum

Belt-Fed

Rounds are linked in a belt (disintegrating links that fall away, or non-disintegrating reusable belts). A feed mechanism in the gun strips rounds from the belt and chambers them. Machine guns: M240, M249 SAW, M60, M2 .50 cal, PKM. Highest sustained fire capacity but requires a belt container or assistant gunner.

M240BM249 SAWM2 .50 Cal

Loading Devices (Clips)

Clips are not feed systems. They're loading devices that help you charge a magazine faster. The magazine is what feeds the gun. The clip feeds the magazine.

Stripper Clip

A stamped metal strip that holds rounds by their case rims or extraction grooves. You place the clip on a guide machined into the receiver or bolt, then thumb the rounds down into the internal magazine. The empty clip is discarded. Speed loaders for the pre-detachable-mag era. Still used to quickly load detachable mags via spoon/guide adapters.

Mauser stripperSKS stripperAR-15 stripper + spoon

En-Bloc Clip

The clip goes INTO the gun WITH the rounds and is integral to the feeding process. The M1 Garand is the famous example: eight rounds in a stamped steel clip are inserted as a unit into the receiver. The action feeds from the clip directly. When the last round fires, the clip is ejected with that iconic "PING." You cannot partially load a Garand clip easily in the gun.

M1 Garand en-blocMannlicher clips

Moon Clips

Thin circular metal discs that hold revolver rounds by their rims. Allows revolvers chambered for rimless cartridges (like 9mm or .45 ACP) to headspace and extract properly. Also speeds up revolver reloads because you load/unload all rounds simultaneously. Full moon (holds full cylinder), half moon (holds half).

S&W 625 (.45 ACP)S&W 986 (9mm)
Chapter 06

Clip vs. Magazine

This is the single most common terminology error in firearms. Here's the definitive answer.

The Rule

A magazine is a spring-loaded container that actively feeds rounds into the chamber. It has a body, a spring, a follower, and feed lips. When you slam a box into the bottom of an AR-15 or a Glock, that's a magazine.

A clip holds rounds together for loading into a magazine or into the gun's feeding mechanism. It does not have a spring. It does not feed rounds. It's a loading tool.

The thing in your Glock? Magazine. The thing you use to load an SKS? Stripper clip. The thing you shove into an M1 Garand? En-bloc clip. The thing in your AR-15? Magazine. The metal strip you use to quickly load your AR-15 magazines? Stripper clip.

Magazine

  • Has a spring and follower
  • Actively feeds rounds
  • Stays in/on the gun during firing
  • Detachable or internal/fixed
  • Box, drum, tubular, rotary
VS

Clip

  • No spring, no follower
  • Holds rounds for loading
  • Discarded or ejected after loading
  • Stripper, en-bloc, or moon
  • A loading device, not a feed system
Why it matters: It doesn't really, in casual conversation. But if you're at a gun shop, a range, or talking to anyone who knows firearms, calling a magazine a "clip" will immediately mark you as uninformed. It's like calling a monitor a "CPU." People will know what you mean, but they'll also know you don't know what you're talking about.
