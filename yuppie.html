IThe Manifesto

What we mean when we say yuppie.

Yuppie isn't a class so much as a longing. It is the costume of inherited summers, of boarding-school weekends and tennis-club summers and sailboats with names like Persephone. It is the late-American belief that the right polo shirt, the right dog, the right wagon in the drive could prove that you belong.

The acronym, of course, is from young urban professional: Reagan-era money, Wall Street suspenders, the BMW in the garage and the cocaine in the desk drawer. But the aesthetic survived its decade. It went underground, faded like a Polaroid left on a sunlit windowsill, and resurfaced in 2010 on the cover of an indie record. Two flavors. One uniform. Eternal nostalgia.

IIThe Two Flavors

One look. Two centuries of longing.

The same wardrobe carries two different ghosts. One says acquire; the other says remember.

Flavor One · Hot

The 80s Power Yuppie

Patrick Bateman in a corner office. Pinstripes from Brooks, suspenders in arterial red, hair laid down like wet tile. The Filofax open, the Cristal chilling, the Rolodex spinning. This is yuppie as verb: to acquire, to leverage, to dominate brunch. Cold, ambitious, Reagan-era opulence.

Soundtrack · Genesis, Phil Collins solo, Whitney Houston

Flavor Two · Faded

The Contra-Era Indie Yuppie

Same uniform, but the photograph is twenty years old and someone you loved is in it. This is the Vampire Weekend version: ironic Columbia worship, ironic-not-ironic Polo Ralph Lauren, polaroids on a kitchen wall in Brooklyn. The longing is for a summer that may have only ever existed in the picture.

Soundtrack · Vampire Weekend, Paul Simon, Talking Heads

IIIThe Uniform

The uniform, by the catalog.

Every aesthetic has a kit. The yuppie kit is a closed system: pieces signal each other, a polo invites khakis, khakis invite Top-Siders, Top-Siders invite a signet ring. None of it is invented. All of it is inherited, even when bought new at Bloomingdale's last Tuesday.

  • ARalph Lauren polo. The pony is required. Collar may be popped, never both points.
  • BKhakis or chinos. Pleated front by 1988, flat front by 2010. Cuffed if you sail.
  • CSperry Top-Siders. Brown, broken in. White rope laces. Worn sockless from May to September.
  • DCartier Tank watch. Or a Timex if you're being honest. Either reads correctly.
  • ESignet ring. Pinky, gold, ideally inherited. A bare crest beats invented heraldry.
  • FPearls or Tiffany. Studs, single strand, or the silver bean. Restraint is the point.
  • GCable knit, draped. Tied around the shoulders. Never actually worn.
  • HRay-Ban Wayfarers. Black, square. Risk Persol if you've earned it.

IVThe Setting

Where it happens.

The aesthetic requires a set. Without a horizon line of pastel beach houses, without hydrangeas in cobalt pots and an American flag on a white pole, it is just a polo shirt and a watch.

  • ★Martha's Vineyard. Edgartown specifically. Rent a house with a name.
  • ★Nantucket. ACK code on the luggage. Reds optional but earned.

VThe Polaroid Look

How the photograph itself reads.

The aesthetic is half wardrobe, half film stock. A yuppie in a sharp digital photo is just an investment banker with a haircut. The polaroid does the work: it ages the present, photographs the longing, makes everyone in the frame already a memory.

The look, technically.

  • iPolaroid 600 / SX-70. The square frame, white border, slight aging halo at the edges.
  • iiDirect flash. No bounce, no diffusion. Eyes catch the strobe.
  • iiiSlight overexposure. Skin glows, walls go cream, the world looks generous.
  • ivWarm yellow cast. Kodachrome memory in instant-film clothing.
  • vSoft focus. Always a touch out, the way an actual happy memory blurs.
  • viCaption strip. A handwritten date in blue ballpoint at the bottom is the closer.
