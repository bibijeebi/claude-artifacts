1 · The mental model

Every estimate is the same equation dressed up differently:

PRICE = (crew-days × crew day rate) + travel + materials + equipment/consumables + margin
…then sanity-checked against per-unit market rates.

You price bottom-up (count the work, convert to days, stack the costs) and check top-down (does the total land in a sane $/vent, $/sq ft, or $/day range for this kind of building?). When the two disagree, one of your assumptions is wrong — find it before the customer does.

Jeff: "Honestly, pricing jobs isn't difficult. The main thing is you have to have somebody go walk it."

He's right: the math is arithmetic. The skill is the counting — seeing everything on the walk that becomes hours, and everything that becomes an adder. Money gets left on the table in the walk, not in the spreadsheet.

2 · The site walk — where the money is

Each factor below isn't trivia; each converts directly to hours, equipment, or risk. Walk with this list until it's instinct.

Look atWhy it moves the price
Ceiling heightsOver ~12 ft = ladders slow you down; over ~16 ft = lift rental + certified operator + slower production. A lift can double a day count.
System count & tonnageEach air handler is a setup/teardown cycle. Count AHUs/RTUs, note coil condition while you're there.
Opening countSupplies + returns per system. The core unit of residential pricing and the cross-check on commercial.
Duct type & conditionFlex vs metal vs duct board. Bare metal on the supply side = encapsulation conversation (see red flags). Duct board = gentler methods, slower.
Hose runsHow far from truck/vacuum to the furthest register? Every extra 50 ft of hose is setup time and suction loss. Jeff's literal question: "how many vacuum hoses are we gonna need?"
Parking & setup zonesWhere does the truck sit? Loading dock? Can you stage negative air machines close? Urban parking or long carries add dead hours.
Access & schedulingOccupied office = nights/weekends (multiplier). Jail = escorts, tool control, counts — everything takes longer. Server rooms, clean areas = containment.
Power & waterAvailable circuits for equipment, or generator?
Distance from shopDay-trip or overnight? This flips the whole travel line (next section).
Photos of everythingMechanical rooms, worst ducts, access panels, staging areas. Photos price the job AND sell the job.
Jeff: "If they just send you blueprints and pictures, it doesn't paint the whole picture. If you don't know, you're leaving sometimes a lot of money on the table."

3 · Production rates — the estimating heart

Days = work ÷ production rate. These are industry-typical ranges to reason with until you replace them with CQA actuals (section 7). A "crew" here is 2–3 techs.

Work typeTypical crew productionNotes
Residential system1–2 full systems / day~3–5 hrs per average system incl. setup. Big custom homes = 1/day.
Commercial office, good access~3,000–6,000 sq ft of served floor area / dayDrops hard with lifts, nights, containment.
Institutional (jail, hospital)50–70% of office rateEscorts, security, infection control, occupied wings.
Dryer vents (multifamily)15–30 units / dayAccess and stack length dependent.
Encapsulation passRoughly comparable to the cleaning pass on the same ductClean first — always. Separate pass, separate day count.
The habit that builds this skill: after every job you work, note (1) building type, (2) sq ft or opening count, (3) crew size, (4) actual days. Four numbers. Twenty jobs of that = your own production table, better than any industry chart.

4 · The cost stack

LineHow it's built
Laborcrew-days × crew day rate. Day rate = loaded wages + overhead + profit target. (Capture CQA's — section 7.)
Travel — day tripMileage + drive hours (door-to-door pay means drive time IS labor cost).
Travel — overnight$150 / person / night (hotel + food — Jeff's number) × crew × nights, plus mobilization drive time both ways.
MaterialsEncapsulant ≈ $100/gal, ≈ $500/job typical (Jeff's numbers). Sanitizer, filters, plastic, tape.
EquipmentLift rental, extra negative air machines, generator. Rentals get quoted, not guessed.
AddersNight/weekend work (~1.15–1.5×), security processing time, disposal, parking.
Margin math — don't mix these up: a 30% markup on $10k of cost = $13,000. A 30% margin means cost ÷ 0.70 = $14,286. Decide which one CQA means when Jeff says a percentage.
Jeff: "If we're staying the night — that's where the money, that's where the jobs start adding up." Overnight jobs are bigger tickets. Price the per diem in and they're the best revenue days on the calendar.

6 · Practice scenarios

Work each one on paper (or the calculator) before revealing the model answer. The point is the reasoning chain, not hitting the exact number.

RESIDENTIAL

A · The Fuquay two-system house

2,900 sq ft, two systems (attic + crawl), 16 supplies, 5 returns, flex duct in decent shape, easy driveway parking, 35 minutes from your house. Homeowner mentions "dust on the vents."

Count: 2 systems ≈ one full crew-day for a 2-man crew (production table: 1–2 systems/day; two systems with attic + crawl access = the slow end). Day trip, no per diem.

Stack: 1 crew-day at day rate + ~1.5 hr total drive + minimal materials. If your 2-man residential day rate is ~$1,200–1,600, the bottom-up lands ~$1,300–1,700.

Cross-check: 21 openings × $35–45/vent ≈ $735–945 per the cheap per-vent lens — but two separate systems means two setups, so system pricing beats vent pricing here: 2 × ($600–800/system) = $1,200–1,600. Converges. Quote in that band.

The look-for: attic system + flex + dust complaint = check the supply plenum for bare metal or liner damage. Photo it. That's the encapsulation seed planted on day one.

GOVERNMENT

B · The jail dorm wing

County detention center, one dorm wing: ~9,000 sq ft served area, 2 AHUs, hard metal duct, 10-ft ceilings, escort required at all times, tool control in/out adds ~45 min each way daily, day-shift work only. 75 minutes from the shop — day trips. They want cleaning per NADCA ACR.

Count: 9,000 sq ft at institutional rate (office rate × ~0.6 ≈ 2,500–3,500 sq ft/day) ≈ 3 crew-days. Add the escort/tool-control tax: ~1.5 hr/day of paid non-production ≈ half a day across the job → call it 3.5.

Stack: 3.5 days × commercial crew day rate (say $2,400–3,000 for a 3-man crew) = $8,400–10,500. Drive: 2.5 hr/day × days × loaded rate — door-to-door pay makes this real money, ~$900–1,200. Materials light unless encapsulating. Total lands ~$9,500–12,000.

Cross-check: ~$1.05–1.35/sq ft — sane for secure-facility work (open offices run lower, institutions higher).

The edge: the escort tax is exactly what competitors who've never worked corrections underestimate. CQA prices it from experience — that's a moat, not a cost.

COMMERCIAL

C · The overnight office job

Three-story office, 40,000 sq ft, 6 RTUs, occupied — nights only (6 pm–4 am), 14-ft lobby ceilings need a scissor lift for two days, 2 hr 45 min from the shop. Crew of 3.

Count: 40,000 sq ft at good-access office rate (4,000–5,000/day) = 8–10 days, but nights + lift drag production ~20% → call it 10–12 crew-nights.

Travel: too far to commute — the crew stays. 3 people × ~11 nights × $150 = ~$4,950 per diem, plus mobilization drives. This line is why "who's closer wins" matters on far jobs.

Stack: 11 nights × day rate ($2,400–3,000) with a night multiplier (~1.15) ≈ $30k–38k labor + $5k per diem + lift rental (~$400–600) + materials. Total ~$36k–44k.

Cross-check: ~$0.90–1.10/sq ft for after-hours multi-story = believable. If a competitor bids $22k on this, they either have a local crew (no per diem) or they missed the nights — you don't have to chase it.

Jeff's point made concrete: this single job = a month of residential one-offs. Overnight jobs are where it adds up.

UPSELL

D · Encapsulation attach

Mid-job on a commercial cleaning, you open a supply trunk and see bare, oxidizing metal where liner used to be. Cleaning was quoted at $7,000. What's the encapsulation conversation worth?

Jeff's rule: encapsulation usually bills more than the cleaning side. So the attach on a $7k cleaning is a $7k+ line, against ~$500 of coating and a comparable pass of labor already mobilized on site — no extra travel, no extra sale cycle. Highest-margin work in the company.

The close (his words): photograph the bare metal, show the customer: "there's a chance this needs to be encapsulated — we won't know until we get into it — here's what we're seeing." Photos make the sell.

Your job as a tech: you are the sensor. Bare metal on the supply side = flag it, shoot it, text it. One attach a month pays for a lot.

8 · Red flags & money-left-on-the-table

  • Bare metal on the supply side → encapsulation conversation, every time. Photo first.
  • Pricing from blueprints alone → pad heavily or insist on a walk. The picture never tells you parking, hose runs, or ceiling truth.
  • Forgot the drive → door-to-door pay means a 2-hr-away day job carries ~12 paid crew-hours of windshield per day. It's a line item, not noise.
  • Occupied building, day rate quoted → if they can't shut down, it's night work. Ask before pricing, not after winning.
  • Coils not quoted → dirty coils found on the walk are a clean adder (and the customer's actual airflow complaint half the time).
  • No photo set from the walk → you priced it once; photos let Jeff re-price it without a second trip, and sell it besides.
  • Suspiciously low competitor → they're closer (no per diem), they missed a multiplier, or they're a blow-and-go. Only the first one should change your number.
The apprenticeship loop: walk jobs with this checklist → guess the price before Jeff says his → compare → ask about the gap. Twenty reps of guess-then-check and you'll be inside 10% of him. That's the whole skill.
