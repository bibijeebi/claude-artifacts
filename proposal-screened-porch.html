Proposal #018 — Draft

Screened-In Porch Overhaul

Strip the failed brad-nailed screen framing, fix whatever the builder hid underneath, and rebuild the screen walls the right way: real 2x4 framing, structural screws, a dog-proof knee wall.

Est. Cost $1,500–$4,000
Difficulty 3/5
Time Multiple weekends
Category Home
Status Draft
This proposal runs on assumptions.

Costs and the cut list assume a ~12x14 ft porch, 3–4 bays between posts, ~8 ft ceiling, solid floor, intact roof. Real numbers need: linear feet of screen wall, ceiling height, bay count, and a screwdriver probe of every 4x4 base.

The Vision

The porch came screened when we bought the house in 2023. Within a month it started falling apart. The screen tore, the 2x2 framing bowed, and the whole thing was held on with brad nails and a "crampy" glue that never bonded. It was cosmetic work done to pass a walkthrough, not to last.

The good news: the expensive structure is fine. The floor framing, the 4x4 posts, and the sectioned roofs they hold up are all intact and original. What failed is the cheapest, most replaceable layer, the screen infill. So this isn't a teardown. It's a strip-and-rebuild of one non-structural layer, plus an inspection of the structure we're keeping while it's exposed.

End state: a screened porch framed like an actual wall, with a solid knee wall at the bottom that blocks Kong, pet-grade screen above it, and a finished ceiling with a fan and lights. Built once, properly, so we are not re-screening it every spring.

Why It Failed (so we don't repeat it)

Brad nails are 18-gauge wire. They are a clamping fastener: you shoot them to hold trim still while glue cures, then the glue does the real work. The builder used them as the permanent fastener, which they were never designed to be. Brads have almost zero withdrawal resistance, so any load, wind on the screen, a dog leaning, the wood twisting, pulls them straight back out.

The glue made it worse. Outdoor wood expands and contracts constantly with humidity and temperature. A rigid or non-exterior-rated glue can't survive that cycling, the bond shears, and the joint is left holding nothing. That is the "fell apart a month after closing" timeline exactly.

The 2x2 lumber is the other half. 2x2 is cut from the twist-prone outer part of the log. Poorly fastened at wide intervals, it has nothing forcing it straight, so it bows, and the bow pops whatever brads were left. The fix is not just "screws instead of brads." It's screws AND ditching the 2x2. Screwing bad lumber together just gives you a stronger version of a wall that still bows.

The Big Fork: Decide This First

Three end states, each step up roughly doubles cost and code burden. This proposal is priced for the first row. If we want year-round use, that's a different (much bigger) project.

Keep vs. Toss

Keep (verify, don't assume): floor / deck framing, 4x4 posts, roof rafters and beam.

Toss completely: every 2x2, all brad-nailed screen frame, old screen, old spline, old caulk. None of it is worth saving and none of it was structural.

Inspect before closing anything up. Once new framing and a ceiling go in, these become invisible for years:
  • 4x4 post bases. Classic rot point, especially if a post sits flat on the deck with no metal standoff. Probe each one with a screwdriver. Soft wood = replace the post.
  • Post-to-beam connections. Should be a real metal connector or through-bolt, not toenails.
  • Roof framing and ledger. No attic access here, so the only inspection window is from below before a new ceiling goes up, or from on the roof. Look for rot at the ledger (porch roof to house) and water staining on rafters.

Sub-Projects

A. Demo & Structural Inspection

Pull all 2x2s, screen, spline, and caulk. Get back to bare posts, floor, and roof. Then run the inspection above. Repair any rot found, add post standoffs or base flashing if a post is sitting unprotected on the deck. This is the cheap-but-critical phase, materials are near zero, it's mostly labor and a screwdriver.

Est: $0–$300 (mostly labor; more if a post or section of ledger needs replacing)

B. Frame the Screen Walls Properly

This is the real upgrade and where the money goes. Build each screen wall like an actual wall, not like trim:

Plates: 2x4 pressure-treated bottom and top plate, screwed (not nailed) to the floor and up into the roof beam. Verticals: 2x4 PT studs every 24–32 in on center, so each screen panel spans short and the spline has plenty to bite. Knee wall: a solid framed wall ~24–36 in tall along the bottom, sheathed and clad with PVC or siding panel; screen only above it. All framing-to-framing with exterior structural screws, pre-drilled near board ends.

Est: $400–$900 in PT lumber, screws, and knee-wall cladding

C. Screen System & Pet-Grade Screen

Skip raw staple-and-2x2. Use a Screen Tight-style track-and-snap-cap system: a base track screws to the new framing, screen rolls in, a snap cap hides the spline channel. Forgiving to install, and when a panel tears you re-screen that bay in minutes instead of rebuilding trim. The cap is decorative and snaps on with no fasteners fighting the wood movement.

Kong factor: a Saint Bernard shreds standard fiberglass screen on day one. The knee wall blocks dog-height pushing; above it, use heavy-duty pet-grade screen on at least the lower screened section. Costs more per roll, saves an annual re-screen.

Est: $250–$600 (track, caps, pet-grade screen, spline)

D. Ceiling, Fan & Electrical (do it while open)

The ceiling is open right now, which will never be this easy again. Add a beadboard or PVC-panel ceiling, a fan-rated box for a ceiling fan, and GFCI exterior outlets. New circuits are permitted electrical work, do it on the books. Optional but this is the moment.

Est: $300–$800 (ceiling panel, fan, GFCI outlets, lights, wire)

Only the joints that actually need sealing. Caulk seals, it does not hold.

Code Reality Checks

Guardrail: if the porch floor is more than 30 in above grade, code requires a real structural guardrail (36 in min). Screen framing is not a guardrail. The knee wall can double as one if it's framed and anchored to guardrail standard, build it that way.

Footings: any replacement 4x4 post gets a footing 12 in min below grade on a proper pad.

Electrical: exterior-rated, GFCI-protected outlets; fan box rated for the fan weight.

What Needs to Happen First

Measure everything: linear feet of screen wall, ceiling height, number of bays between posts, and floor height above grade (the 30 in guardrail trigger). Probe every 4x4 base before pricing anything, a single rotten post changes the budget. Confirm which county has jurisdiction and what the porch-alteration permit costs.

Decision for Bekah

This can run lean at ~$1,500 (demo, reframe, re-screen, no ceiling work) or full at ~$4,000 with the knee wall, finished ceiling, fan, and lights. The structure is sound, so there's no emergency, this is a quality-of-life rebuild done on our timeline. Two questions worth deciding up front: do we want pure screen, or spend a bit more framing so a future jump to 3-season vinyl panels is easy? And do we tackle the ceiling/fan/electrical now while it's open, or screen-only and revisit later?
