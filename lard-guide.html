A Cute Guide to Pig Fat

Bacon grease and lard come from the same animal, but they are NOT the same thing. Here's everything you need to know.

There are really two questions that determine what kind of pig fat you're dealing with: where on the pig does it come from, and what did someone do to it before it reached your kitchen? Those two axes give us four very different ingredients.

The Fancy One

👑 Leaf Lard

The visceral fat surrounding the kidneys and loin. This is the cleanest, whitest, most neutral fat on the entire pig. When rendered, it has almost zero pork flavor, which makes it the undisputed queen of pastry fats.

It produces flakier pie crusts than butter because of its crystal structure: larger fat crystals create more distinct layers when laminated into dough. Pro move: 50/50 leaf lard and butter gives you flakiness AND flavor.

Smoke Point~400°F
Pork FlavorAlmost None
Where to Get ItButcher / Farmer
Best for: pie crusts, biscuits, pastries, tamales
The Workhorse

💪 Rendered Lard (Back Fat)

The thick subcutaneous layer along the pig's back (fatback). When you chop it up, heat it low and slow, and strain out the cracklings, you get clean rendered lard. White when solid, mild porky flavor, and a great all-purpose cooking fat.

This is what your great-grandmother used for literally everything: frying, biscuits, beans, tortillas, cornbread, refried beans. It's the default "lard" in most recipes and most of human culinary history.

Smoke Point~370-400°F
Pork FlavorMild
Where to Get ItButcher / Render It
Best for: frying, tortillas, biscuits, beans, everything
The Flavor Bomb

🔥 Bacon Drippings

The stuff left in the pan after you cook bacon. Technically it IS rendered pig fat, but from belly fat that was cured (salt, sugar, nitrates) and smoked first. So it carries all that delicious baggage: smoky, salty, deeply porky, and brown from Maillard compounds.

Incredible for frying eggs, seasoning greens, cornbread, and cast iron maintenance. But it'll make your pie crust taste like a BLT, and it goes rancid faster because of dissolved meat solids. Strain it through cheesecloth and keep it in the fridge.

Smoke Point~325-375°F
Pork FlavorMAXIMUM
Where to Get ItYour Breakfast Pan
Best for: eggs, greens, cornbread, refried beans, cast iron
The Grocery Store One

🏭 Hydrogenated Store Lard

The white block or tub on the shelf (Armour, Morrell, store brands). Industrially rendered, bleached, deodorized, and often hydrogenated to be shelf-stable. The irony: hydrogenation adds trans fats, which destroys one of real lard's biggest advantages over Crisco (zero trans fats naturally).

It works fine for frying and makes decent tortillas, but it's lost most of what makes real lard special. Read the label: if it says "hydrogenated" or "BHT added," that's this one. It's the vegetable shortening of the lard world.

Smoke Point~370°F
Pork FlavorNone (bleached out)
Where to Get ItAny Grocery Store
Best for: when you can't get real lard, basic frying

💡 Storage Tips

Real Rendered Lard

~3 months at room temp, 6-12 months refrigerated, basically forever frozen. Keep it in glass jars away from light.

Bacon Drippings

Strain through cheesecloth first to remove the meat bits (they accelerate spoilage). Refrigerate and use within 1-2 months. The jar by the stove is tradition, but the fridge is safer.

The Homesteader Move

When processing a hog, separate the leaf fat (around kidneys) from back fat and belly trimmings. Render them in separate batches. Label the leaf lard jar "PASTRY" and guard it with your life. Everything else is your daily driver.
