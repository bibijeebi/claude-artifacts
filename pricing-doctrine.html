1 · Who prices, and how the number moves

  • DOCTRINEJeff and Perry are the only two who price jobs.
  • DOCTRINEBecky sits in, listens, writes the numbers down, and emails the prices out. She transmits, she does not set.
  • IMPLIEDPricing capacity is capped at two people's calendars, and a site walk means one of them physically drives there. Every quote far from Greenville costs principal windshield time.
  • GAPWhat does Becky's price email look like, and does a quote template exist?

2 · The cardinal rule: walk it

Jeff: "Honestly, pricing jobs isn't difficult. The main thing is, you have to have somebody go to it to walk around and look."
  • DOCTRINEThe walk checklist, in his words: how tall are the ceilings, how far (the distances), how many vacuum hoses are we gonna need, where can we park, where can we set up. Plus: "there are a lot of things that you got to know."
  • DOCTRINEBlueprints and pictures alone do not paint the whole picture. Pricing without a walk leaves money on the table.
  • IMPLIEDThe walk factors are all labor-hour variables: height drives ladder/lift time, distance and hose count drive setup time, parking and staging drive dead time. The walk is an hours audit, not a look-around.
  • GAPDoes CQA ever price small jobs sight-unseen, and if so, what padding rule applies?

3 · Travel and overnight economics

  • DOCTRINEOvernight cost basis: ~$150 per person per night, covering the hotel and the day's food.
  • DOCTRINEStaying the night is where the money is, where the jobs start adding up. Multi-day away work = the big tickets.
  • IMPLIEDA 3-man crew away for a 5-night week carries $2,250 of per diem before a single duct is cleaned. Any bid on far work must eat that line; any competitor closer to the site than CQA has a structural price edge, and vice versa.
  • IMPLIEDHours are paid door-to-door, so drive time is real labor cost on every quote, day trip or not.
  • GAPMileage/vehicle cost figured per job, or absorbed in overhead?

4 · The encapsulation playbook

  • DOCTRINEEncapsulating usually bills more than the duct cleaning side of the same job.
  • DOCTRINESequencing rule: you cannot encapsulate dirty ductwork. Clean first, always. It is an upsell on a current job, not a standalone.
  • DOCTRINEThe spotting trigger: bare metal visible on a supply trunk means the liner is gone internally. That is the flag.
  • DOCTRINEThe customer line: there might be a chance this needs to be encapsulated, we won't know until we get into it.
  • DOCTRINEThe close: take a lot of pictures. Show the customer what you've got. "It's a lot easier to sell it." And the finished product looks good, which sells the next one.
  • DOCTRINEMaterial: paint runs about $100/gallon, roughly $500 a job.
  • DOCTRINEJeff's stated target: encapsulate more commercial jobs. Even one out of five, one out of ten, adds a lot of money.
  • IMPLIEDAttach economics: on a $7k commercial cleaning, the encapsulation line is $7k+ against ~$500 of material and a crew already mobilized. No extra travel, no new customer acquisition. Highest-margin work in the company.
  • IMPLIEDSpotting is an in-the-duct skill. Whoever is inside the ductwork daily is the sensor; estimators who no longer do the cleaning will miss it. The fix is doctrine for techs, not sharper eyes at the top.
  • GAPHow is encapsulation priced: per square foot, per linear foot, or judgment? Coverage per gallon?

5 · Photos as company practice

  • DOCTRINETake a lot of pictures, on every job. Photos are how findings get shown and how upsells get sold.
  • IMPLIEDThe same photo set serves four jobs: mid-job upsell close, re-pricing without a second site visit, the completion documentation government specs demand, and marketing. One habit, four payoffs. Worth formalizing as a per-job photo protocol.
