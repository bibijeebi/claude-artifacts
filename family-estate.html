The Short Version

How It Actually Works

If There's a Will (Most Likely)

Dad almost certainly has a will based on what he's said. A will names an executor (SC calls them a "personal representative"). That person is legally responsible for filing the will with probate court, inventorying all assets, paying any debts and taxes, and then distributing everything according to the will's instructions.

The executor could be his wife, one of us, a lawyer, or a trusted friend. We don't currently know who it is. Probate court in the county where Dad lives supervises the whole process.

If There Is No Will

If someone dies without a will in South Carolina ("intestate"), the state decides who gets what by formula. The surviving spouse gets a portion and the children split the rest. In blended family situations this gets messy fast. We don't think this is the case here, but it's worth confirming.

Timeline

Probate in South Carolina typically takes 8 to 12 months minimum, often longer when there are complex assets like multiple properties, investment accounts, and so on. Nothing gets distributed to anyone until debts, taxes, and administrative costs are settled. This isn't optional, it's how the process works by law.

What Dad's Wife Is Entitled To

This is important to understand clearly, because SC law gives surviving spouses strong protections that exist regardless of what the will says.

The Elective Share

Under SC Code § 62-2-201, a surviving spouse has the right to claim one-third of the probate estate, even if the will leaves them nothing. This is the legal minimum. She cannot be fully cut out, and she doesn't need anyone's permission to claim it. She just files a petition with probate court.

However, the elective share only applies to the probate estate, which is a specific legal term. It does NOT include assets that pass outside of probate (life insurance, retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, etc.). More on that in the next section.

The spouse must file for the elective share within 8 months of the date of death or 6 months after probate of the will, whichever is later. If she misses that window, the claim is barred.

A spouse can waive their elective share rights, but only through a properly executed written agreement (like a prenup) signed voluntarily after full written financial disclosure. A verbal agreement means nothing. We don't know if a prenup exists.

Probate vs. Non-Probate Assets

This distinction is probably the most important thing on this entire page. Not everything goes through probate, and the stuff that doesn't go through probate is where beneficiary designations control who gets what. Nobody, including the executor or a court, can change those designations after death.

Goes Through Probate

Assets owned solely in Dad's name with no beneficiary designation. Bank accounts in his name only. Real estate in his name only. Personal property (vehicles, valuables, etc.). These are what the will controls, and what the spouse's 1/3 elective share comes out of.

Bypasses Probate

Life insurance pays directly to named beneficiaries. Retirement accounts (401k, IRA) go to named beneficiaries. Joint accounts with survivorship rights go to the surviving owner. Transfer-on-death (TOD) accounts go to whoever's named. Trust assets distribute according to trust terms. These are untouchable by anyone not named.

When Dad says "everyone is set" and "you'd all get a lot of money," he most likely means he's set up beneficiary designations on insurance policies and investment accounts. If that's the case, that money flows directly to whoever is named, it doesn't go through probate, and it's not part of the pot that the elective share comes from. This is the cleanest, most protected way to structure things.

The key takeaway

If the bulk of the money is in life insurance and retirement accounts with us named as beneficiaries, that money is essentially locked in. Nobody can contest it, redirect it, or claim a share of it. It goes straight to whoever Dad named, usually within weeks, not months.

The Concern We Need to Talk About

Dad's wife has recently been telling people that Dad has dementia. Nobody else in the family believes this. No doctor has said this. Dad seems fully competent to everyone who interacts with him.

We need to be aware of why this matters legally, because it could be the groundwork for one of two things.

Play #1: Conservatorship / Guardianship

If she can establish that Dad is mentally incapacitated, she could petition probate court for conservatorship (control over his finances) and/or guardianship (control over his personal and medical decisions). This would give her legal authority to manage his money, make his medical decisions, and potentially influence his estate planning.

The good news: SC law has protections. She'd need a physician's examiner report, the court appoints both an independent attorney for Dad AND a separate Guardian ad Litem. All interested family members get notified and can show up, testify, and contest it. She can't just walk into court and make it happen.

Play #2: Will Contest After Death

The other possibility is that she's building a narrative now that she can use later. After Dad passes, she could try to contest the will by claiming he lacked "testamentary capacity" when he signed it (i.e., he had dementia and didn't know what he was doing). If successful, the will could be invalidated.

The protections here: Under SC Code § 62-3-407, the burden of proof is on whoever is contesting the will. She'd have to prove lack of capacity by "clear and convincing evidence," which is a high bar. The standard for testamentary capacity is actually pretty low: the person just needs to be aware they're making a will, understand what a will is, and know what it says. Even mild cognitive decline doesn't mean someone lacks capacity.

Red flags to watch for

Isolating Dad from family. Restricting phone calls or visits. Gatekeeping medical appointments. Doctor shopping for a dementia diagnosis. Pressuring Dad to change estate documents. Moving money into joint accounts. Getting added to accounts or deeds. If any of this starts happening, it's time to act immediately.

What We Need to Do

None of this requires panic, hostility, or accusations. The strongest move is calm, organized, and proactive.

Have a direct conversation with Dad

Not about the concern. Just about his estate plan. Frame it as wanting to understand the setup so we can help execute his wishes smoothly. See the questions list below.

Encourage a competency evaluation

If Dad gets a clean neuropsychological evaluation documented NOW from his own doctor, it becomes nearly impossible for anyone to later claim he was incapacitated when he signed his documents. This is the single most protective thing he can do.

Confirm his power of attorney documents

If Dad has a Durable Power of Attorney and Healthcare Power of Attorney naming someone he trusts, it completely blocks the conservatorship/guardianship play. If those documents exist and are current, nobody can petition the court for control over his finances or medical decisions. If they don't exist, he should create them.

Make sure his estate attorney knows the family dynamics

A good SC probate attorney will add protective language to documents, potentially video-record signings, and ensure everything is airtight against capacity challenges.

Document everything

If his wife continues making dementia claims or any of the red flags above start appearing, write down dates, what was said, who was present, and exact words. This creates a record that's useful in court if it ever comes to that.

Keep communication open

Stay in regular contact with Dad. If access starts getting restricted, that's the escalation point. South Carolina Adult Protective Services handles elder financial exploitation: 1-888-CARE4US (1-888-227-3487).

Questions We Need Answered

These can come up naturally in conversation. Nobody needs to interrogate him. We just need to know the structure, not the dollar amounts.

Do you have a will? Where is it physically kept? (Lawyer's office, safe, safe deposit box?)
Who is the executor / personal representative named in the will?
Do you have a trust, or just a will?
Who are the named beneficiaries on your life insurance policies?
Who are the named beneficiaries on your retirement / investment accounts?
Do you have a Durable Power of Attorney? Who is named?
Do you have a Healthcare Power of Attorney / Living Will? Who is named?
Is there a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement?
What is the name and contact info for your estate attorney?
Why these questions matter

We don't need to know who gets what or how much. We need to know the structure: who's in charge, where the documents are, and whether the right legal protections are in place. If Dad has a will, beneficiary designations, and power of attorney documents all set up properly, we're in good shape regardless of what anyone tries to do.

Bottom Line

Dad says we're taken care of. We have no reason to doubt that. But right now we're relying on trust and secondhand assurances without actually knowing the specifics. The smartest thing we can do, as a family, is have a straightforward conversation with Dad about the structure of his estate plan, confirm the right protections are in place, and make sure nobody is caught off guard.

This isn't about money. It's about making sure Dad's actual wishes are honored and that nobody can manipulate the situation. The law is on our side as long as the documents are in order.

Stay calm. Stay communicating. Stay together on this.
