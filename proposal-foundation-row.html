Front Bed Makeover

Bottom Line Up Front

This started as "swap the one gold shrub" and grew, by Bekah's call, into a full front-bed makeover. Five parts: (1) swap the lone gold shrub for matching boxwood, (2) add a boxwood on the right to extend the hedge past the side window, (3) cut and shape the whole overgrown hedge into one uniform line held below the windowsills, (4) wrap the bed in a continuous brick perimeter border, bricks sourced cheap off Marketplace, and (5) plant an all-perennial flower band in front, layered to bloom in relay so the band always shows color. Roughly $250–$520 all in, spread over two or three weekends, best done in fall. The reference image Bekah made is a layout target, not a literal spec, two honest gaps between it and reality are called out below so nobody is surprised.

Reference vs Reality

Bekah built a concept render to show where she wants this bed to land. It is a great layout target: uniform low hedge, continuous bordered bed, a full flower band, hedge extended right. Use it for the plan. But an AI render is aspirational, not buildable as-drawn, and two gaps between it and the current bed need stating up front.

Gap 1, the hedge cutback is a cut-and-recover job, not a one-day trim. The render shows a tight hedge held well below the windowsills. The real boxwoods are currently grown up over the sills. Boxwood does not love being hard-cut all at once, and cutting back into bare old wood can leave brown patches that take a full season to green back in. Getting from here to the render is staged over time, not instant.
Gap 2, the render's flower band behaves like annuals. That dense, uniform, blooming-everywhere look is how annuals behave: full color all season, then dead at frost, replant every spring. Bekah wants perennials, which is the right long-term call. An all-perennial band can look full and colorful all season, but only if it is built right, see the flower section. The fix is layering, not a single magic plant.

The Five Parts

Top-down plan. The five parts can be done in stages, but the order matters: hedge work first, border second, flowers last.

Part 1 & 2: The Boxwood Work

Two plants get bought and planted. Part 1 is the original job: pull the lone gold shrub by the entry and replace it with a dark-green boxwood that matches the row. Part 2 extends the hedge to the right, the current row peters out before the side window, leaving an awkward bare gap, so one more matching boxwood goes in on the right end to carry the line past the window.

That is two matching boxwoods to buy. Both should be the same cultivar as the existing hedge, confirmed with the crush-leaf test and a cut sprig taken to the nursery, see the next section. Buy them at a size that blends with the established hedge, not 1-gallon runts. Recommended: 10-gallon, planted in fall (about 2.5 to 3.5 ft, $100-180 each), or 7-gallon if the lift and dig is a concern. Fall planting is what makes a plant that size safe; a big rootball cannot pull enough water in summer heat.

Sprig-match both: bring one 6-inch cutting from the existing hedge to the nursery and buy two of the matched cultivar in one trip. Mismatched boxwood cultivars have slightly different leaf color and texture that will nag at the eye in a uniform hedge.

Part 3: Cut and Shape the Hedge

The existing boxwoods are overgrown, lumpy, and grown up over the windowsills. The render shows them as one tight, uniform line held below the sills. Getting there is the trickiest part of this whole project, because of how boxwood responds to hard pruning.

Why it cannot be one hard cut

Boxwood readily regrows from green, leafy wood. It is slow and stubborn about regrowing from bare old interior wood. If the whole hedge is sheared down hard in one pass, the cuts expose bare brown wood that can take a full growing season, sometimes two, to green back over. Do it all at once and the hedge looks rough for a year.

The staged cutback

  1. Decide the target height and line: a flat or gently rounded top held a few inches below the windowsills, and a consistent face. Run a string line so the whole row is cut to the same height.
  2. First cut, in fall or late winter: bring the hedge down toward the target, but do not chase a perfect tight line yet. Take the worst of the overgrowth off. Some bare patches will show; that is expected.
  3. Through the following growing season, the boxwood pushes new green growth over the cut areas. Water and let it recover.
  4. Second season, shear to the final tight uniform shape. From here it is a normal once or twice a year shearing to hold the line.
  5. Feed lightly in spring during recovery, a balanced shrub fertilizer, to push the regrowth that fills the gaps.
Reality check on timeline: the dead-tidy render hedge is a two-season result, not a two-weekend one. The bed will look dramatically better after the first cleanup, but the final crisp uniform line takes one full recovery season to fill in. That is just how boxwood works, not a project failure.

Confirm the Cultivar First

Both new boxwoods have to match the existing hedge, so confirm what it is before buying. The dark-green shrubs are most likely boxwood ('Wintergreen' or 'Green Velvet' are the usual suburban cultivars), with dwarf holly the alternate.

The crush-leaf test: crush a leaf off the existing hedge. Musty, faintly cat-pee smell plus leaves in opposite pairs means boxwood. Glossy, no smell, leaves alternating up the stem, maybe tiny berries means holly. Then cut a 6-inch sprig, bag it, and hand it to a real nursery, they will name the exact cultivar and sell the match. Beats any phone app.

Part 4: The Brick Perimeter Border

A continuous brick border wrapping the bed is what makes the whole thing read as intentional rather than just "shrubs against a wall." It also gives a clean mowing edge and holds the mulch in. Bekah's plan to source the brick off Facebook Marketplace is the right move, this is the easiest and cheapest part of the project.

Marketplace brick, done right

  • Buy one matching lot, all at once. A continuous border in two different brick types looks patchy. Find a single seller with enough brick for the whole run. People offload leftover pallets cheap or free constantly.
  • Estimate the run. Measure the full bed perimeter that gets edged. A standard brick laid end-to-end is about 8 inches, so number of bricks is roughly perimeter-in-inches divided by 8, then add 10-15% for cuts and breakage. Get a real number off the tape before buying.
  • Pick a style and commit to it: bricks laid flat end-to-end is the simplest. Bricks set at a 45-degree angle gives the classic "sawtooth" garden edge. Bricks stood on end is more formal and uses more brick. One style, the whole way around.
  • Watch for: crumbling or spalling faces, heavy paint, or mortar still stuck on, those slow the job. Clean, solid common brick or pavers are what to grab.

Setting the border

For a low edge border it can be as simple as a shallow trench: dig a flat-bottomed trench along the bed line, set the bricks tight together, level them with a rubber mallet, and backfill soil snug on both sides. For a border that never shifts, a thin bed of paver base or sand under the brick keeps it locked. Either way the goal is the top of the brick sitting just above soil level so the mower wheel can ride the lawn side cleanly.

Part 5: The All-Perennial Flower Band

Bekah wants perennials, plant once, comes back every year, no annual replant chore. That is the right long-term call. The one thing to understand going in: most single perennials bloom for only 3 to 6 weeks, then they are just green foliage. A perennial band that looks colorful all season long does not come from one plant, it comes from layering several perennials that bloom in relay, one taking over as the last fades, so the band as a whole always has something going.

How the relay works

The bed gets planted in drifts, small clusters of 3 to 5 of the same plant, repeated down the band, rather than one of everything scattered. Each plant is chosen for when it blooms, so the color moves down a relay from spring to fall.

Layout rules of thumb

  • Tallest at the back (against the hedge), lowest at the front (along the brick). Roughly: coneflower and rudbeckia and Russian sage at the back, catmint and daylily in the middle, coreopsis and dianthus along the front edge.
  • Plant in drifts, not singles. Clusters of 3 to 5 of one plant, repeated down the band. A repeated rhythm reads as designed; one-of-everything reads as a jumble.
  • Space for mature size. Perennials look sparse the first year, then fill in. Resist cramming, follow the spacing on the tag, the gaps close by year two.
  • All sun-lovers. This is a south-and-west-facing foundation bed, full sun and hot. Every plant above is picked for zone 8a heat and sun. Do not put shade plants here.
If a green gap between bloom waves ever bugs her: the easy future tweak is tucking a few annuals into the front-row pockets for that stretch. Not a commitment now, just the escape hatch. The all-perennial band is the plan.

Sequencing — What Order, Barney Style

The five parts are phased, but the order is not free. Doing it out of order means redoing work.

  1. Confirm the cultivar. Crush-leaf test, cut a sprig, get it identified. Do this before buying anything.
  2. First hedge cut (Part 3). Knock the overgrown hedge down toward target height. Doing this first means working in the bed before flowers are in the way.
  3. Swap the gold shrub and plant the right-side boxwood (Parts 1 & 2). Both new boxwoods go in. Transplant the healthy gold shrub the same day to a spot that wants a color pop, see below.
  4. Set the brick border (Part 4). Define the bed edge before planting flowers, so the flower spacing works to a known edge.
  5. Plant the perennial band (Part 5). Flowers last, into a bed that is already shaped, edged, and clean.
  6. Mulch and water in. Fresh mulch over the whole bed, deep-water everything.
  7. Following season: second hedge shearing to the final tight line once the boxwood has recovered from the first cut.

What To Do With the Old Gold Shrub

The gold shrub coming out of Part 1 is a healthy, established plant worth roughly $40 to $60 to replace. It does not get binned. Best option: transplant it the same day to a spot that wants a bright color pop, anchoring a bed corner, by the mailbox, near the shed, along a back fence line. Golden Euonymus and Sunshine Ligustrum both want to be a standalone accent, which is the opposite of being one stray in a uniform row. Dig it with a big rootball, replant it in the same dig session. If there is genuinely no spot for it, give it away, a "free plant, you dig" Marketplace post moves an established shrub fast. Only compost it if it is actually unhealthy.

Do not replant it back into this bed. Anywhere in this foundation line just relocates the mismatch. Gold only belongs here if it is deliberately repeated, which is a different look than the uniform hedge Bekah's render shows.

Timing

This whole project wants fall, roughly late September through November in central NC. Fall is the ideal window for planting the boxwoods (big plants establish safely in cool air and warm soil), the best time for the hard hedge cutback, and a good time to plant perennials so they root in before summer. Brick-laying can be done any time. Spring is the fallback if fall is missed, but late spring into summer is the worst window, heat punishes new plantings and stresses a hard-cut hedge.

Two honest expectations to sign up for: the tidy render hedge is a two-season result because boxwood needs a recovery year after a hard cut, and an all-perennial band blooms in waves with color moving across the bed, not every plant blooming at once like the render's annual-style look.
