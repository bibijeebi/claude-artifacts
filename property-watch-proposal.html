How it all works together

One app, four jobs, no monthly bill

The three camera-and-control pieces all live in the Apple Home app on your iPhone. Here's how they fit, plus the one clever trick that fixes the doorbell's only weak spot.

☉

Everything lands in Apple Home

Doorbell, garage, and (later) a yard camera all show up in the one app you already have on your iPhone. The doorbell records to your Apple TV and iCloud, scrambled so only you can see it. Saved automatically for 10 days.

☀

The porch light and doorbell team up at night

On its own, the doorbell sees at night in dim black-and-white. So the two smart bulbs hold a soft glow all evening, which is enough for the doorbell to see in color. When someone walks up, the doorbell tells the bulbs to jump to full brightness, so you get a clear, lit, color look at whoever's at the door. This is the exact setup the doorbell's own maker recommends.

⚛

The garage answers "did I leave it open?"

Glance at your phone from anywhere to see if it's open, tap to close it, or set it to close itself if it's been up too long. The old remotes and wall button keep working exactly as they do now.

⚑

The driveway alarm is the one thing that needs no phone

It just dings a box inside the house when a vehicle passes. No app, no internet, no battery to worry about indoors. It's your early heads-up that someone's coming up the drive.

✓

If the internet hiccups, the basics still work

The doorbell still rings inside, the driveway still dings, the garage remotes still work. The phone features come back on their own when WiFi does. Nothing here leaves you locked out.

What we looked at, and why we landed here

The choices behind the build

Every piece here beat out a real alternative. Here's what else was on the table and why this won, so you know nothing was picked at random.

Doorbell Aqara G400, over two pricier ones

The step-up Aqara G410 ($135) costs more for a narrower view that can cut off a tall person's face. The Logitech ($160-200) has a built-in night light but it's an older design, lower resolution, and can't tell a package from a person.

Went with the G400 ($100): wider top-to-toe view, spots packages, smarts built into the doorbell itself. We get the night-light benefit cheaper with the porch bulbs.

Night vision Smart bulbs, over a motion floodlight

A plain motion floodlight sounds simpler, but it turns on a beat after the camera starts recording, so the first couple seconds, the best look at a face, come out dark and then blown out as the light catches up. An always-on bulb fixes that but burns brighter than it needs to all night.

Went with smart bulbs: a soft glow holds all evening, full brightness only when someone's there. Best picture, least wasted light.

Which bulb Linkind 2-pack, after the socket surprise

Turned out the lantern uses the skinny candelabra base, not the fat standard one, and there are two sockets. That rules out most smart bulbs. Of the few that fit, one was cheaper but lower-rated.

Went with the higher-rated Linkind 2-pack. On something going in at your house, the better track record is worth a few dollars.

Garage A controller, over a cheap open/closed sensor

A bare sensor only tells you open or closed, and it needs an extra hub gadget to even talk to your phone. A controller does the same job and lets you actually close the door.

Went with the Meross controller: only a few dollars more, runs on plain WiFi with no extra hub, and can auto-close if the door's left up.

Driveway The simple metal sensor, over a fancier system

The Mighty Mule reads a car's metal, so wildlife never trips it and the plow can't kill it. A pricier magnetic system reaches farther but costs three to four times as much.

Started with the simple one. If the range comes up short on your long drive, the fancier one is the known step-up, and returns are free, so there's no risk in trying this first.

Front camera Left for later, on purpose

A doorbell covers the door. A yard camera is a separate job, and adding it now would push the price up and the simplicity down.

Skipped for this round. When you want eyes on the whole yard, it drops into the same app with no rework, mounted on the garage gable or the flagpole.

Who makes this stuff

Are these brands any good?

Short version: yes, and the privacy story is a lot better than the Ring-style cameras you see advertised.

Aqara
The doorbell

A big, established smart-home maker and the go-to affordable brand for Apple Home, sold right in Apple Stores. Because the doorbell records through Apple, the video is locked to your iCloud and even Aqara can't see it.

Meross
The garage control

One of the most popular garage-door controllers for Apple Home. Simple, well-reviewed, and it's been doing this one job for years. Nothing exotic, which is what you want on a garage.

Mighty Mule
The driveway alarm

An American driveway-gate hardware brand that's been around for decades. The FM231 is a plain, proven sensor that farms and rural driveways have run for years.

Linkind
The porch bulbs

A budget smart-bulb brand, certified to work with Apple Home. It's just a porch light, so nothing critical rides on it, but the reviews are solid.

Why not Ring or Nest?

They push a monthly fee to unlock the features, and the privacy record is worse: Ring settled with the government over employees watching customers' videos, and has handed footage to police. This setup skips all of that. The video is yours, scrambled to your account, and there's nothing to pay each month.

Putting it in

The install, start to finish

Benny handles all of it. Rough order, so you know what a visit looks like.

Doorbell

Pull the old button, connect the two wires, screw the new one on, scan its code into Apple Home. About 20 minutes.

Porch bulbs

Swap the two old bulbs for the smart ones, add them to Apple Home, set the soft-glow-then-brighten routine. A few minutes.

Garage

Plug the little box in by the opener, run two thin wires to it, stick the sensor on the door, add to Apple Home. Half an hour.

Driveway

Set the outdoor unit into the treeline beside the drive, run its cable, aim it, and plug the chime in inside. Tucked back so the mower and plow never touch it.

One thing to confirm first: that the doorbell's existing wiring is the standard low-voltage kind (8-24 volts). On a house this age it almost certainly is, and it's a 30-second check before anything gets ordered or mounted.
