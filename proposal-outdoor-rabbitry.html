Proposal #001

Outdoor Rabbitry

Full breeding infrastructure for Rabbi Yosef & Rabbi Thump. Elevated wire hutch, pin-hitch rabbit tractor, and scalable grow-out system.

Total Cost $275–$375
Difficulty 3/5
Build Time 2–3 weekends
Category Homesteading
Status Proposed

The Problem

Rabbi Yosef and Rabbi Thump currently live inside in a pen/cage combo. It works for now, but it won't scale. Once we start breeding, we'll have litters of 6-10 kits every 10 weeks, and each adult breeder needs their own separate cage. Males fight if housed together, does will have unwanted pregnancies, and kits can get injured. The indoor poop situation is already a nuisance, and it only gets worse with more rabbits.

Moving the operation outside solves all of this: waste falls through wire floors straight to the ground (free garden compost), we get unlimited expansion room, and the rabbits are actually healthier with fresh air and natural light cycles. NZ/Silver Fox crosses are hardy breeds that handle NC weather just fine.

The Plan (3 Phases)

Phase 1: Breeder Hutch $150–$200

A 3-hole elevated wire cage hutch on a pressure-treated 2x4 frame with a sloped corrugated roof. Each hole is a 30"×36" individual cage, 18" tall. Wire floors (1/2"×1" welded wire, NOT hardware cloth) let waste drop through. Cages are 24-30" off the ground for easy access and airflow underneath.

Position against the shed or on the north-shaded side of the yard. Shade in summer matters more than warmth in winter. Three holes gives us: one for Yosef, one for Thump, and one spare for a future doe or grow-outs.

Winter note: Rabbits in NC Zone 7b do NOT need a heated shed. Adults handle cold far better than heat. For winter, tack up canvas or heavy plastic on 3 sides (leave front partially open for ventilation). Stuff a nest box with straw. For newborn kits in deep cold snaps, "shelve the litter" by bringing the nest box inside between feedings. Does only nurse once or twice per day.
Summer is the real enemy. Anything above 85°F risks heat stroke. Site the hutch where it gets afternoon shade. Frozen water bottles in cages on hot days. A box fan on a timer helps too.
Phase 2: Pin-Hitch Rabbit Tractor $50–$75

A lightweight movable grazing pen that hitches to the Husqvarna's rear pin. Roughly 4'×8' rectangle, ~16" tall, with 1"×2" welded wire on all sides including the bottom (prevents digging). The bottom wire sits directly on the grass so rabbits can graze through it.

A simple tongue/drawbar bolts to the front crossbar with a pin hole sized to the Husqvarna's receiver. Drive to fresh grass, unhitch, done. Drag it to a new spot every day or two for fresh grazing and even poop distribution across the lawn.

Phase 3: Grow-Out Expansion $75–$100

As litters come, you'll need dedicated grow-out cages. Weanlings (4-6 weeks old) get separated by sex into 36"×30" cages. Plan on 2 sq ft per kit. A litter of 8 needs roughly 2-3 grow-out cages. Add these as bolt-on extensions to the existing hutch frame, or build a second shorter rack beside it.

This phase scales with your breeding pace. Start breeding → see how many kits survive → build grow-out capacity to match. Don't overbuild before you know your throughput.

Key Design Rules

Every breeder needs their own cage. No exceptions. Mature rabbits housed together means fights, injuries, and surprise litters. Bucks together will fight. Does together will stress and can injure each other's kits.

Floor wire must be 1/2"×1" welded wire. Hardware cloth (the stuff you'd use for a chicken run) is too rough on rabbit feet and causes sore hocks. The 1/2"×1" size lets droppings fall through but supports their feet properly. Add resting mats or a small plywood platform in each cage as a comfort spot.

Side/top wire is 1"×2". For cages that will house does with newborn kits, line the bottom 4-5 inches of the cage sides with 1/2"×1/2" mesh. Newborn kits are mouse-sized and can wiggle through larger openings.

Nest boxes go in 4 days before the doe is due (day 27 of a 31-day gestation). Wooden nest box, slightly larger than the doe, packed with shavings and straw. She'll pull her own belly fur to line it. Don't overthink this.

Predator Protection

Elevated cages are already the best defense. Being 24-30" off the ground with wire floors means nothing can dig in from below. The welded wire stops raccoons and dogs. For extra security: use latches that require two motions to open (raccoons can undo simple hooks), and consider burying a strip of hardware cloth around the hutch base if you see signs of digging predators.

Kong and the smaller dogs need to be introduced to the rabbitry carefully. A Saint Bernard's prey drive varies, but supervised introductions and keeping the hutch area consistent will let them normalize it. The rabbits will be above dog-nose height, which helps.

Breeding Timeline (Once Built)

Month 1 Move Yosef & Thump outside to individual cages. Let them acclimate 2-3 weeks before any breeding.
Month 2 Sex the rabbits (born Feb 1, they'll be ~4 months by June, fully sexable). If you have a buck + doe pair, first breeding attempt. Always bring the doe TO the buck's cage.
Month 3 If bred successfully: kits arrive ~31 days later. Nest box goes in on day 27. Expect 6-10 kits.
Month 4-5 Wean kits at 4-6 weeks. Separate by sex. Grow-outs to processing weight (5 lbs) by 10-12 weeks. Begin Phase 3 build if litter is large.
Month 6+ Doe can be rebred 2 weeks after weaning. Evaluate: keep best doe kit as replacement breeder? Acquire unrelated buck for genetic diversity? Scale to 2 does + 1 buck trio.

What About the Existing Indoor Setup?

Keep it. The indoor pen/cage combo becomes your nursery and emergency station. If a doe kindles during a freak cold snap, you bring the nest box inside. If a rabbit gets injured or sick, they recover indoors. If you get a new rabbit that needs quarantine before joining the herd, it goes inside first. The indoor setup never stops being useful; it just stops being the primary habitat.

Decision for Bekah

This is a $275-$375 total investment across 3 phases, spread over as many weekends as we want. Phase 1 is the only must-do to get the rabbits outside. Phase 2 (the tractor) is a quality-of-life upgrade that lets them graze and distributes fertilizer across the lawn. Phase 3 only happens once we actually start breeding and need the grow-out space.

The question is: are we ready to commit the rabbits to an outdoor life and start thinking about breeding? If yes, Phase 1 is a single weekend build.
