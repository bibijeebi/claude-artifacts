01

Airflow is the whole job

Air is the delivery vehicle for every Btu the equipment makes. Get the airflow wrong and nothing downstream can be right, no matter how good the equipment is.

Three quantities describe moving air, and they are locked together by geometry. Volume is CFM, cubic feet per minute. Velocity is FPM, feet per minute. Area is the duct cross-section in square feet. Fix any two and the third is determined.

CFM = Velocity (FPM) × Area (ft²)
Rearranged: V = CFM ÷ A · A = CFM ÷ V
Round duct area in ft², from diameter d in inches: A = π × d² ÷ 576
Rectangular duct area in ft², w and h in inches: A = (w × h) ÷ 144

That single relationship is why a duct system cannot be resized by feel. Squeeze the area and velocity climbs to carry the same volume, which raises friction, noise, and the pressure the blower has to overcome. Every one of those is a design consequence, not an installation defect.

Moving heat with air

Air carries heat two ways. Sensible heat changes the temperature you can read on a thermometer. Latent heat changes the moisture content and does not move the thermometer at all. Both take airflow to deliver, and each has its own constant.

Qsensible = 1.08 × CFM × ΔT
Qlatent = 0.68 × CFM × Δgrains
Qtotal = 4.5 × CFM × Δenthalpy
Q in Btu/hr · ΔT in °F · grains of moisture per pound of dry air · enthalpy in Btu/lb
The constants 1.08, 0.68, and 4.5 assume standard air at sea level. At altitude they shrink with air density.

The 1.08 is not magic. It is the specific heat of air (0.24 Btu/lb·°F) times the density of standard air (0.075 lb/ft³) times 60 minutes per hour. Memorize the product, understand the parts, and you can rebuild it when someone asks why Denver is different.

Worked · what is this system actually delivering?
A 3-ton split system. You measure 1,150 CFM at the air handler and a 19°F temperature drop across the evaporator coil.
Q_sensible = 1.08 × 1150 × 19
= 23,598 Btu/hr sensible
Nominal 3 ton = 36,000 Btu/hr total
Airflow per ton = 1150 ÷ 3 = 383 CFM/ton
→ 383 CFM/ton is inside the normal 350–450 band, low end. Low airflow per ton means a colder coil and more moisture removal, which is what you want in a humid climate. The 23.6 kBtu sensible is not the whole story; the rest of the capacity is going into latent, wringing water out of the air.
The 400 CFM/ton rule and when it lies 400 CFM per ton is the default design airflow for cooling. It is a starting point, not a law. Humid climates dial down toward 350 to get a colder coil and more dehumidification. Dry climates dial up toward 450 to get more sensible capacity out of the same box. Heat pumps in heating mode often want more air than cooling mode. Manual S tells you the real number for a specific piece of equipment from the manufacturer's expanded performance data.

02

Static pressure is the budget

The blower is issued a fixed pressure allowance. Everything in the air path spends some of it. Design is the act of not overspending.

Three pressures matter. Static pressure is the outward push on the duct walls, what your manometer reads. Velocity pressure is the forward push of moving air. Total pressure is the sum. In field diagnostics you live almost entirely in static, measured in inches of water column (in. w.c.).

Total External Static Pressure is the number you get by putting a probe on the return side of the air handler and another on the supply side, then adding the absolute values. That is what the blower is fighting outside its own cabinet. Most residential blowers are rated to deliver their nameplate CFM at 0.50 in. w.c. of external static. Many modern units are rated to 0.7 or 0.8, and variable-speed ECM blowers will hold CFM to higher static by drawing more watts, right up until they can't.

TESP = |supply static| + |return static|
Available Static Pressure = Blower rating − Σ(component drops)
ASP is what's left over for the duct runs themselves. It is the entire budget Manual D gets to work with.

The most common design failure in America A designer budgets 0.50 in. w.c., spends 0.35 on components, leaves 0.15 for 400 equivalent feet of duct, and gets a friction rate of 0.037. Nobody sizes duct that big, so the installer uses normal-looking duct sized around 0.10, and the finished system runs at 0.9 or 1.1 in. w.c. TESP. The blower never makes rated airflow, the coil runs cold and ices, capacity drops, and the homeowner is told they need a bigger unit. The bigger unit makes it worse. This is the single most common chain of failure you will meet in the field, and it starts on paper.
03

Psychrometrics without the chart anxiety

Air is a mixture of dry air and water vapor. Psychrometrics is the bookkeeping for that mixture. You need four properties and one ratio.

Dry-bulb temperature is what an ordinary thermometer reads. Wet-bulb temperature is what a thermometer reads with a wet sock over the bulb and air moving across it, which is lower because evaporation cools it. The gap between them is a direct measure of how dry the air is. Identical dry-bulb and wet-bulb means saturated air, 100% relative humidity, no evaporation possible.

Humidity ratio is the actual mass of water per pound of dry air, expressed in grains (7,000 grains to the pound). Unlike relative humidity, it does not change when you just heat or cool the air. That makes it the honest measure. Enthalpy is total heat content per pound of dry air, sensible plus latent in one number.

Relative humidity is the one everybody quotes and the one that misleads. It is a percentage of the maximum moisture the air could hold at that temperature. Cool the same air without removing any water and RH climbs, which is exactly why a basement at 68°F reads 70% RH while the upstairs at 78°F reads 50% with identical moisture in both.

SHR = Sensible load ÷ Total load
Sensible Heat Ratio. Typical residential: 0.70–0.80 in humid climates, 0.85–0.95 in dry ones.
An SHR of 0.75 means one quarter of the equipment's work is removing water, not lowering temperature.

What a coil actually does

Air enters the evaporator, the coil surface sits below the air's dew point, water condenses out onto the fins and runs to the pan. That is the entire dehumidification mechanism. Two things control how much of it happens:

  • Coil temperature. Colder coil, more condensation. Lower airflow makes a colder coil.
  • Runtime. A coil only removes water while it is running and wet. Short cycles never get there. This is why oversized equipment leaves a house cold and clammy.
Why you find biofilm in drain pans A properly operating coil produces liquid water continuously in cooling season, roughly 1 to 3 gallons a day in a humid climate. That water sits in a dark warm pan with organic dust as a food source. Biological growth in a drain pan is not evidence of a problem, it is evidence the system is dehumidifying. What matters is whether the pan drains, whether the trap is correct, and whether the growth stays in the pan or gets carried onto the coil face and downstream. A pan holding standing water has a drainage design or slope failure, and it is one of the highest-value findings in an inspection.
Design indoor conditions, from Manual J Heating: 70°F. Cooling: 75°F dry-bulb at 45, 50, or 55% RH, unless local code overrides. Those are the assumptions the whole load calculation rests on. A homeowner who keeps the house at 68°F in July is operating outside the design point and their system will run long and hard by construction.
04

Manual J · what the building actually needs

A load calculation asks one question: at the worst reasonable weather, how many Btu per hour must be added or removed to hold the design indoor condition? Everything else in design is downstream of that number.

Manual J is ACCA's ANSI-recognized procedure for residential loads, currently 8th edition. North Carolina has required it for residential HVAC permits statewide since 2012. It replaced rules of thumb like "500 square feet per ton," which ignore orientation, insulation, glazing, infiltration, and climate, which is to say they ignore everything that determines the answer.

The inputs that move the number

  1. Outdoor design conditions. The 1% dry-bulb for cooling, the temperature exceeded only about 1% of hours, plus the mean coincident wet-bulb. The 99% dry-bulb for heating, the temperature the location stays above 99% of hours. These come from Manual J Table 1A, not from the record high and low.
  2. Envelope. Every wall, ceiling, floor, window, and door with its area and U-value. Windows carry both conduction and solar gain, and solar gain depends on orientation and shading.
  3. Infiltration. Air leaking through the envelope, ideally from a blower door test rather than a guess. This carries both sensible and latent load.
  4. Internal gains. People, lighting, appliances. Manual J assigns standard values; each occupant is roughly 230 Btu/hr sensible and 200 latent at rest.
  5. Duct losses. Ducts in unconditioned attics or crawlspaces lose real capacity. This is a load line item, not an afterthought.
Piedmont NC design conditions, approximate Raleigh and the rest of the Piedmont sit in Climate Zone 3A, mixed-humid. Cooling design lands near 92°F dry-bulb / 76°F wet-bulb, heating design near 20°F. Use these to build intuition only. Pull the exact station values from Manual J Table 1A for any real calculation, because using nearest-city data instead of the actual station can introduce 5–10°F of error, which becomes 15–30% of load error.

Use the estimator to feel how sensitive load is to envelope and ΔT, and never to size anything.
05

Manual S · picking the box

Manual J gives you a load. Manual S turns that load into a specific model number using the manufacturer's expanded performance data, not the nameplate tonnage.

The central idea is that nominal tonnage is marketing, not capacity. A "3 ton" condenser does not produce 36,000 Btu/hr. It produces whatever the expanded performance tables say it produces at the actual outdoor design temperature, the actual indoor wet-bulb entering the coil, and the actual airflow. That number is usually lower than nominal on a hot day, and its sensible/latent split shifts with entering conditions.

The selection rules

  • Cooling: total capacity at design conditions should land between about 90% and 115% of the Manual J total cooling load. Latent capacity must meet or exceed the latent load.
  • Heat pumps: sized primarily to the cooling load in cooling-dominated climates, with a balance point calculated and supplemental heat sized to cover the gap below it.
  • Furnaces: output capacity, not input, matched to the heating load. Oversizing a furnace is common and cheap to avoid.
  • Airflow: the CFM used in the selection becomes the design CFM that Manual D must then deliver. These are not separate decisions.
Worked · why the nameplate lies
Manual J says 31,400 Btu/hr total cooling, of which 24,500 sensible, 6,900 latent. SHR = 0.78.
SHR = 24500 ÷ 31400 = 0.78
Candidate: nominal 3-ton unit. Nameplate suggests 36,000.
Expanded data at 92°F outdoor, 63°F entering wet-bulb, 1150 CFM:
total = 32,600 · sensible = 24,100 · latent = 8,500
Sizing check: 32,600 ÷ 31,400 = 104% of load
Latent check: 8,500 available vs 6,900 required ✓
→ 104% is inside the 90–115% window and latent capacity clears the requirement. The unit works. Note that the real total was 32,600, not 36,000, and that sensible came in slightly under the sensible load while total and latent both clear. Manual S is the only step that catches this, and it is the step that gets skipped most often.
The oversizing spiral Oversized cooling equipment short-cycles. Short cycles mean the coil never stays wet long enough to dehumidify, so the house hits setpoint while still feeling clammy. The occupant lowers the thermostat to compensate, which makes the cycles shorter. Energy use rises, comfort falls, and the equipment wears out on start-stop cycles. The Department of Energy has put sizing errors in the great majority of installed residential systems. When somebody tells you a house "needs a bigger unit" because it isn't comfortable, the honest first questions are what the load calculation says and what the static pressure reads.
06

Manual D · the ducts you crawl through

This is the module that maps directly onto your day. Manual D is the procedure that produced every duct you have ever put a brush into, and its whole output is one number applied to a drawing.

Manual D works backward from the pressure budget. You know the blower's rated external static. You subtract the component drops. What remains is the Available Static Pressure, and it has to cover the entire longest air path. Divide it across that path and you get the friction rate, the pressure the design is allowed to spend per 100 feet of duct.

Friction Rate = (ASP × 100) ÷ Total Effective Length
TEL = longest supply run + longest return run, each including the equivalent length of every fitting.
Target for most residential systems: 0.06 to 0.12 in. w.c. per 100 ft. Below 0.06 the duct gets impractically large; above 0.12 velocity and noise climb.

Effective length is not tape-measure length

Fittings are where the pressure actually goes. A 90° elbow is not 3 feet of duct, it behaves like 15 to 25 feet. A supply boot with a takeoff can behave like 35 to 90 feet. On a typical residential system, fitting equivalent length often exceeds straight-run length. This is why a system that "looks fine" on a floor plan chokes in reality.

Reading a system from inside it When you are in a trunk and the duct feels undersized for the tonnage it serves, you can now check rather than guess. Estimate CFM as tonnage × 400. Measure the trunk. Compute velocity. If a 3-ton system's main trunk is running 1,400 FPM in a house, the design overspent its friction budget, and you will find the evidence downstream: whistling registers, dust streaking, a blower that never makes rated airflow, and a coil that ices in July.
Flex duct is where designs go to die Flex duct's published friction values assume it is pulled taut and fully extended. Compressed to 90% of its length, its effective resistance roughly doubles. Sagging between supports adds effective elbows nobody drew. A design that penciled out at 0.09 friction rate can land at 0.20 in the attic without a single part being different from the drawing. When you find compressed flex, you have found the gap between the design and the building.

The load and the ducts get air to the room. The terminal decides whether the room is comfortable.

Four numbering systems that are not related This trips up everyone: Method 1–3 are the ACR cleanliness verification methods. Type 1–2 are dry and wet cleaning. Level 1–4 are containment levels. Condition 1–3 are mold contamination states from IICRC S520. Four independent scales, nothing in common but the digits.

Terminals: throw, spread, drop

A supply outlet is not a hole. Throw is how far the air stream travels before it slows to a terminal velocity, usually defined at 50 or 100 FPM. Spread is how wide it fans out. Drop is how far the stream falls before it reaches the occupied zone. Selecting a diffuser is choosing those three to cover the room without dumping cold air on someone's neck.

Ceiling diffusers work by the Coanda effect, where the supply jet clings to the ceiling surface and mixes with room air before it descends. Break that attachment, with an obstruction or too little velocity, and cold air dumps straight down and the room complains. That is also the mechanism behind the ceiling streaking you see around dirty diffusers.

Return sizing is where residential design cheats Supply gets designed; returns get whatever is left. A single central return in a house with closed bedroom doors starves every closed room, pressurizes it, and drives conditioned air out through the envelope while pulling unconditioned air in elsewhere. You will meet this as dusty rooms that never come to temperature, doors that whistle, and return trunks that are visibly undersized against the supply side. Transfer grilles and jumper ducts are the fix and they are a design item.

The payoff. Every finding you write up has a design ancestry. Naming it correctly is what separates an inspection report from a cleaning invoice.

The one sentence that changes how a client hears you "The system is dirty" is a cleaning pitch. "The system is dirty and here is the mechanism that made it dirty, which will make it dirty again in eighteen months unless it is addressed" is an assessment. The ACR's own position is that cleaning cannot be warranted to correct IAQ concerns or produce energy savings, precisely because those outcomes depend on design conditions that cleaning does not change. Knowing the design side is what lets you say that accurately instead of overpromising.

Where to take this next

Nothing on this page requires a certificate, and no credential exists that certifies "basic HVAC system design" specifically. What documents the knowledge is training records and demonstrated work. If you want to go further, in order of return:

  1. ACCA Manual D is the single highest-value purchase for what you do. It is the procedure behind every duct you touch. Available from ACCA's technical manuals catalog.
  2. ACCA Manual J next, if you want to argue about sizing with authority.
  3. A manometer and the habit of taking TESP on every system you enter. Measurement is what converts this reading into working knowledge, and it costs less than any course.
  4. NATE Air Distribution if you want a credential on the technician side, though it certifies service skill rather than design. Core plus one specialty, roughly $300 to $500 total, around a 70% threshold. Details at natex.org.
