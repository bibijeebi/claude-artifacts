Downloading

You are not downloading a file.
You are assembling pieces.

How .torrent files, magnet links, trackers, DHT, and a swarm of strangers move data with no server in the middle, and why you can trust any of it.

BLUF: a .torrent file is metadata describing content, chopped into hashed pieces. A magnet link is just the fingerprint of that metadata, letting you fetch the metadata itself from peers. Everything else is machinery for finding peers and keeping them honest.

The bar above is this page's swarm. Scroll to download. Pieces arrive out of order, exactly like the real thing.

handshake · the problem

One server is a bottleneck and a target

Classic downloading is a hub and spokes: one server, N clients, and the server pays for every byte N times. Popularity is punished. The thing everyone wants is the thing hardest to get.

BitTorrent inverts it. Every downloader immediately becomes an uploader of whatever it already has, so capacity grows with popularity. The catch: now you're pulling data from random strangers with zero reason to trust them. The entire protocol is an answer to two questions: how do I find these strangers, and how do I verify what they send me.

metadata · the .torrent file

A .torrent is a map, not the territory

The file itself is tiny. It contains no content, just everything needed to download and verify content: the file list with sizes, the piece length, a hash for every piece, and tracker URLs. It's written in bencoding, BitTorrent's dead-simple serialization: length-prefixed strings (4:spam), integers (i42e), lists (l…e), dictionaries (d…e) with keys in sorted order.

d ← dictionary opens
  8:announce 31:http://tracker.example/announce
  4:info d ← the part that matters
    4:name 9:ubuntu.iso
    6:length i5368709120e
    12:piece length i2097152e ← 2 MB pieces
    6:pieces 51200:▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓… ← 2560 × 20-byte SHA-1
  e
e

Multi-file torrents swap length for a files list, and here's the subtle part: content is treated as one continuous byte stream, files concatenated in order. Piece boundaries ignore file boundaries. One piece can span the tail of one file and the head of the next, which is why deselecting a file still downloads slivers of it. The client needs boundary pieces to verify the neighbors.

why sorted keysBencoding mandates sorted dictionary keys so encoding is deterministic. Same data, same bytes, every client, every time. That determinism is load-bearing, as you're about to see.
piece · hashing & verification

Trust strangers, verify everything

Content is chopped into fixed-size pieces, typically 256 KB to 4 MB, and the .torrent carries a SHA-1 hash for each. Your client downloads a piece from whoever, hashes it, and compares. Mismatch: the piece is discarded and re-fetched, and the peer that fed it to you gets flagged. This is the whole trick. You can pull from a fully hostile swarm and still land a bit-perfect copy.

On the wire, pieces are further split into 16 KB blocks. A piece only gets hashed once every block arrives, so one flipped bit throws away the whole piece. Creators balance piece size accordingly: smaller pieces mean a fatter .torrent (more hashes) but finer verification; the usual target is 1000–2000 pieces total.

have · the infohash

One hash to name them all

Take the info dictionary, exactly as bencoded, and SHA-1 it. That 20-byte digest is the infohash: the torrent's identity on the network. Not the filename, not the tracker, the infohash.

  • Trackers, comment, creation date live outside info. Edit them freely, the identity doesn't change.
  • Same content + same piece length + same file layout = same infohash, even made by different people on different tools. Swarms merge naturally.
  • Change one filename and it's a different swarm entirely. The hash covers everything inside info.

This is why bencoding's sorted keys matter: the hash is computed over exact bytes, so serialization has to be deterministic or identity falls apart.

announce · trackers

The tracker is a phone book

A tracker never touches content. Your client announces over HTTP or the cheaper UDP protocol: infohash, peer ID, port, bytes up/down/left, and an event (started, stopped, completed). The tracker replies with a re-announce interval and a peer list, usually compact format: 6 bytes per peer, 4 for IP, 2 for port. That's it. Coordination, not distribution.

The tracker also can't verify your stats, which is why private trackers tie announces to per-user passkeys, and why ratio faking is an eternal cat-and-mouse game.

choke / unchoke · peer economics

Tit-for-tat keeps the swarm honest

Connected peers exchange bitfields (which pieces each has) and have announcements as pieces complete. But nobody uploads to everyone. Each client unchokes only a handful of peers at a time, chosen by who uploads to them fastest. Feed me, I feed you. Freeload, get choked. That reciprocity is the incentive core of the whole protocol, no accounts, no reputation system, just measured bandwidth.

  • Optimistic unchoke. Every ~30 s, one random peer gets unchoked for free. That's how a brand-new peer with nothing to trade bootstraps into the economy.
  • Rarest-first selection. Your client counts piece availability across the swarm and grabs the rarest ones, keeping distribution healthy so the swarm survives seeders leaving. Exception: the very first piece is random, since a rare piece from a slow peer delays your ability to trade anything.
  • Endgame mode. The last few blocks get requested from every peer at once, first arrival wins, the rest are cancelled. Kills the classic stall on one slow peer.
find_node · the DHT

A tracker made of everyone

Mainline DHT is Kademlia. Every client is a node with a random 160-bit ID living in the same address space as infohashes. Distance between two IDs is their XOR. Each node keeps a routing table biased toward IDs near its own, so a lookup converges fast: ask the closest nodes you know about infohash X, they answer with nodes even closer, repeat. Roughly O(log n) hops across millions of nodes.

The nodes whose IDs land closest to X are the ones that store the peer list for X. No server, no single point of failure, no takedown target. Bootstrap comes from well-known entry nodes or cached peers from your last session. This is what DHT: working in your client means, and it's why public torrents survive with zero trackers.

urn:btih · magnet links

A magnet is a fingerprint, not a file

A magnet link carries the infohash and nothing of substance beyond it. No file list, no piece hashes. You can't download anything from a magnet alone; it's a claim ticket. Tap the parts:

magnet:?xt=urn:btih:c9e15763f722f23e98a29decdfae341b98d53056&dn=Cosmos+Laundromat&tr=udp%3A%2F%2Ftracker.opentrackr.org%3A1337
xt
Exact topic: the infohash. 40 hex chars (or older base32). The only part that matters, sufficient by itself on a DHT-enabled client.
dn
Display name. Pure cosmetics, shown while metadata is still in transit. Trust it exactly as much as you trust a filename from a stranger.
tr
Tracker hint. Repeatable. Gives DHT-less clients a way in and speeds up first contact. Others exist (x.pe direct peer, ws web seed, so select-only), all optional sugar.
extended · metadata exchange

Fetching the map from the territory

So how does a fingerprint become a download? Your client finds peers for the infohash via DHT, then uses the extension protocol's ut_metadata to pull the info dictionary itself from those peers, 16 KB chunks, multiple peers in parallel. Reassemble, SHA-1 it, compare against the infohash from the magnet.

Match means the metadata is byte-identical to the original .torrent, cryptographically guaranteed. You asked strangers for the map and can prove nobody redrew it. From that moment the two paths converge completely: same metadata, same download, same verification.

  • magnet → infohash in hand, nothing else
  • DHT lookup → peers who claim to have this torrent
  • ut_metadata → pull info dict in chunks, verify against infohash
  • identical state → as if you'd opened the .torrent file, proceed normally
the tradeoffMagnets need no hosting, which is why indexers moved to them under takedown pressure. But they start slower (DHT lookup + metadata fetch) and a dead swarm means nobody can even hand you the metadata. A .torrent file at least lets you queue the download and wait.
pex · the discovery stack

Redundant ways to find the same people

Peer discovery is layered, and every layer feeds the same pool: the tracker answers announces, the DHT answers lookups, PEX has connected peers gossip their peer lists to each other, and local peer discovery multicasts on your LAN. In practice, one good peer plus PEX floods you with the rest of the swarm faster than anything else.

Web seeds bolt HTTP onto the swarm: the torrent points at a plain web server hosting the same content, and clients fetch byte ranges from it like it's just another peer. Linux distros lean on this, the mirror guarantees availability while the swarm eats the bandwidth bill.

bep 52 · v2 and hybrids

The modernized protocol nobody rushed to

v1 has real warts: SHA-1 is broken enough that colliding torrents have been demonstrated, pieces spanning file boundaries is ugly, and you can't verify anything smaller than a whole piece. v2 fixes all three.

v1 · 2001

  • SHA-1 piece hashes, collisions demonstrated
  • Flat hash list, one per piece
  • Pieces span files, boundary slivers
  • Verify per piece, up to MBs wasted per bad bit
  • magnet: urn:btih:

v2 · bep 52

  • SHA-256 throughout
  • Per-file merkle trees, root hash per file
  • Files align to pieces, dedupe across torrents
  • Verify per 16 KB block, poisoning caught instantly
  • magnet: urn:btmh:

Hybrid torrents carry both structures, so one torrent has two infohashes and joins two swarms. Clients support v2 fine (libtorrent, qBittorrent); indexers and private trackers remain overwhelmingly v1. Inertia, plus the two-swarm split is genuinely annoying.

private=1 · walled gardens

Private trackers: opting out of the open swarm

Set private=1 inside the info dict and compliant clients disable DHT, PEX, and local discovery for that torrent. Because the flag lives inside info, it's part of the infohash, so stripping it forks the swarm rather than unlocking it. All peer discovery now flows through the tracker's passkeyed announce, which is what makes ratio economies enforceable.

It's also why magnets are useless there (the metadata isn't in any public DHT) and why the .torrent they hand you has your personal passkey baked into the announce URL. Share that file and you've shared your account.

seeding · recap

The whole thing in five sentences

  • Content is chopped into pieces and every piece is hashed, so strangers can't hand you garbage undetected.
  • The .torrent file is that metadata; the infohash is the SHA-1 of its info dict and serves as the torrent's global identity.
  • A magnet link is just the infohash, and peers hand you the metadata itself, verified against it.
  • Trackers, DHT, and PEX are redundant phone books, none of which ever touch content.
  • Tit-for-tat choking makes uploading rational, which is why the swarm exists at all.
