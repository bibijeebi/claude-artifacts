Proposal #017

Trailer Tailgate Stop

Weld a permanent stop so the 4x8 utility trailer tailgate folds flat to horizontal instead of swinging past level and dragging the ground. A clean, low-stakes first real MIG project.

Total Cost$0–$25
Difficulty2/5

ToolHobart Handler 140
StatusProposed

Bottom Line Up Front

The tailgate is hinged so low that folding it down swings it past level and into the dirt. The fix is to weld a hard stop that catches the gate at exactly horizontal, turning a useless drag-flap into a flat bed extension and a real load ramp surface. The simplest, strongest version is a welded stop tab on each side. A chain backup can be added for redundancy. Material is scrap-bin steel, so cost is near zero. This is a textbook first welding project: short welds, forgiving steel thickness, and a clear pass/fail test at the end.

The Problem

The trailer is a 4x8 utility trailer with a 3/4" CDX plywood floor, carriage-bolted down, pulled by the Model Y on a hitch. The rear tailgate is hinged at or below the bed floor line. When it folds down it does not stop at horizontal, it keeps rotating until the free edge hits the ground. That makes the gate useless as a bed extension and useless as a ramp, and it means the gate either stays up all the time or drags.

The goal is a gate that drops and stops dead level with the bed floor. At that point it adds usable floor length, gives a flat surface to slide lumber or gear across, and stops loads from walking off the back.

The Two Approaches

Both get welded with the Hobart Handler 140. They are not mutually exclusive, the strongest build does the stop tab as the primary and adds chains as backup.

Option A: Welded Stop Tab

A stop tab is a small piece of steel welded to the trailer frame so the underside of the tailgate, or the hinge arm, lands squarely on it when the gate reaches horizontal. The gate's weight and any load on it press down into the tab, so the weld is loaded in compression and shear, which is the strong direction for a weld. This is the primary fix.

What it looks like

Picture the gate folded out flat. Just under it, where the gate or its hinge arm passes over the trailer frame rail, you weld a chunk of steel, a short piece of angle iron or a thick flat bar, standing up off the frame. The gate swings down, lands on that chunk, and physically cannot go further. One on each side, mirrored, so the load is shared evenly.

Barney-style build steps

  1. Fold the tailgate down and prop it dead level with the bed floor. Use a board, a jack, anything. Check it with a level. This propped position is your target.
  2. With the gate held at level, look at where the gate or hinge arm crosses the trailer frame. That contact zone is where the tab goes.
  3. Cut two stop tabs from scrap: a 2 to 3 inch piece of angle iron or 1/4" flat bar works. Both sides get an identical tab.
  4. Hold a tab in position so the gate, still propped level, rests right on top of it. Mark the spot on the frame.
  5. Grind both the frame and the tab to bright bare metal at the weld zone. Paint, rust, and especially galvanizing must come off, galv coating ruins the weld and the fumes are toxic.
  6. Tack the tab in place with one short weld. Unprop the gate and test: does it land flat and level on the tab? Adjust the tack if needed.
  7. Once both tabs land the gate perfectly level, weld them out fully. Short, solid beads on the tab's contact edges.
  8. Repeat dead identical on the other side. Both tabs must contact at the same moment or one side carries everything.
  9. Knock off any spatter, wire-brush, prime and paint the bare steel so it does not rust.

Option B: Stop Chains

A chain on each side runs from an anchor on the tailgate to an anchor on the trailer frame. The chain length is set so it pulls taut exactly when the gate reaches horizontal, then it physically cannot drop further. The chain is loaded in tension. This is dead simple, and as a backup over the tabs it means a failed tab weld still cannot drop the gate to the ground.

Barney-style build steps

  1. Prop the gate dead level again, same as before.
  2. Pick an anchor point on the gate and a matching one on the trailer frame, roughly in line so the chain runs cleanly.
  3. Cut a length of chain for each side, long enough to reach both anchors with the gate at level and zero slack. Measure twice, the gate position depends on this length.
  4. Grind to bare metal at all four anchor spots.
  5. Weld a tab, a heavy washer, or a forged eye to each anchor point, gate and frame, both sides. The chain's end link will connect here.
  6. Connect the chain: weld the end links directly, or use a bolt-on quick link or shackle so the gate can still be fully removed later if wanted.
  7. Test: lower the gate, confirm both chains go taut at the same moment and the gate stops level. Adjust link count if one side is off.
  8. Prime and paint the bare welds.
Quick-link tip: if you connect the chains with bolt-on quick links instead of welding the chain solid, you keep the option to drop or fully remove the gate later. Small touch, keeps the trailer flexible.

Welding Setup & Settings

This is a genuinely good first real project on the Handler 140: short welds, no overhead or out-of-position work, and forgiving steel thickness. Trailer frame and tailgate steel is typically 1/8" to 3/16", squarely in the 140's comfort zone, the machine handles up to roughly 1/4" steel.

Practice beads on scrap of similar thickness until they are consistent before touching the trailer. A trailer carries load down a public road, so get your reps in first. Helmet, gloves, long sleeves, no welding over a propane tank or fuel.

The Pass/Fail Test

When it is done, the test is simple and obvious. Drop the tailgate. It should stop dead level with the bed floor, both sides landing at the same instant, no drag on the ground, no wobble. Stand on it near the hinge, it should feel solid. If the gate is level, stable, and the ground stays untouched, the project passed. If one side drops lower than the other, one tab or chain is off and needs adjusting.

Recommendation

Do the recommended build: welded stop tabs as the primary stop, plus stop chains as backup. The tabs carry the daily load in compression, the strong direction for a weld, and the chains mean even a failed tab weld cannot drop the gate to the dirt. Cost is essentially scrap-bin steel and a short length of chain. More importantly, it is the right size of project to get real reps on the new MIG setup, low stakes, short welds, forgiving steel, and a crystal-clear pass/fail at the end. Practice on scrap first, then make the trailer right.
