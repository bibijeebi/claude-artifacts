Locked taxonomies and conceptual architecture for WGU Principles of Financial and Managerial Accounting. Keep this open next to the cram app.

BLUF: Accounting is one equation (A = L + E), four statements that tell one story in order (IS → RE → BS → CFS), and double-entry to enforce the equation. The rest is classification.

How the Four Statements Connect

Each statement answers a different question. They feed each other in a strict order. Net Income flows from IS to RE Statement to the Balance Sheet equity section. Cash flows from the CFS plug into Balance Sheet cash. The opening BS provides beginning values.

D196 Accounting Architecture (the concepts, distilled)

BLUF: Accounting is one equation (A = L + E), four statements that tell one story, and double-entry bookkeeping that forces the equation to stay balanced. Everything else is terminology and conventions built on top. Once you see how the statements articulate (feed each other), the math stops feeling arbitrary.

The Foundation: One Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Rewrite it as: what you own = what you owe + what's left for you.

Every transaction must keep this equation balanced. That's the ENTIRE game. Double-entry bookkeeping exists solely to enforce this rule. Every journal entry has equal debits and credits so the equation never breaks.

Why it's structured that way

  • Assets are resources (cash, inventory, equipment, amounts customers owe you)
  • Liabilities are claims from outsiders (loans, AP, taxes owed)
  • Equity is the residual claim of owners after liabilities are satisfied

If a company liquidated tomorrow, you'd sell all Assets, pay all Liabilities, and Equity is whatever's left for shareholders. That's literally what the equation says.

The Four Statements (one coherent story)

Each statement answers a different question, and they feed each other in a specific order.

1. Income Statement — "How did we perform this period?"

Revenue − Expenses = Net Income

  • Covers a PERIOD (Q1, FY2025)
  • Accrual-based: revenue recorded when EARNED, expenses when INCURRED (not when cash moves)
  • Output: Net Income, which flows into Retained Earnings

Structure:

Revenue
− COGS
= Gross Profit

− Operating expenses (selling, G&A, depreciation)
= Operating Income (EBIT)

− Interest expense
= Pre-tax Income

− Tax expense
= Net Income

2. Statement of Retained Earnings — "What happened to owner profits?"

Beginning RE + Net Income − Dividends = Ending RE

This is the bridge statement. It takes Net Income from the IS and feeds Ending RE into the BS equity section. Dividends are NOT an expense. They're a direct reduction of RE (you're giving profits back to owners).

3. Balance Sheet — "What's our financial position RIGHT NOW?"

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

  • Snapshot at a POINT IN TIME (Dec 31, 2025)
  • Includes Ending RE from the RE statement
  • Output: Ending Cash, which must match the Cash Flow Statement's ending cash

4. Cash Flow Statement — "Where did cash actually go?"

Operating CF + Investing CF + Financing CF = Change in Cash

  • Covers a PERIOD
  • Reconciles why Net Income (accrual) differs from actual Cash movement
  • Beginning Cash + Δ Cash = Ending Cash (matches BS)

The articulation (how they link)

Beg Balance Sheet (Jan 1)
    Cash = $10,000
    RE = $50,000
         │
         ▼
During the year:
    Income Statement runs → Net Income = $20,000
    Dividends declared = $3,000
    Cash flows tracked on CFS → Δ Cash = +$5,000
         │
         ▼
RE Statement:
    $50,000 + $20,000 − $3,000 = $67,000 Ending RE
         │
         ▼
End Balance Sheet (Dec 31)
    Cash = $15,000 (matches CFS ending cash)
    RE = $67,000 (from RE statement)

The four statements are ONE story told four ways. A change in one forces changes in the others.

Double-Entry Mechanics: Debits and Credits

Every transaction touches at least two accounts. Total debits must equal total credits. Always.

Normal balances (which side increases the account)

Account type Increases with Decreases with
Asset Debit Credit
Expense Debit Credit
Dividend Debit Credit
Liability Credit Debit
Equity (common stock, RE) Credit Debit
Revenue Credit Debit

Memory hook: "DEAD CLIC" - Debits increase: Expenses, Assets, Dividends - Credits increase: Liabilities, Income (revenue), Capital (equity)

Why this works

Expenses and Dividends ultimately REDUCE equity (via NI or directly), so they sit on the debit side (opposite of equity's credit side). Revenue INCREASES equity, so it sits with equity on the credit side. The system is internally consistent because every account's normal side is on the side it needs to be to balance the equation.

Transaction examples

Transaction Debit (+) Credit (+) What the equation does
Buy equipment $5k cash Equipment 5k Cash 5k Asset up, Asset down (net zero)
Buy equipment $5k on credit Equipment 5k AP 5k Asset up, Liability up
Pay $2k salary Salary Exp 2k Cash 2k Expense up (equity down), Asset down
Customer pays $1k for service Cash 1k Revenue 1k Asset up, Revenue up (equity up)
Borrow $10k Cash 10k Loan Payable 10k Asset up, Liability up
Issue stock for $20k Cash 20k Common Stock 20k Asset up, Equity up
Declare $500 dividend Dividends 500 Dividends Payable 500 Equity down, Liability up
Pay the dividend Dividends Payable 500 Cash 500 Liability down, Asset down

Notice: every row has equal debit and credit totals. The equation stays balanced.

Why Net Income ≠ Cash (the heart of accrual accounting)

This is where D196 gets tricky. The Income Statement follows accrual rules, so NI can be huge while cash is negative, or vice versa.

The 5 big reasons NI and Cash diverge

  1. Revenue recognized before cash collected → Accounts Receivable increases
  2. Expense incurred before cash paid → Accounts Payable increases
  3. Cash collected before revenue earned → Unearned Revenue (a liability!)
  4. Cash paid before expense incurred → Prepaid Expense (an asset)
  5. Depreciation → expense on IS, but no cash moves (the cash moved years ago when you bought the asset)

The CFS indirect method reconciles this

Start with Net Income, then adjust for every non-cash item and every working capital change:

Net Income                              $20,000
+ Depreciation                            5,000    (no cash, add back)
+ Decrease in AR                          2,000    (collected cash)
− Increase in Inventory                  (3,000)   (cash tied up in stock)
+ Increase in AP                          1,500    (got stuff, haven't paid)
− Decrease in Unearned Rev                 (500)   (delivered the service)
= Cash from Operations                  $25,000

The universal rule for working capital adjustments

Asset up → Cash down. Asset down → Cash up. Liability up → Cash up. Liability down → Cash down.

Think of it as: if an asset account grew, cash got consumed to grow it. If a liability grew, somebody else funded your operations and your cash was preserved.

Cash Flow Statement: the three buckets

Every cash movement fits one of these three categories.

Bucket What goes here Examples
Operating Day-to-day business cash Customer payments, supplier payments, wages, taxes, interest paid, interest received
Investing Buying/selling long-term assets Buy equipment, sell land, buy/sell investments
Financing Transactions with owners and lenders Issue stock, pay dividends, borrow, repay loan principal

The two classic CFS traps

  1. Interest paid is OPERATING (not financing), under US GAAP. This feels wrong because loans are financing, but the interest portion is operating. Principal repayment IS financing.
  2. Dividends paid is FINANCING. Dividends received (from investments you own) is OPERATING. Not symmetric. Tricky.

What Goes Where: Balance Sheet Anatomy

Assets (debits normal)

Current Assets (expected to become cash within 1 year): - Cash and cash equivalents - Short-term investments - Accounts Receivable (customers owe you) - Inventory - Prepaid expenses (already paid, service still owed to you)

Long-term Assets: - PP&E (Property, Plant, Equipment) at cost - (Accumulated Depreciation) — contra-asset, reduces PP&E - = Net PP&E - Intangibles (patents, goodwill) - Long-term investments

Liabilities (credits normal)

Current Liabilities (due within 1 year): - Accounts Payable (you owe suppliers) - Short-term debt - Accrued expenses (incurred but not yet paid, like wages owed) - Unearned Revenue (cash received, service not yet delivered) - Current portion of long-term debt

Long-term Liabilities: - Long-term debt (bonds, mortgages, notes) - Deferred tax liabilities - Pension obligations

Equity (credits normal)

  • Common Stock at par value (usually tiny)
  • Additional Paid-in Capital (APIC) — everything above par when stock was issued
  • Retained Earnings — accumulated profits not paid as dividends
  • (Treasury Stock) — contra-equity, reduces equity when company buys back its own stock

Common Trip-Ups (every one of these shows up on OAs)

  1. Depreciation reduces NI but not cash. Expense on IS, added back on CFS.
  2. Dividends are NOT an expense. They don't touch the IS. They reduce RE directly.
  3. Principal loan payment is NOT an expense. It reduces a liability. Cash out = Financing.
  4. Interest expense IS an expense. Reduces NI. Cash out = Operating.
  5. Unearned revenue is a LIABILITY. You received cash, you still owe the product. Classic asset/liability flip trap.
  6. Prepaid expenses are an ASSET. You paid, they still owe you service.
  7. Accrued expenses are a LIABILITY. You incurred a cost, haven't paid yet.
  8. Issuing stock doesn't touch the IS. It's a BS + CFS Financing event only.
  9. Buying inventory doesn't immediately hit NI. It sits in inventory (Asset) until sold, then flows to COGS.
  10. AR write-off vs bad debt expense. Bad debt expense hits IS when estimated (allowance method). Write-off just reduces AR and allowance, no IS impact.

Temporary vs Permanent Accounts (the closing cycle)

At period end, you "close the books":

Temporary accounts (zeroed out, start fresh next period): - All Revenue accounts - All Expense accounts - Dividends

They get closed into Retained Earnings. That's HOW net income and dividends end up on the BS.

Permanent accounts (carry over forever): - Assets - Liabilities - Equity accounts (including the new Ending RE)

This is why the BS shows a POINT in time (accumulating balances) and the IS shows a PERIOD (flows that get zeroed out).

The Sequence of Preparation (tested directly)

Order matters because each statement depends on the previous one:

  1. Income Statement (need to know NI before RE)
  2. Statement of Retained Earnings (need End RE before BS)
  3. Balance Sheet (needs End RE from step 2)
  4. Cash Flow Statement (needs Beg/End BS values to calculate changes)

Cut rule: IS → RE → BS → CFS. If a question asks "which statement is prepared first?" it's the Income Statement. Always.

The 7 Moves That Explain 90% of Transactions

Everything you'll see on the OA is a flavor of one of these:

  1. Earn revenue on credit → AR up, Revenue up → Asset up, Equity up
  2. Collect AR → Cash up, AR down → Asset up, Asset down (net zero on totals)
  3. Incur expense on credit → Expense up (equity down), AP up (liability up)
  4. Pay AP → Cash down, AP down → Asset down, Liability down
  5. Buy asset with cash → Asset up, Asset down (swap, no net change)
  6. Borrow cash → Asset up, Liability up
  7. Issue stock → Asset up, Equity up

Memorize these seven patterns and you can decode any transaction question instantly.

D196 LOCKED TAXONOMIES

BLUF: D196 is 80% classification (which cost type, which costing method, which statement) and 20% formulas (CM, break-even, variances, ratios). Lock the taxonomies cold, memorize 6 formulas, and the OA becomes pattern-matching.

1. COST CLASSIFICATIONS (the most-tested domain)

Every cost gets classified on four orthogonal axes. A single cost has FOUR labels simultaneously. Know all four.

Axis 1: Traceability (direct vs indirect)

Type Definition Example
Direct Easily traced to one product Wood in a chair, wages of the chair-maker
Indirect Benefits multiple products, must be allocated Factory rent, supervisor salary, glue

Cut rule: "can you point at this cost and name ONE product?" → direct. Otherwise indirect.

Axis 2: Behavior (variable vs fixed vs mixed)

Type Per Unit Total
Variable Constant Changes with volume
Fixed Changes with volume Constant (within relevant range)
Mixed Both components Both components

Cut rule: "does TOTAL cost change when I make more units?" → variable. Classic trap: per-unit fixed cost LOOKS variable because it drops as volume rises. Ignore per-unit, look at total.

Axis 3: Function (product vs period)

Type Definition Examples
Product Costs to MAKE the product, sit in inventory until sold DM, DL, MOH
Period Costs of running the business this period, expensed immediately Selling, admin, marketing, CEO salary

Cut rule: "did this cost touch the factory floor?" → product. Sales and office = period.

Axis 4: Decision-relevance (this is where D196 gets you)

Type Definition Tracked in books?
Out-of-pocket Actual cash spent YES
Direct/Indirect Traceable or not YES
Sunk Already spent, can't be recovered, IRRELEVANT to future decisions YES (but ignore for decisions)
Opportunity Value of the alternative NOT chosen NO, never on the books
Differential/Incremental Difference between two options Calculated, not recorded

The "not tracked" question → OPPORTUNITY COSTS every time. Real but never recorded. You just nailed this one.

Mnemonic: "The road not taken has no toll booth." Opportunity costs are the highway you skipped. Real traffic happened on the other road, but no receipt ever hit your books.

Prime vs Conversion costs

  • Prime costs = Direct Materials + Direct Labor (the "raw inputs")
  • Conversion costs = Direct Labor + Manufacturing Overhead (what converts raw material into product)
  • DL is in BOTH. Classic trap.

Mnemonic: "Prime picks the parts, Conversion cooks the product."

2. COSTING METHODS (Job / Process / ABC)

Method Use when Classic examples
Job order Unique, custom, one-off Custom homes, law firms, hospitals, shipbuilding, wedding cakes, auditing
Process Mass identical units flowing through stages Cereal, chemicals, gasoline, paint, soft drinks, paper
ABC (Activity-Based) Complex overhead, multiple products with different resource consumption Hospital with varied procedures, multi-SKU manufacturer

Cut rules: - "Custom" or "unique" or "one-of-a-kind" → Job order - "Mass produces" or "identical units" or "continuous flow" → Process - "Multiple products" + "complex overhead" + "traditional allocation is distorting costs" → ABC

Mnemonic: Job = Jewelry (each piece custom). Process = Pasta (endless identical noodles). ABC = Airport (tons of activities, each product uses them differently).

You just nailed the cereal question. Lock that.

3. CVP (Cost-Volume-Profit) FORMULAS — memorize cold

Formula What it gives you
CM per unit = Price − VC per unit Contribution margin per unit
CM ratio = CM / Sales % of each sales dollar that covers FC
Break-even units = FC / CM per unit Units to zero profit
Break-even $ = FC / CM ratio Revenue to zero profit
Target profit units = (FC + Target Profit) / CM per unit Units to hit a profit goal
Margin of safety = Actual sales − Break-even sales Cushion before loss
Operating leverage = CM / Operating Income % NI changes per 1% sales change

Cut rule: every CVP question asks one of three things: break-even, target profit, or margin of safety. Identify which, pick the matching formula, plug in.

Common trap: sales commissions are VARIABLE (not fixed selling). Delivery is variable. Rent is fixed. Depreciation is fixed.

4. VARIANCE ANALYSIS

Materials and Labor variances

Variance Formula What it measures
Price/Rate variance (Actual Price − Std Price) × Actual Qty Did you pay too much per unit of input?
Quantity/Efficiency variance (Actual Qty − Std Qty) × Std Price Did you use too much input per output?

Favorable (F) = Actual < Standard (spent less than expected) Unfavorable (U) = Actual > Standard (spent more than expected)

Cut rule for direction: fix the sign. If actual cost exceeds standard, it's U. If actual is under standard, it's F. Applies to both price and quantity.

Mnemonic: "PAQ beats QSP." Price uses Actual Quantity. Quantity uses Standard Price. Keeps you from mixing them up.

5. MASTER BUDGET FLOW

The budget cascade — memorize the order:

Sales Budget → Production Budget → DM + DL + MOH Budgets → Ending Inventory Budget → Cost of Goods Sold Budget → Operating (S&A) Budget → Budgeted Income Statement → Cash Budget → Budgeted Balance Sheet

Mnemonic: "Sales Produces Direct Everyday Cost Overhead. Cash is Budgeted Last." Every budget flows from the sales forecast. Sales drives everything.

Cut rule: the FIRST budget prepared is ALWAYS the sales budget. The LAST one prepared is the budgeted balance sheet.

6. INVENTORY METHODS

Method Who leaves first Ending inventory Rising prices effect
FIFO Oldest stock sold first Reflects NEWEST costs Lower COGS, higher NI, higher taxes
LIFO Newest stock sold first Reflects OLDEST costs Higher COGS, lower NI, lower taxes
Weighted Avg Blended cost on every sale Avg of all units Middle ground
Specific ID Tracked individually Actual cost of remaining Used for cars, jewelry, art

Mnemonic: "FIFO First-In-First-Out, LIFO Last-In-First-Out." Straight acronyms, but visualize a conveyor belt (FIFO) vs a stack of plates (LIFO, grab from top).

Cut rule: rising prices + want lower taxes? → LIFO. Rising prices + want better-looking income statement? → FIFO.

7. DEPRECIATION METHODS

Method Formula Pattern
Straight-line (Cost − Salvage) / Useful Life Equal amount every year
Units of Production [(Cost − Salvage) / Total Units] × Units This Year Based on usage
Double-declining (DDB) 2 × (1 / Life) × Beginning Book Value Accelerated, ignore salvage until near end

DDB trap: ignore salvage value in the calculation EXCEPT don't depreciate below salvage. Book value can't drop below salvage.

Mnemonic: "Straight line is boring. DDB burns hot early. Units match the odometer."

8. FINANCIAL RATIOS

Ratio Formula Tells you
Current ratio Current Assets / Current Liabilities Short-term liquidity
Quick ratio (acid-test) (Cash + AR + Marketable Sec) / CL Liquidity WITHOUT inventory
Debt-to-equity Total Liabilities / Total Equity Leverage
Gross margin % Gross Profit / Sales Pricing power
Net margin % Net Income / Sales Bottom-line efficiency
ROA Net Income / Total Assets How well assets generate profit
ROE Net Income / Equity How well equity generates profit
Inventory turnover COGS / Avg Inventory How fast inventory moves
AR turnover Net Credit Sales / Avg AR How fast customers pay
Days sales outstanding 365 / AR Turnover Avg days to collect

Cut rule: ratio questions follow a pattern. "Liquidity" → current or quick. "Leverage/debt" → D/E. "Profit" → margin or ROA/ROE. "Speed" → turnover.

9. CAPITAL BUDGETING

Method Decision rule Ignores time value?
NPV Accept if NPV > 0 No (uses discount rate)
IRR Accept if IRR > cost of capital No
Payback period Shorter is better YES (ignores TVM, weakness)
ARR (Accounting Rate of Return) Higher is better YES (uses accounting income, not cash)

Cut rule: NPV is the gold standard — always right answer when question asks "best method" or "most theoretically sound." Payback's weakness: ignores cash after payback point + ignores TVM.

Mnemonic: "NPV Never Plays (with time) Vanilla — it discounts everything. Payback is the lazy roommate who doesn't care about tomorrow."

10. FINANCIAL STATEMENTS — classification cheat sheet

Statement What it shows Key equation
Income Statement Performance over a period Revenue − Expenses = Net Income
Balance Sheet Financial position at a point in time Assets = Liabilities + Equity
Statement of Retained Earnings Changes in RE Beg RE + NI − Dividends = End RE
Cash Flow Statement Cash in/out by activity Operating + Investing + Financing = Δ Cash

Cash flow activity classification (tested a lot)

Activity What goes here
Operating Day-to-day: AR, AP, inventory, wages, taxes, interest paid/received
Investing Long-term assets: buy/sell PP&E, buy/sell investments
Financing Money in from owners/lenders: issue stock, pay dividends, borrow, repay loans

Cut rule: "day-to-day operations" → Operating. "Buying/selling equipment or investments" → Investing. "Dealing with owners or lenders" → Financing.

TRAP: interest paid is OPERATING (not financing) under GAAP. Dividends paid is FINANCING. Don't swap these.

11. INTERNAL CONTROLS (COSO framework)

5 components: Control Environment, Risk Assessment, Control Activities, Information & Communication, Monitoring

Mnemonic: "CRCIM" doesn't stick. Use this instead: "Cool Rick Crashes Into Monsters" — image of an internal auditor named Rick crashing his truck into a wall of risk monsters. (Benny preference: mnemonics need a hook, not just letters.)

Key control activities: separation of duties, authorization, documentation, physical controls, independent verification.

12. ACCOUNTING EQUATION DRILL

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Every transaction affects at least two accounts and the equation STAYS balanced. If a question asks "what happens to equity when X?" trace through: - Revenues increase equity (via NI) - Expenses decrease equity (via NI) - Owner contributions increase equity - Dividends decrease equity

META-RULES FOR D196 SPECIFICALLY

  1. Read the verb first. "Which is NOT..." and "Which is the LEAST..." flip the answer. Miss this, miss the point.
  2. Identify the domain before picking. Every question lives in one of the 12 domains above. Name the domain, then apply its cut rule.
  3. Formula questions: write the formula BEFORE you look at answer choices. Prevents distractor anchoring.
  4. If two answers both look right, pick the broader/more formal one. (Rule A from your general playbook.)
  5. Trust your stated reasoning. If you muttered "that's opportunity cost" while reading, pick opportunity cost. Don't second-guess.

THE 6 FORMULAS TO MEMORIZE COLD

  1. CM = Sales − VC
  2. Break-even units = FC / CM per unit
  3. Price variance = (AP − SP) × AQ
  4. Quantity variance = (AQ − SQ) × SP
  5. Straight-line dep = (Cost − Salvage) / Life
  6. Current ratio = CA / CL

If you can write these 6 from memory in under 60 seconds, you own the formula half of this OA.
