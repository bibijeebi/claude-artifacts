Quick Summary

Yes, you can find sassafras right now in Willow Spring, NC. It's one of the most common understory trees in Wake County. The snow doesn't matter much for identification since the bark and twigs are your primary winter ID features.

The One-Step ID Test Scratch a twig or peel a small piece of bark. If it smells like root beer or Fruit Loops cereal, you've found sassafras. No other tree in eastern North America has this scent.

Sassafras (Sassafras albidum) grows abundantly in disturbed areas, forest edges, old fields, and roadsides throughout the NC Piedmont. You probably have some on your 0.57 acres, especially near any wooded edges.

Winter Identification

Without leaves, sassafras is still identifiable using bark, twigs, and most importantly, the smell test.

The Smell Test (Works Year-Round)

How to Perform: Scratch a young twig with your thumbnail, or gently peel a small piece of outer bark. Bring it to your nose. Sassafras has an unmistakable sweet, spicy aroma identical to root beer. If you're unsure, it's not sassafras.

Winter Visual Cues

  • Green twigs: Young twigs remain bright yellow-green even in winter
  • 60° branching: Twigs on young trees branch at a consistent 60° angle
  • Clonal groves: Look for clusters of trees of varying sizes (they share root systems)
  • Persistent fruit stalks: Red cup-like pedicels may remain on female trees
  • Leaf litter: Even under snow, sassafras leaves on the ground have that distinctive mitten shape

Spicebush Confusion

The only plant commonly confused with sassafras by smell is spicebush (Lindera benzoin), which also has aromatic twigs. However:

  • Spicebush smells peppery/spicy (like allspice), NOT like root beer
  • Spicebush is always a shrub (under 15 feet), not a tree
  • Spicebush twigs are brown, not green

Both are edible and useful. Spicebush is actually great for tea and seasoning.

Can You Dig Roots When Ground is Frozen?

The current snow/cold is actually not a major obstacle:

  • NC Piedmont soil rarely freezes more than 2-3 inches deep
  • Sassafras roots run shallow (top 12 inches)
  • Wait for a thaw day, or focus on small saplings which are easier to extract
  • Root suckers (small shoots around a mother tree) are ideal — take these instead of the main root

Harvesting Sassafras

What to Harvest

Sassafras root bark — the most flavorful part for root beer

Root bark is the traditional harvest for root beer. It has the highest concentration of aromatic oils.

Twig bark is easier to harvest and still flavorful, just use more.

Leaves are dried and ground into filé powder for gumbo (no safrole concerns).

Sustainable Harvest Technique

  1. Find a clonal grove with multiple stems
  2. Select small suckers (1-2 inch diameter) rather than the mother tree
  3. Dig around the base to expose roots
  4. Cut roots where they meet the stem, leaving the mother tree's root system intact
  5. Scrub roots clean, then peel the outer bark
  6. Dry bark pieces in a warm area for 1-2 weeks
Pro Tip: For twig bark, you don't need to dig at all. Prune small branches (pencil thickness), scrape off the outer bark, and use fresh or dried. Faster and the tree recovers easily.

Homemade Root Beer

Sassafras Root Beer Syrup

Makes ~3 cups concentrate. Mix 1-2 tbsp per pint of seltzer.

Ingredients

  • 3 oz sassafras root bark (or 4 oz twig bark)
  • 1 oz dried burdock root (optional, adds depth)
  • 1 oz sarsaparilla root (optional, traditional)
  • 1 whole star anise
  • 2 whole cloves
  • 1 tsp coriander seeds
  • 1 cinnamon stick
  • 2 tbsp molasses
  • ¼ tsp wintergreen extract (or 1 tbsp dried wintergreen)
  • 6 cups water
  • 2 cups sugar (or 1.5 cups for less sweet)

Method

  1. Chop bark into ½-inch pieces
  2. Combine water and all botanicals (except wintergreen) in a pot
  3. Bring to boil, reduce to simmer, cover for 20 minutes
  4. Add molasses, simmer 5 more minutes
  5. Remove from heat, add wintergreen, steep covered for 2 hours
  6. Strain through cheesecloth, squeezing to extract liquid
  7. Return to pot, add sugar, simmer until dissolved
  8. Cool, bottle, refrigerate (keeps 1 year)

To Serve

Mix 1-2 tablespoons syrup per 12 oz cold seltzer. Add ice. Adjust sweetness to taste.

Fermented Root Beer (Optional)

For naturally carbonated root beer, add ⅛ tsp champagne yeast or ¼ cup ginger bug to cooled (not hot) syrup diluted to serving strength. Bottle in pressure-safe bottles, leave at room temp 24-48 hours until carbonated, then refrigerate immediately to stop fermentation.

Fermentation Warning: Bottles can explode if over-fermented. Use flip-top bottles designed for pressure. Burp bottles daily. Never leave fermenting root beer unattended at room temperature for more than 48 hours.

Safety & Legal Notes

Safrole Concerns

Sassafras contains safrole, which was banned by the FDA in 1960 for commercial food use after rat studies showed liver tumors at extremely high doses. Context matters:

  • The rat studies used doses equivalent to drinking 6+ cups of strong sassafras tea daily for years
  • Occasional traditional use (a few cups of tea per month, seasonal root beer) is considered low-risk by most herbalists
  • Safrole doesn't extract well in water — tea has far less than oil
  • Many common foods contain small amounts of safrole (nutmeg, black pepper, cinnamon)
Bottom Line: Making root beer a few times a year with real sassafras is a traditional practice that continues safely. Don't drink it daily, don't give it to pregnant women, and don't use concentrated sassafras oil.

Safrole as Drug Precursor

Safrole is a DEA List I precursor chemical because it can be converted to MDMA. This affects concentrated sassafras oil, not raw bark or tea. You can legally buy, sell, and use sassafras bark. Large purchases of sassafras oil may trigger scrutiny.

Filé Powder (Leaves)

Sassafras leaves contain no safrole and are completely legal and safe. Dried, powdered leaves are used as filé in Cajun cooking — a traditional thickener for gumbo.
