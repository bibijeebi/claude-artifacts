🌿 Sassafras Field Guide

Winter Foraging & Root Beer Making | NC Piedmont & Coastal Plain

👃 The Definitive Test

🌿 → 🍺
Scratch bark or snap twig → ROOT BEER SMELL = SASSAFRAS

No smell or wrong smell = not sassafras. Works year-round, even in winter with no leaves.

This is the only test that matters. The distinctive root beer/licorice/citrus-spice aroma is unmistakable and confirms identification 100% of the time.

🌱 Leaf Identification (Summer Reference)

All three shapes appear on the same tree. This is unique among North American trees. Bright green in summer, 4-6 inches long, pale undersides.

🥚

OVAL

Unlobed, simple egg shape. No thumbs.

🧤

MITTEN

2-lobed with one "thumb." Left or right-handed.

🔱

TRIDENT

3-lobed like a ghost or fork. Most distinctive.

Fall color: Spectacular orange, red, yellow, purple. In winter, look for dried leaves on the ground near the tree to help confirm ID.

Typical habitat: Pioneer species in disturbed areas. Powerline clearances, abandoned farmland, woodland borders, fence rows. Often grows with sweetgum, dogwood, cedar, hickories. Intolerant of shade - look in sunny openings.

Frozen Ground? NC Piedmont soil rarely freezes more than 2-3" deep. Sassafras roots run shallow (top 12"). Wait for a thaw day, or target small saplings which are easier to extract. A grocery bag of roots from saplings will make a year of tea and root beer.

⛏️ Harvesting Roots

Best time: Late winter through early spring (January-February) when tree is dormant. Sap concentrated in roots = stronger flavor, less material needed. Ground must be soft enough to dig.

Tools Needed

  • Shovel or garden spade
  • Pruning shears or hand saw
  • Bucket with water (field rinsing)
  • Scrub brush (cleaning at home)

Process

  1. Find saplings (1-3 ft tall) clustered around a parent tree. These are root suckers.
  2. Confirm ID with scratch/sniff test. Must smell like root beer.
  3. Dig around sapling to expose roots. Sassafras roots are shallow and spread horizontally.
  4. Cut roots with shears. Target smaller roots (wrist-size or smaller).
  5. Field rinse in bucket to remove dirt.
  6. At home: Scrub clean, chop into ½" pieces, peel bark from root wood.
Ethics: Don't kill the parent tree. Take only what you need from saplings or side roots. A grocery bag of roots is plenty for a year of tea and root beer.

⚠️ Look-Alike: Spicebush

Spicebush (Lindera benzoin) is also aromatic and grows in similar habitats. Here's how to tell them apart:

FeatureSASSAFRAS ✓SPICEBUSH ✗
Leaves3 shapes: oval, mitten, tridentOnly oval, no lobes
SmellRoot beer / licoricePeppery / allspice
FruitBlue drupes in red cupsBright red directly on stem
SizeTree (up to 60 ft)Shrub (under 15 ft)
TwigsBright greenBrown

The smell test is definitive. Root beer smell = sassafras. Peppery/spicy smell = spicebush (also edible, but different use).

🍺 Root Beer Syrup Recipe

Non-fermented. Keeps 1 year refrigerated. Mix with seltzer to serve.

Ingredients

3 oz sassafras root bark 1 oz burdock root (optional) 1 star anise 1 whole clove 1 tsp coriander seeds 2 tbsp molasses ¼ tsp wintergreen extract 6 cups water 2 cups sugar

Method

  1. Chop sassafras root bark into small pieces (~½ inch). Chop burdock if using.
  2. Combine water, sassafras, burdock, clove, star anise, and coriander in pot.
  3. Bring to boil, then simmer covered for 15 minutes.
  4. Stir in molasses. Simmer 5 more minutes.
  5. Remove from heat. Add wintergreen extract. Cover and cool 2 hours.
  6. Strain through cheesecloth.
  7. Return to pot, add sugar, simmer 5 minutes until dissolved.
  8. Cool and bottle. Store refrigerated.
To serve: Mix 1 tablespoon syrup per 1 pint seltzer. Adjust to taste. Add ice.

⚕️ Safety Notes

Safrole: Sassafras contains safrole, banned by FDA in 1960 for commercial use after rat studies showed liver tumors at extremely high doses. Context matters:

  • Rat studies used doses equivalent to 6+ cups strong tea daily for years
  • Safrole doesn't extract well in water (tea has far less than oil)
  • Many common foods contain trace safrole (nutmeg, black pepper, cinnamon)
  • Occasional traditional use is considered low-risk
Bottom line: Making root beer a few times a year with real sassafras is a traditional practice. Don't drink it daily, don't give to pregnant women, don't use concentrated oil.

Filé powder (leaves): Sassafras leaves contain no safrole. Completely legal and safe. Ground dried leaves are used as filé in Cajun gumbo.
