Vial 5 · 1:10,000 · Silver

What is in the shot

The serum contains the same proteins that cause the allergy. In this case, dust mite, grass pollen, tree pollen, and weed pollen.

A lab collects those proteins and purifies them. Then the clinic mixes them with saline to make five strengths. The recipe is specific to one person. Nobody else can use that serum.

The full list of ingredients is short:

  • Allergen extract. The pollen and dust mite proteins.
  • Saline. Salt water. This is the diluent that makes the five strengths.
  • Glycerin and phenol. Preservatives. They keep the extract stable in the refrigerator.

There is no aluminum in it. European shots use an aluminum adjuvant. American shots do not. There is also no antihistamine and no steroid in the serum. The shot does nothing for symptoms on the day of the shot.

Vial 4 · 1:1,000 · Green

Why small doses fix a big problem

An allergic body makes an antibody called IgE against pollen. The IgE sits on mast cells. Mast cells are full of histamine.

When pollen arrives, it locks onto the IgE. The mast cell then dumps histamine. Histamine is the itch, the sneeze, the runny nose, and the swelling. This takes seconds.

Repeated small doses teach the body to make a different antibody instead. That antibody is IgG4, and allergists call it a blocking antibody. IgG4 floats free in the blood and grabs pollen first. The pollen never reaches the IgE.

The body also grows a population of regulatory T cells. These cells actively tell the allergic response to stand down. This is the part that lasts after the shots stop.

Five visits, not six months

There are two ways to climb the ladder. The old way is one shot per week for six to nine months. The fast way is called cluster. Cluster gives three shots per visit, spaced about 30 minutes apart, for five weekly visits.

Both routes end at the same red vial and the same final dose. Cluster just gets there sooner. Johns Hopkins ran both schedules side by side and counted the results.

That is the whole trade. Seven months of weekly appointments, or five weeks and a higher chance of a bad afternoon.

What a reaction looks like

There are two kinds, and the difference is only about location.

Local reaction

A red, warm, itchy lump at the injection site. It can be the size of a quarter or the size of a fist. It appears within a few hours and fades in a day.

This is normal and expected. It is not dangerous. Ice and an antihistamine are enough. Tell the clinic at the next visit, because a large lump can mean the next dose must be repeated instead of increased.

Systemic reaction

Anything that happens away from the arm. This is the one that matters. The signs are:

  • Hives or itch somewhere the needle never went. Palms, scalp, and groin often come first.
  • A cough, a wheeze, or a tight chest.
  • A tight or scratchy throat, or a hoarse voice.
  • A stuffy nose and sneezing that start suddenly.
  • Stomach cramps, nausea, or vomiting.
  • Dizziness, a fast heartbeat, or a feeling of doom.
Warning

If the throat feels tight, or breathing is hard, use the epinephrine pen first. Do not wait to see if it passes. Do not take an antihistamine instead.

Press the pen into the outer thigh. Hold it for three seconds. Then call 911, even if he feels better right away. Epinephrine can wear off before the reaction does.

Almost every systemic reaction starts within 30 minutes. That is the only reason the clinic makes him sit in the waiting room. A late reaction is possible up to a few hours later, which is why the rules below exist.

The rules for the rest of the shot day

  • No exercise for two to three hours. A faster heart rate pulls the allergen out of the arm faster.
  • No hot shower, no hot tub, no sauna. Heat does the same thing.
  • No alcohol. It widens blood vessels and it hides early symptoms.
  • No ibuprofen or naproxen on shot day.

Beta blockers are a separate problem. They blunt epinephrine, so a rescue pen works poorly on someone who takes them. Any new prescription is worth a mention to the allergist.

Vial 1 · 1:1 · Red · Maintenance

No, it is not forever

This is the part most people get wrong. Allergy shots are not a lifelong prescription. They are a course of treatment with an end date.

After the red vial, the schedule drops to one shot every two to four weeks. That continues for three to five years total. Then it stops.

3 years the minimum course. A shorter course lets the effect fade and the allergy return

Three years is the number in every international guideline, and the evidence behind it is strong. Trials that follow people after they quit show the benefit holds for at least two to three years with no further shots.

The longest follow‑up is better than that. A dust mite study checked patients ten years after the last shot and still found the effect. It only held in the people who completed three full years.

How do you prove it worked

You cannot, at least not with a lab test, and this surprises people.

A repeat skin test is the obvious idea and it does not work. In a controlled grass pollen study, treated patients had no drop in IgE and no real drop in skin test size. Their symptoms improved anyway. The bumps on the arm stay positive while the person feels fine.

Blood tests are no better. Specific IgG4 rises during treatment and it tracks the response, but no antibody level tells anyone when it is safe to stop. Researchers looking at venom allergy, where the stakes are highest, stated it plainly. There is no biomarker that can make the decision to stop.

So the real test is a calendar. Finish the course, stop the shots, and live through a full spring and a full ragweed season with no treatment. Count the antihistamines. That is the measurement.

The version with the ultrasound

There is a faster method in the research. It is called intralymphatic immunotherapy, or ILIT. A doctor uses an ultrasound probe to find a lymph node in the groin and injects the allergen directly into it.

The whole course is three shots, one month apart. The total dose is about one thousandth of what the standard shots use, because the allergen goes straight to the tissue that has to learn.

The evidence is genuinely split. Some follow‑up studies report an effect that lasts five years and longer. A review of 11 trials and 452 patients found no difference between ILIT and a placebo on any measured outcome. It is not approved in the United States and it is available only inside research studies.
