The HOA Situation

Legal Analysis — Stamey's Walk Covenants

Here's the exact language from the recorded Protective Covenants of Stamey's Walk Subdivision (Book of Maps 2005, Page 258-261, Wake County Registry), Article X — Animal Restrictions:

"No animals, livestock or poultry of any kind shall be raised, bred, or kept on any lot except no more than four of either dogs, cats or other household pets which are not dangerous or kept, bred or maintained for commercial purposes. No vicious animal or other pet may be kept that threatens or is a reasonable threat to anyone. Any household pet will be restrained or confined and not be allowed to run free beyond the lot of the owner. All confinement areas, (defined as pens and /or kennels) and/or housing will be directly behind the main dwelling and no closer than thirty feet (30') to any lot line." — Stamey's Walk Protective Covenants, Article X, Page 4 of 8 (Recorded 02/09/2005, BK011215 PG01617-01624)

Now here's why rabbits are in a completely different legal universe than poultry:

❌ Quail / Chickens

Explicitly "poultry" — the covenant bans "poultry of any kind." Quail, chickens, ducks, turkeys: all dead on arrival. No wiggle room.

✅ Rabbits

NOT poultry. NOT livestock by NC law. Rabbits are the third most popular household pet in America. The covenant explicitly allows "other household pets." That's your door.

The Legal Argument (It's Actually Strong)

Key Point #1 — The Structure of the Sentence

The covenant says "No animals, livestock or poultry... except... dogs, cats or other household pets." If "animals" meant literally ALL animals, then dogs and cats would be banned too. The exception for "other household pets" is clearly intended to allow common domestic pets beyond just dogs and cats. Rabbits are a textbook household pet by every definition that exists.

Key Point #2 — Local Law Backs You Up

Your local municipality does NOT classify rabbits as livestock. The Town of Apex ordinance explicitly states: "Livestock includes: Cows, goats, sheep, swine, and other similar animals." It then specifically lists rabbits among animals that are NOT prohibited. The Wake County Triangle area real estate guide confirms that even in neighborhoods where livestock is prohibited, rabbits can often still be raised. Rabbits are sold in pet stores, treated by companion animal vets, and recognized as domestic pets by the ARBA.

Key Point #3 — The "Commercial Purposes" Clause

The covenant says pets can't be "kept, bred or maintained for commercial purposes." Personal food production for your own family is not commercial activity, the same way growing tomatoes in your garden isn't running a farm. You're not selling meat. You're not advertising. You're feeding your household. There's no NC law that classifies personal rabbit consumption as commercial activity.

Key Point #4 — The Covenant Already Tells You HOW

The covenant itself provides rules for pet confinement areas: pens, kennels, and housing are allowed "directly behind the main dwelling" and at least 30 feet from lot lines. The covenant contemplates and permits outdoor pet enclosures. A rabbit hutch behind your house IS a pet confinement area. You're following their rules to the letter.

Strategic Recommendation

Keep your breeding trio (1 buck + 2 does = 3 rabbits, well under the 4-pet limit). House them behind the house, 30+ feet from lot lines. They're silent, odorless when maintained, and look exactly like pet rabbits because they ARE pet rabbits. They just happen to also be delicious. This is a 46-home subdivision with a Yahoo email address for the HOA. Nobody is coming with calipers. If anyone asks: "Yeah, those are our pet rabbits. They're really sweet." And that's true. They are sweet. In multiple senses of the word.

Fun Legal Fact
Many U.S. counties explicitly do NOT classify rabbits as livestock. This is why backyard rabbit operations are one of the most popular "stealth homestead" animals. They're quiet, compact, and legally ambiguous in the best possible way.

Breed Selection

Choosing Your Warrior Bunnies

Not all rabbits are built for the plate. You want commercial meat breeds with fast growth, good temperament, and proven meat-to-bone ratios. Here are the top picks for North Carolina's climate:

New Zealand White
9-12 lbs · The Standard

The AK-47 of meat rabbits. Reliable, prolific, fast-growing. Most commercial rabbit operations run NZWs. Great mothers, calm temperament, big litters. This is your default pick.


Recommended
Californian
8-11 lbs · The Runner-Up

White body with dark points (ears, nose, feet, tail). Excellent feed-to-meat conversion ratio. Cross them with NZWs for hybrid vigor that produces absolute unit kits.


Excellent Choice
Silver Fox
9-12 lbs · Heritage Breed

Beautiful silver coat, excellent meat quality, great mothers. Heritage breed on the Livestock Conservancy watch list. Bonus: gorgeous pelts if you want to tan them.


Slower Growth
Champagne d'Argent
9-12 lbs · Old School Cool

Heritage breed dating to the 1600s. Born black, develop stunning silver coat. Prized for both meat and fur. Less common but highly respected. NC breeders available.


Harder to Find
Common Beginner Mistake

DO NOT buy Flemish Giants. Yeah, they're huge (up to 20 lbs), but they have terrible feed-to-meat ratios. They eat like a damn horse and most of the weight is bone. A 12 lb New Zealand will give you more actual meat than a 20 lb Flemish Giant. Don't be seduced by size. Efficiency is king.

Pro Tip
Start with a trio of New Zealand Whites from a proven meat line. After a few litters, if you want to experiment, get a Californian buck and cross-breed. NZW × Californian crosses produce some of the best commercial meat rabbits in existence due to hybrid vigor. Bigger kits, faster growth, better survival rates.

The Breeding Program

No Champagne Required. No Lube Either. They've Got This.

Let's address the elephant in the room. Or rather, the rapidly multiplying rabbits in the room. Breeding rabbits is absurdly easy. There's a reason "breeding like rabbits" is a phrase. Here's how it works:

The Birds and the Bees (Rabbit Edition)

Rabbits don't have a heat cycle like dogs or cattle. Does are what's called "induced ovulators," meaning they ovulate in response to mating. Translation: they're basically always ready to go. No need for mood lighting, Barry White, or any of that. The buck knows what to do, and the doe is almost always receptive.

The Actual Procedure

Step 1: Take the doe TO the buck's cage. Never the other way around. Does are territorial and will fight a buck who shows up in their space. The buck is more chill on his home turf.

Step 2: Watch. The buck will mount. The entire act takes about 10-30 seconds. When successful, the buck will literally fall off sideways and sometimes let out a little grunt or squeak. That's the signal. That's it. That's the whole thing.

Step 3: Let him go again for a second round for insurance. Then return the doe to her cage.

Step 4: Mark your calendar. Gestation is exactly 28-32 days (typically 31). Put a nesting box in her cage on day 28.

The Fall-Off
The buck falling sideways is not a malfunction. This is literally how you know it worked. Rabbit breeders call it "the fall-off." If he doesn't fall off, he didn't finish. Give him a minute and let him try again. If you've ever seen it, it's genuinely one of the funniest things in animal husbandry. Dude just goes completely rigid and topples over like a furry little fainting goat.

Breeding Schedule

Here's a sustainable breeding schedule that won't burn out your does:

Event Timeline Notes
Breeding Day 0 Doe to buck's cage. Watch for the fall-off.
Palpation check Day 12-14 Feel for marble-sized lumps in the doe's belly to confirm pregnancy
Nesting box in Day 28 Fill with straw/hay. Doe will pull fur to line it.
Kindling (birth) Day 31 (avg) Usually happens at night. 6-10 kits typical.
Kits weaned Week 4-6 Move kits to grow-out pen
Rebreed doe Week 6-8 After weaning. Give her body a break.
Harvest fryers Week 8-12 4.5-5.5 lbs live weight → 2.5-3.5 lbs dressed
Key Insight

By staggering your two does (breed them 2-3 weeks apart), you'll have a nearly continuous pipeline of meat. While one doe's litter is growing out, the other is kindling a new one. It's like having two shifts at the rabbit factory.

Infrastructure & Setup

Building the Rabbitry

This is where your DIY skills come in. You've got options from dead simple to proper homestead-grade.

Housing Options

Wire Hutch (Recommended for HOA stealth) — All-wire cages are the gold standard for sanitation. Wire floor lets droppings fall through. Stack them on a simple wooden frame. Three cages minimum: one for the buck, one for each doe. Size: 30"×36"×18" for does, 24"×24"×18" for the buck. Build or buy for $30-100 per cage.
Hutch Frame — Build a simple wooden frame to hold the cages at waist height. Makes cleaning easy, keeps predators out, and looks like a normal backyard structure. Add a corrugated roof for weather protection. Position behind your house, against the fence, under shade.
Grow-Out Pen — You need somewhere to put the 7-10 kits from each litter while they grow to harvest weight. A larger wire cage or a ground-level tractor (moveable pen) works. Budget $30-100.

Processing

The Business End — From Bunny to Freezer

This is the part where some people tap out. But if you hunt (and you do), you already have the mindset and the knife skills. Processing a rabbit is simpler than field dressing a deer and takes about 10-15 minutes per animal once you've done a few.

The Process (High Level)

Dispatch — Cervical dislocation (quick neck break) is the most common humane method. Takes about 1 second. The Hopper Popper tool makes this consistent and fast. Alternatively, a pellet gun to the back of the skull works and may feel more familiar given your background.
Hang and bleed — Hang by the rear legs from a hook or gambrel. Remove the head to bleed out completely. Takes 2-3 minutes.
Skin — Make a circular cut around each rear leg at the hock. Peel the hide down like pulling off a sock. The whole skin comes off in one piece in about 60 seconds. No scalding, no plucking. WAY easier than poultry.
Eviscerate — Open the belly, remove the guts. Save the liver, kidneys, and heart if you want. Rinse with cold water.
Chill and pack — Ice bath for 1-2 hours, then vacuum seal and freeze. Or cook fresh that night.
Why Rabbits Are Easier Than Chickens
No feathers. No scalding. No plucking machine. No pin feathers. The skin peels off clean like a banana. The whole process from alive to in the cooler is 10-15 minutes per rabbit. You can process an entire litter in about 2 hours. Compare that to a chicken processing day and you'll never look back.

What to Do With the Meat

Rabbit is incredibly versatile. Young fryers (8-10 weeks) are tender enough to fry, grill, or roast just like chicken pieces. Older rabbits (12+ weeks) are perfect for stews, braises, and slow cooking. The flavor is mild, slightly sweet, and leaner than chicken breast. Here are the hits:

Young Fryers (8-10 weeks)

Pan-fried, grilled, roasted, air-fried. Treat them like chicken thighs. Season with whatever you'd put on chicken. Rabbit cacciatore is basically Italian chicken cacciatore but better.

Roasters & Stewers (12+ weeks)

Slow-cooker rabbit stew. Rabbit pot pie. Hasenpfeffer (German rabbit stew with wine and vinegar, the namesake of this operation). Smoked rabbit. Ground rabbit for burgers, sausage, or tacos.

Investment: Meat Grinder

If you get into ground rabbit (burgers, sausage, meatballs), a meat grinder is your biggest processing equipment purchase. $30 for a basic hand-crank, $150+ for a powered model. Ground rabbit mixed 80/20 with pork fat makes an elite burger. Worth it for the versatility.

Other Sourcing Options

When you buy, inspect the rabbits, double-check gender (they get sexed wrong more often than you'd think), and check teeth alignment. Bring a box or carrier.

What to Pay

Expect $20-50 per rabbit for quality breeding stock of commercial breeds (New Zealand, Californian). Heritage breeds (Silver Fox, Champagne d'Argent) run $50+. "Meat mutts" (crossbreds) can be had for $10-20 but quality varies wildly. Budget $60-150 for your initial trio. Don't cheap out on breeding stock. Bad genetics cost you way more in the long run than the $30 you saved on a discount doe.

Operational Timeline

From Zero to Freezer Meat
Week 1-2: Recon & Procurement

Build or buy hutch setup. Order/buy cages, feeders, waterers, nesting boxes. Source your breeding trio from an NC breeder. Get a bag of rabbit pellets and a bale of timothy hay.

Week 3-4: Settling In

Let rabbits acclimate to their new home for 1-2 weeks before breeding. Get them on a consistent feed schedule. Learn their personalities. The buck will start cage-pacing when he smells the does. That's normal. He's ready.

Week 5: First Breeding

Take Doe #1 to the buck's cage. Watch for the fall-off. Return her. Breed Doe #2 two weeks later for staggered litters. Mark your calendar.

Week 9-10: First Litter Born

Day 28: nesting box goes in. Day 31 (ish): 6-10 tiny, hairless, blind kits arrive. Don't panic. Don't touch them for the first few days. Mom handles everything. Check daily that they're being fed (full bellies = round, tight).

Week 14-16: Weaning

Kits move to grow-out pen. They're eating solid food and drinking water. Now they just need time and feed to pack on weight.

Week 17-21: First Harvest

The big day. Your first fryers hit 4.5-5.5 lbs live weight. Process them. You now have 15-25 lbs of fresh rabbit meat in your freezer. You are a rabbit farmer. Congratulations.

Month 6+: Continuous Production

With staggered breeding, you're now harvesting a litter roughly every 4-5 weeks. The pipeline is flowing. You have more rabbit meat than you know what to do with. Start experimenting with recipes. Consider tanning pelts. Tell nobody about your secret meat operation.

The Wisconsin Endgame

Scaling Up When the HOA Is in the Rearview

Everything you learn here translates directly to the Wisconsin homestead, but without the 4-pet limit or the need for stealth. On acreage with no HOA:

Scale Up

Go from a trio to a full colony. 5 does + 2 bucks = 150+ rabbits per year = 375+ lbs of meat. Add a second buck for genetic diversity. Build a proper rabbitry building. Start selling breeding stock to other homesteaders at $35-50/head.

Diversify

Combine rabbits with your planned chicken operation and garden. Rabbit manure goes straight onto the garden (no composting needed, it's a "cold" manure). Grow fodder and greens to supplement feed costs. True closed-loop homestead.

Revenue Potential in Wisconsin

In rural Wisconsin, you can sell rabbit meat as "pet food" for ~$9/lb without USDA inspection, sell breeding stock for $35-50/head, sell tanned pelts for $20+, and sell manure by the bucket. A well-run 10-doe operation can realistically generate $3,000-5,000/year in supplemental income while feeding your family. Not quit-your-job money, but it covers the feed bill for ALL your animals and then some.

The Big Picture
Willow Spring is training camp. Wisconsin is the show. You're learning animal husbandry, breeding management, processing skills, and feed optimization now, on a small scale, with low stakes. By the time you hit that Wisconsin acreage, you'll be operating on a completely different level than someone starting from scratch. This is the way.
