Proposal #013

openpilot Port: 2017 Accord EX Sensing

The car already has the steering, braking, and ACC hardware. Honda Sensing was just never given to comma.ai by the community for the 9th gen Accord. Reverse-engineer the custom steering CAN protocol, build the port, slap a comma four under the windshield, drive itself to work. AI-assisted from CAN dumps to Python code.

The Goal

Take a 2017 Accord EX Sensing (currently with a non-functional Honda Sensing ADAS because the front camera is uncalibrated after a windshield replacement) and turn it into a self-driving car. Specifically: install a comma four device under the rearview mirror, port the openpilot software stack to recognize and control this specific year/trim, and have the car execute lane-centering, adaptive cruise control with stop-and-go, automatic lane change, and navigation-aware highway driving. Same capability as a 2018+ Accord that came with openpilot support out of the box.

This is not a kit install. The 9th-gen Accord has never been successfully ported to openpilot. The community has tried since 2017 (literally GitHub issue #153 on the commaai/openpilot repo) and never finished. This proposal is to be the person who finally does it, using AI as the primary research tool to reverse engineer the missing CAN protocol pieces.

Why this car is the right target: The Honda Sensing camera is already broken and uncalibrated. The factory ADAS is dead either way. Either pay King's Auto Service in Raleigh ~$230 to diagnose and ~$400 to calibrate the OEM camera, or skip that entirely and replace the camera with the comma device. The broken front camera is not an obstacle, it's a green light.

What openpilot Actually Is

openpilot is an open-source SAE Level 2 driver assistance system built by comma.ai. It runs on a dedicated device that mounts under the rearview mirror, sees the road through its own camera, and sends steering torque, throttle, and brake commands to the car through CAN bus messages. It does not replace the car's safety systems. It piggybacks on the car's existing electric power steering (EPS), braking system, and adaptive cruise control hardware. If the car can be controlled electronically (and the 2017 Accord can, because it shipped with Honda Sensing), openpilot can drive it once the messages are decoded.

The software stack includes a neural network that processes the dashcam feed and predicts a driving path, a controller that turns the path into torque/brake/throttle commands, a safety layer that clamps those commands to safe limits, and a car-specific port that translates the generic commands into the specific CAN messages your car expects. The first three parts work on any supported car. The car-specific port is what's missing for the 9th-gen Accord.

Why the 9th Gen Has Never Been Ported

Honda Nidec cars (the 9th-gen Accord, older Civics, older CR-Vs, etc.) all use a standardized openpilot car interface. Steering is controlled by sending message 0xE4 on the powertrain CAN bus with a specific torque value. This works on every Honda Nidec car openpilot supports. Except the 9th-gen Accord.

The 9th-gen Accord uses a custom steering CAN protocol that differs from all other Honda Nidec vehicles. 0xE4 does not control steering on this car. Nobody in the open-source community has identified which message ID does. Developer @mvl-boston contributed a partial DBC file for the 9th-gen Accord to the sunnypilot/opendbc repo (released in sunnypilot v2026.001.000) that decodes most of the powertrain CAN traffic, but the steering control message remains an open question. Older forks by billy2kool and webnizam/nzpilot got further on some pieces but never landed a working port.

The work to do is identify the right message and torque encoding. Once that one message is figured out, the rest of the port (gas, brake, ACC, fingerprint, safety limits) is mostly mechanical work that mirrors the standard Nidec template.

The 5-Phase Plan

Phase 1: CAN Sniffing & Data Capture 2-4 weeks

Plug a Red Panda into the OBD-II port and capture every CAN message the car broadcasts. Drive in scripted scenarios: cruise control on/off, lane keep on/off, steering wheel turned manually at various torques, brake pedal pressed at various pressures, throttle pressed at various positions. Each scenario isolates the messages associated with that subsystem. Output is hours of CAN bus recordings in Cabana format.

Tools: Red Panda ($99), Cabana (free, runs in browser), comma cabana cloud upload (free). Time investment: 20-30 hours of driving with the panda attached, then weeks of offline analysis.

Phase 2: DBC File & Fingerprint Creation 4-8 weeks

A DBC file is the codebook that says "message ID 0x158 is the wheel speed, bits 0-15 are the front-left speed in 0.01 km/h." Build one for the 2017 Accord by combining @mvl-boston's existing partial DBC with new findings from Phase 1 captures. The "fingerprint" is a unique combination of message IDs that openpilot uses to identify the car automatically when the device boots up.

This is the phase where AI does the heaviest lifting. Feed Claude or GPT 5-hour CAN dumps in CSV/MAT format alongside the @mvl-boston DBC. Ask the model to identify which message IDs change when the steering wheel is turned, which change when brake is pressed, which encode wheel speed, etc. Cross-reference against the official Honda Nidec DBC from commaai/opendbc. The AI will not be perfect but it will narrow a search space of ~200 messages down to 5-10 candidates per signal.

Output: a complete 2017 Accord DBC submitted as a PR to commaai/opendbc, plus the fingerprint dictionary entry.

Phase 3: Identify the Steering Control Message 2-6 weeks (the hard part)

This is the unique 9th-gen problem. The standard 0xE4 message does not work. To find the actual steering control message, use the existing Ocelot hardware (you own 3 of these) as a CAN gateway. Set up a man-in-the-middle config where the Ocelot can inject test messages onto the EPS CAN bus while the car is parked with the ignition on. Probe message IDs in the suspected range (0xE4, 0xE5, 0x18F, 0x294, and other Honda-typical IDs) with various torque payloads and observe the steering wheel.

If the wheel twitches in response to a message, you've found it. Then characterize the encoding: torque range, sign convention, message frequency requirement, watchdog timer, fault conditions. This is dangerous work and is done with the car on jack stands or in an empty parking lot at 0 mph.

Backup approach: replicate billy2kool's older fork code (steering control via different message), see if any of his proposed message IDs cause a wheel response. Worst case, sniff the same message bus when the factory LKAS is briefly turned on (you'd need to recalibrate the factory camera first to do this, or simulate the camera with a CAN message replay).

This phase has real safety risk. Injecting CAN messages onto a live powertrain bus can cause unintended steering inputs, brake activations, or throttle commands. Always test with the car parked, ignition on but engine off, wheels off the ground or in an empty lot, and never with the car in gear. Have a kill switch (unplug the Ocelot) within reach at all times.
Phase 4: Write the Car Port 2-4 weeks

Once you have the DBC and the steering control message, the actual code is mostly mechanical. Three Python files: values.py (fingerprint, CAN message rates, steering limits), carcontroller.py (assembles outbound CAN messages from openpilot's control commands), and carstate.py (parses inbound CAN messages into openpilot's state struct).

Use the existing honda_nidec car port as the template. Most of the file content is shared across all Honda Nidec cars. Substitute in the 2017 Accord steering message ID and encoding. Set conservative torque limits (Honda EPS hardware enforces ~3.5 Nm peak anyway, but software limit should be 2.5 Nm to start). Set the lateral acceleration limit to 1.8 m/s² (Honda Nidec standard).

AI lifts heavy here too: feed Claude the existing honda_nidec files plus your phase-3 findings, ask it to generate the modified files for the 2017 Accord. Review every line, but expect the model to do 80%+ of the code correctly on first pass.

Phase 5: Bench Test, Jack Stands, Then Drive 2-4 weeks

Test in escalating tiers. First, with car parked and ignition on, verify openpilot reads all sensors correctly (speed, steering angle, brake pressure, throttle, gear). Then with car on jack stands, engage openpilot at 0 mph and command small steering torques. Confirm wheel responds correctly, fault conditions trigger correctly, kill switch works.

Then empty parking lot at 5-10 mph, monitor steering behavior. Then low-traffic suburban roads at 25 mph with hand hovering over the wheel. Then highway at 45-55 mph with full attention. Each tier exposes different failure modes. Most failures at this stage are tuning issues (wheel oscillates, overcorrects on curves, drifts on crowned roads), not safety issues.

Finally, submit the port as a PR to sunnypilot first (which is more permissive with experimental ports), then upstream to commaai/openpilot once it has thousands of road-test miles logged. Community wins.

AI as Force Multiplier

This project is the most ambitious AI-assisted reverse engineering project on the table for a normal person right now. Three places where Claude or GPT meaningfully accelerate the work:

1. CAN Message Identification

Feed an AI a 30-minute CAN dump in CSV format along with a synchronized log of driving actions ("steering wheel turned right at 15 seconds, brake pressed at 22 seconds"). Ask it to correlate message changes with actions. The AI cannot perfectly identify every signal, but it can narrow a 200-message search space to 5-10 candidates per signal. Repeat per subsystem.

2. DBC Generation

Once message IDs are identified, the AI can generate the DBC file syntax mechanically. Provide the existing Honda Nidec DBC as a template, the new signal mappings, and the AI writes the DBC. Saves hours of manual byte-position math.

3. Python Port Code

The carcontroller.py, carstate.py, and values.py files for the 2017 Accord can be generated by AI given the existing honda_nidec template plus the new signal mappings. Expect 80% correct on first pass, then iterate. This used to be days of work, now it's a few hours of careful prompting and review.

What AI cannot do: validate that the port is safe to use on a real car at highway speeds. That part is still human work with a real car and a real risk-of-injury. AI is a research accelerator, not a safety engineer.

Risks & Mitigations

Bricking the EPS module
Injecting malformed CAN messages can put the EPS in a fault state requiring dealer reset. Mitigation: only inject during Phase 3 testing on jack stands, with the OEM EPS firmware untouched. Honda dealers can clear fault codes via HDS for ~$120.
Accident liability
No insurance company covers openpilot use. Any accident while engaged is on you. Mitigation: keep hands on wheel always, treat openpilot as Tesla Autopilot equivalent (assistance, not autonomy), and disengage on weather/traffic uncertainty. Drive defensively.
Project time blowout
3-9 months is optimistic. Could easily be 18 months between life, work, and unrelated rabbit holes. Mitigation: set a hard checkpoint at Phase 3. If the steering message hasn't been identified after 3 months of dedicated effort, declare the port infeasible and revert to using the 2017 Accord as a regular car. Sell the comma four to a 10th-gen Accord owner. Recoup most of the spend.
Honda dealer warranty drama
2017 Accord is out of factory warranty. Any extended warranty might be voided by aftermarket CAN injection. Mitigation: remove the comma four and Red Panda before any dealer visit. The OBD-II and harness leave no permanent modification.
Camera calibration loss
The factory Honda Sensing camera is already uncalibrated. If you decide later to pay the ~$400 for King's Auto Service to recalibrate it (in case you want to sell the car with working ADAS), do that BEFORE removing the camera and installing the comma device. Otherwise, the camera stays out and the comma device replaces it.

The First Move

Buy a Red Panda. $99 on comma.ai. Plugs into the OBD-II port. Comes with cable and Cabana software. Don't buy anything else yet.

Drive the Accord for 2-3 weeks with the panda plugged in, doing scripted scenarios (highway with cruise on, parking lot with steering wheel turns, brake pressing sequences, etc.). Upload captures to Cabana's free cloud tier. By the end of the first month, you have hours of CAN data to analyze with AI.

Concurrent: pull @mvl-boston's DBC from the sunnypilot/opendbc repo. Study it. Join the comma.ai Discord. Lurk in the Honda channel for two weeks. Read the existing honda_nidec.dbc and the carcontroller.py code for a supported Nidec car (2016 Civic is a clean reference).

By month 2, you know the codebase, you have data, and you can start Phase 2 (AI-assisted message ID work). Total spend at that point: $99 and 60 hours.

Decision Point

This is the most ambitious project on the proposals board. Difficulty 5/5. Time horizon 3-9 months minimum. Real safety risk in Phase 3 if not done carefully. Real chance the project dies at Phase 3 with the steering message never identified, and the time is sunk.

But it's also the single project on the board with the highest potential upside. A working 9th-gen Accord openpilot port:

  • Makes the daily commute hands-off on the highway
  • Adds adaptive cruise control with stop-and-go (currently broken with the uncalibrated camera anyway)
  • Replaces the broken Honda Sensing system with something far more capable
  • Contributes a port to the open-source community that thousands of 9th-gen Accord owners would use
  • Is the most legitimate AI-assisted reverse engineering project a normal person can attempt in 2026

Phase 1 (Red Panda + data capture) is cheap, reversible, and educational regardless of whether the full project finishes. Even if the port never lands, the CAN sniffing knowledge transfers to any future automotive project. The $99 first step is hard to argue against.
