SEG 01

The refrigeration cycle

par 0:30

A/C doesn't make cold. It moves heat, using a refrigerant's phase changes as the vehicle. Four components, one loop, and every pressure/temp question falls out of it.

Start at the compressor and ride the loop:

  • Compressor — takes low-pressure superheated vapor, squeezes it into high-pressure hot vapor. Only place work is added. Vapor only: liquid entering a compressor is slugging and destroys it.
  • Condenser — hot-side coil (front of the car, ahead of the radiator). Hot vapor rejects heat to ambient air and condenses to high-pressure liquid. Heat leaves the system here.
  • Metering device — TXV or orifice tube. High-pressure liquid forced through a restriction, pressure collapses, part of it flash-boils, and what exits is a cold low-pressure liquid/vapor mist. No heat moves here; it just sets up the evaporator.
  • Evaporator — cold-side coil in the dash. Cold refrigerant absorbs cabin heat and boils to low-pressure vapor. Heat enters the system here. Back to the compressor.
Red = high side (compressor discharge → metering inlet). Blue = low side (metering outlet → compressor suction).

Flash gas, the concept people fumble

On the high side you have liquid at ~180 psig and 110°F; at that pressure it boils at ~125°F, so it's stable. Squeeze it through the orifice to 30 psig, where the boiling point is ~35°F, and it's suddenly 75°F above its boiling point. Part of it boils instantly. That boiling steals latent heat from the rest of the liquid, chilling it to the new saturation temp. Roughly 20-25% flashes; that vapor is dead weight, only the surviving liquid absorbs cabin heat. Colder liquid in = less flash = more capacity, which is why subcooling matters.

Anchors

  • Pressure and temperature are locked together at saturation. That's Segment 3's whole job.
  • Superheat proves no liquid reaches the compressor. Subcooling proves solid liquid feeds the metering device. That's Segment 4.
  • Low side big fitting on the suction line, high side small fitting on the liquid line, sized differently so you can't cross-connect.

SEG 02

Refrigerants

par 0:40

Family name tells you the halogens, the halogens tell you the environmental sin, the sin tells you the regulation. CFC → HCFC → HFC → HFO is the whole industry arc.

FamilyChemistryExamplesProblemStatus
CFCC + Cl + F, no hydrogen, ultra-stableR-12, R-11, R-502Chlorine reaches stratosphere, shreds ozone (~100k molecules per Cl atom)Production banned 1996
HCFCAdds H, breaks down lower in atmosphereR-22Still some chlorineProduction banned 2020, service from reclaim only
HFCNo chlorine at allR-134a, R-410A, R-404AZero ODP but potent greenhouse gasAIM Act phasedown in progress
HFOHFC with a C=C double bond, falls apart in daysR-1234yfMildly flammable (A2L)Current MVAC standard

The two report cards

ODP (ozone depletion potential): R-11 = 1.0 by definition. R-12 ≈ 1.0, R-22 ≈ 0.055, anything chlorine-free = 0. GWP (global warming potential): CO₂ = 1. R-12 ≈ 10,900. R-134a ≈ 1,430. R-410A ≈ 2,088. R-1234yf ≈ 4. The yf number is why every new car uses it: same pressures as R-134a (within a few psi), 99.7% less warming, achieved by an atmospheric lifetime of ~11 days vs ~14 years.

Safety classes

Letter = toxicity (A low, B high). Number = flammability (1 none, 2L mildly flammable with low burn velocity, 2 flammable, 3 very flammable). R-134a = A1. R-1234yf = A2L. R-410A = A1. R-32 = A2L. R-290 (propane) = A3. R-717 (ammonia) = B2L. The whole industry is drifting to A2L, dragging new leak sensors, handling rules, and machine specs with it.

Numbering shortcuts

  • 400-series = zeotropic blends. Components have different boiling points, so they fractionate: vapor leaks change the mix, and you must charge as liquid. They have temperature glide. R-410A = 50/50 R-32 + R-125.
  • 500-series = azeotropic blends, behave as a single compound.
  • 600/700-series = organics and naturals. R-600a isobutane, R-717 ammonia, R-744 CO₂, R-290 propane.

Oil, the silent killer topic

Mineral oil worked with CFC/HCFC. HFCs won't carry it, they need PAG (automotive) or POE (stationary). Both are hygroscopic, they pull moisture straight out of the air, and moisture + POE = acid = dead compressor. That's why open systems get sealed fast, driers get replaced every time a system is opened, and deep vacuum exists. MVAC specifics: R-134a uses PAG, R-1234yf uses its own PAG spec, and hybrid/EV electric compressors use POE because PAG conducts, and a conductive oil film in a high-voltage compressor is an isolation fault waiting to happen. Cross-contaminate PAG into an electric compressor system and you can total the compressor and trip HV isolation faults.

MVAC timeline

R-12 until ~1994 → R-134a from ~1994 → R-1234yf phasing in from 2015, essentially all new vehicles by 2021. Every transition changed the service fittings on purpose so the wrong machine physically can't connect. Retrofits require new fittings and an under-hood label.

SEG 03

PT charts & gauges

par 0:40

At saturation, pressure and temperature are the same fact wearing two hats. The PT chart is the translator, and your gauges are just PT chart hardware.

Pressure vocabulary

  • psig = gauge pressure, zero at atmospheric. What your gauges read.
  • psia = absolute. psia = psig + 14.7. Physics happens in absolute.
  • Below 0 psig, we switch units to inches of mercury vacuum (inHg). Perfect vacuum ≈ 29.92 inHg. Deep-vacuum work switches to microns (Segment 6).

Manifold anatomy

Blue = low side, a compound gauge (reads vacuum AND pressure, because the low side can pull into vacuum). Red = high side, pressure only. Center yellow hose goes to the machine, vacuum pump, or jug. Both hand valves closed = you're just reading; open a valve and the center port is connected to that side. The gauge faces have PT scales printed on the dial rings for common refrigerants: the needle is literally answering "what temperature is my saturated refrigerant" in real time.

What the chart is for

  • Saturation only. The PT relationship holds only where liquid and vapor coexist: inside the evaporator, inside the condenser, inside a jug that has both phases. Fully superheated vapor or fully subcooled liquid are off the chart, that's the whole trick behind Segment 4.
  • Static pressure check. Engine off, system rested: gauge pressure should match the PT value for ambient temp. 85°F day, R-134a should sit ~92 psig static. Way low = you've lost charge, near zero = it's empty and air is probably in it.
  • Jug ID. A mystery jug at a known temp has a known pressure signature per refrigerant. (Shops use identifier machines for legal certainty, but PT gets you a strong prior.)
  • Running targets. MVAC rules of thumb at ~1,500 rpm, doors open, A/C max: low side ~25-40 psig, high side ≈ 2.2-2.5 × ambient °F (95°F day → ~210-240 psig on R-134a). These are sanity bands, not gospel; the vehicle's service data wins.

SEG 04

Superheat & subcooling

par 0:45

Two numbers diagnose 80% of charge problems. Superheat says what's happening at the evaporator exit, subcooling says what's happening at the condenser exit.

Definitions, cold

  • Superheat (SH) = actual suction line temp − saturation temp for the low-side pressure. Measured at the evaporator outlet. It exists only in vapor. Some SH is mandatory: it proves every drop of liquid boiled off before the compressor.
  • Subcooling (SC) = saturation temp for the high-side pressure − actual liquid line temp. Measured at the condenser outlet. It exists only in liquid. Some SC is mandatory: it proves a solid liquid column feeds the metering device (no bubbles, no flash gas upstream).

Procedure: clamp a thermocouple on the line, read the matching gauge, convert pressure → saturation temp on the PT chart, subtract. That's it. The entire mystique of "checking the charge" is two subtraction problems.

The four-corner logic

ReadingMeaningUsual suspects
High SHEvaporator starved, refrigerant finished boiling too earlyUndercharge, restriction, TXV underfeeding
Low / zero SHEvaporator flooded, liquid surviving to the suction lineOvercharge, TXV overfeeding, low airflow over evap
High SCLiquid backing up in the condenserOvercharge, restriction downstream (liquid line / metering)
Low SCNot enough liquid formedUndercharge, huge heat load, condenser airflow problems

Combos are the diagnosis: high SH + low SC = undercharge. Low SH + high SC = overcharge. High SH + high SC = restriction (liquid piles up behind the blockage, evaporator starves ahead of it). Burn those three into memory.

Which number to charge by

  • TXV system → charge by subcooling. The TXV actively holds superheat constant, so SH stops responding to charge; SC becomes your signal. Residential targets typically ~10-12°F SC (nameplate wins).
  • Fixed orifice → charge by superheat. Nothing regulates flow, so SH swings with charge and load. Stationary side uses target-superheat tables (indoor wet bulb × outdoor dry bulb).
  • MVAC → charge by weight, always. The under-hood label gives grams/ounces; a J2788 machine delivers ±0.5 oz. Cars are too small and too variable-speed to charge by feel; gauges and SH/SC are for diagnosis, the scale is for charging. A 10% undercharge on a 1.5 lb system is a visible performance hit.

⌘ SH / SC Calculator

Default numbers above: run them. You should get ~14°F SH and ~31°F SC on R-134a, and the verdict should smell like a restriction/overcharge pattern. Then plug in a healthy set (30 psig / 42°F suction, 225 psig / 128°F liquid) and watch it normalize to ~8°F SH, ~11°F SC.

SEG 05

MVAC architectures

par 0:40

Every car is one of two layouts: orifice tube + accumulator, or TXV + receiver-drier. Learn which side of the loop the tank sits on and you can't confuse them.

CCOT (orifice) systemTXV system
MeteringFixed orifice tube (color-coded plastic, in the liquid line near the evap or condenser outlet)Thermostatic expansion valve at the evaporator, modulates by sensing outlet temp/pressure
Storage/protectionAccumulator on the LOW side (suction line at evap outlet). Catches liquid before the compressor, holds desiccantReceiver-drier on the HIGH side (liquid line at condenser outlet). Stores liquid, holds desiccant, may have sight glass
Flow controlNone at the orifice, so the compressor clutch cycles on a low-pressure switch (CCOT = clutch-cycling orifice tube)TXV holds superheat, compressor can run continuously
TellFat canister on the suction line, cold to the touchSlim can near the condenser, block valve at the evap firewall

Mnemonic: O-A-L, Orifice gets an Accumulator on the Low side. TXV gets the other tank on the other side.

Compressors

  • Fixed displacement + clutch: pumps a fixed volume per rev, capacity controlled by cycling the electromagnetic clutch. The AC "click" you hear.
  • Variable displacement: swash plate changes angle to vary stroke, runs continuously, smoother. Many are externally controlled by the ECM via a solenoid valve, no clutch at all.
  • Electric (hybrids/EVs): high-voltage sealed scroll compressor, its own inverter, POE oil only. PAG conducts; contaminating an HV compressor with PAG risks isolation faults. Dedicated or scrupulously flushed equipment only.

R-1234yf system differences

  • Unique service fittings (again, deliberate incompatibility).
  • Machines must meet SAE J2843 (vs J2788 for R-134a): they leak-check the vehicle before recharging and refuse to charge a leaking system, because the refrigerant is A2L and costs 5-10x more.
  • Many systems add an internal heat exchanger (IHX) between suction and liquid lines to claw back efficiency.
  • Some vehicles run evap-area sensors and design rules to keep any burning yf byproducts (HF, nasty) out of the cabin.

Other layouts you'll meet

Rear/dual-evap systems (minivans, SUVs, your work vans): two evaporators, one condenser, more charge, more line length, more leak points at the rear connections. EV heat pumps: same loop with a reversing strategy so the "evaporator" and "condenser" swap roles for cabin heat; resistive heat is the fallback. Condensers with integrated receiver + subcool loop are the modern norm: the last passes re-subcool the liquid after the receiver for exactly the flash-gas reasons from Segment 1.

The safety switches

A low-pressure cutoff (~25 psig) keeps the compressor from running on a starved system, no refrigerant means no oil circulation. A high-pressure cutoff (~375-425+ psig) protects against blockage/overcharge/fan failure, and a mid-range pressure transducer stages the condenser fans. Modern cars replace discrete switches with one transducer feeding the ECM. When your clutch short-cycles every few seconds, it's almost always the low-pressure switch doing its job on an undercharged system.

SEG 06

Service procedures

par 0:45

Recover → repair → evacuate → charge by weight. Every step exists to keep three enemies out: air, moisture, and the atmosphere out of your refrigerant.

1. Recover

Never open a charged system. Machine pulls the charge into a tank, filtering as it goes. SAE J2788 (R-134a) machines must recover 95%+ of the charge in 30 minutes and recharge to ±0.5 oz accuracy; J2843 is the yf equivalent with mandatory pre-charge leak checks. "Recycle" = the machine's on-board filtration for same-shop reuse. "Reclaim" = sending it off-site to be reprocessed to like-new purity spec (AHRI 700). MVAC shops recycle on the machine daily; badly contaminated refrigerant gets sent out for reclamation or destruction.

Never mix refrigerants. Cross-contaminated refrigerant can be unrecyclable, can wreck the recovery tank's whole contents, and sequential contamination can trash the machine. Shops use refrigerant identifiers on unknown vehicles before hooking up, one DIY "stop leak + mystery gas" car can cost a shop thousands.

2. Evacuate, and why microns

Vacuum doesn't "suck out air" so much as it boils water. At 29.92 inHg-ish absolute vacuum levels, water boils at room temperature and leaves as vapor. Gauge needles are useless down there, so we switch to microns (1 inHg ≈ 25,400 microns; atmosphere ≈ 760,000). Stationary standard: pull to 500 microns or below, measured with an electronic micron gauge at the system (not at the pump). MVAC practice: pull ~29.5 inHg for 30+ minutes minimum, longer if the system sat open, and the machine automates it.

Vacuum decay test: valve off the pump, watch the micron gauge. Rises and keeps climbing past ~1,000+ = leak. Rises then plateaus = moisture still boiling off, keep pulling. Holds = tight and dry. This is the cheapest leak test you'll ever run.

3. Charge

  • By weight, from the label. Scale or machine meters the exact grams. Blends and liquid-charging: jug inverted or dip-tube so liquid leaves the jug (Segment 2's fractionation), metered carefully into the high side or throttled into the low side while running, never dump liquid into the suction of a running compressor.
  • Oil balance: measure what came out during recovery/component replacement, put the same amount of the correct oil back. New condenser/accumulator instructions specify per-component ounces. Too little oil kills the compressor; too much coats the heat exchangers and kills capacity.
  • Nitrogen is your friend between recover and evacuate: sweep/pressurize with dry nitrogen to push moisture out and leak-test (Segment 7). Nitrogen is inert and cheap. Never pressurize with compressed air or oxygen: oil + oxygen + pressure is a genuine explosion mechanism, and air is wet.

Air in the system: the symptom

Air is a non-condensable. It collects in the condenser, can't condense, and squats on head pressure: signature is high high-side with mediocre cooling and noisy/hot operation, plus static pressure that reads above the PT value for ambient. Fix is recover, evacuate properly, recharge. On the 609 exam, "excess air" answers cluster around exactly this.

SEG 07

Leak detection

par 0:30

Refrigerant is heavier than air and leaks live at the bottom of fittings. Probe low, probe slow, and know which tool finds which leak.

Where cars leak, ranked

  • Compressor shaft seal — #1. Spinning seal, engine heat, sits unused all winter and dries out.
  • Condenser — front of the car, eats rocks and road debris.
  • Evaporator core — worst-case labor (dash out); electronic detection via the drain tube or a borescope through a vent.
  • Hose crimps, o-rings, Schrader valves, pressure switch seals — everything with rubber, aged by heat cycles.

The toolkit

MethodBest forReality check
Electronic sniffer (heated diode / infrared)Pinpointing active leaks, evap cores via drainMove ~1 in/sec, bottom of joints, no wind, no shop chemicals nearby. SAE-spec detectors resolve fractions of an oz/yr. Heated diode = sensitive but poisons over time; infrared = long-lived.
UV dyeSlow seepers, comes-back-next-summer leaksMany cars have factory dye already. Correct dose only, dye is oil, overdosing is overcharging oil. Glows at the leak under UV + yellow glasses.
Nitrogen pressure testEmpty/open systems, proof-testing repairsRegulated dry nitrogen, pressure per spec, listen + soap the joints. Big leaks announce themselves audibly.
Soap bubblesAccessible fittings, dirt cheapStill legitimately effective. Bubbles don't lie.
Vacuum decayConfirming tightness before chargingFinds that a leak exists, not where. Micron gauge rise pattern separates leak from moisture (Segment 6).

Yf machines (J2843) run their own leak check before they'll recharge. That's the regulation absorbing the lesson: don't feed refrigerant to a leaking system.

Stationary-side legal layer (608 flavor)

For appliances above the size threshold, EPA leak-rate rules kick in: calculated annualized leak rate above 10% (comfort cooling), 20% (commercial refrigeration), 30% (industrial process) triggers mandatory repair/retrofit/retirement timelines and record keeping. The 2024-25 AIM Act rules extend leak-repair obligations to HFC systems with 15+ lbs of charge. Cars have no leak-rate mandate; the yf machine's refuse-to-charge behavior is the MVAC equivalent.

SEG 08

The law: 609 & 608

par 0:40

One prohibition (never vent), one credential per domain (609 mobile, 608 stationary), one paper trail. This segment is most of the actual exam.

The venting prohibition

Clean Air Act §608(c): knowingly venting refrigerant during service, maintenance, repair, or disposal has been illegal since July 1, 1992 for ozone-depleting refrigerants, and since November 15, 1995 for substitutes (HFCs, HFOs). De minimis releases, the wisps that escape despite good-faith recovery with approved equipment (hose disconnects, gauge purges), are not violations. Deliberate venting is, and the classic penalty framing is fines up to the tens of thousands of dollars per violation, per day (inflation-adjusted, currently north of $40k), plus a CAA award of up to $10,000 for informants whose tips lead to conviction. Yes, snitches get paid.

609 in one box

  • Required since January 1993 for anyone servicing MVACs for consideration (money or barter). Certification is lifetime, no renewal.
  • MVAC = vapor-compression A/C cooling the driver/passenger compartment of a motor vehicle. MVAC-like = same tech on off-road/ag/construction equipment cabs, also serviced under 609.
  • Not 609: refrigerated cargo boxes (reefer trailers) and passenger bus A/C running R-22, those are stationary-style systems under 608.
  • Test: 25 questions, open book, 84% (21/25) to pass. Providers: MACS, Mainstream Engineering (epatest.com), SkillCat, ESCO.
  • Equipment must be EPA-approved/SAE-spec (J2788 for R-134a, J2843 for yf), and shops certify equipment ownership to EPA.

Sales restrictions, the fiddly part

  • Small cans of R-134a (under 2 lbs) may be sold to uncertified DIYers, federal rule, but cans manufactured after Jan 1, 2018 must have a self-sealing valve. (Some states go further; California restricts DIY cans.)
  • Containers 2 lbs and over: 609 (or 608) certification required to purchase. Your 609 card is literally a refrigerant-buying license.
  • R-12 sales have been certified-tech-only since November 14, 1994.
  • Retailers/wholesalers keep purchase records; shops keep tech certifications on file. Records generally retained 3 years.

608 in one box (you hold Universal, keep it warm)

  • Type I small appliances (≤5 lbs, factory-sealed), Type II high-pressure (splits, heat pumps, R-410A world), Type III low-pressure (centrifugal chillers), Universal = all three. Core + type exams, closed book except Type I online option.
  • Recovery equipment must be EPA-certified; systems must be evacuated to specified levels before opening; appliances must have refrigerant recovered before disposal, with documentation.
  • AIM Act (2020): HFC production/import phasedown, 40% cut in 2024 stepping to 85% by 2036, allowance system, refillable-cylinder and tracking rules incoming, tech-transition GWP limits (new residential AC ≤700 GWP from 2025, hello R-454B and R-32). This is why R-410A prices are climbing the R-22 staircase.

Mindset for the exam

When in doubt, the correct answer is the one where refrigerant never touches the atmosphere, records exist, and the machine has an SAE number. Distractors are usually "vent it a little," "top it off without finding the leak," or "any gauge set works."

SEG 09

Electrical for HVAC

par 0:45

The refrigerant loop is half the machine. The other half is a low-voltage brain switching a high-voltage muscle, and it's where most service calls actually live.

Residential control chain (24V world)

A transformer steps 120/240V down to 24VAC control power. The thermostat is just a switch panel: R = 24V hot, C = common (return path), Y = cooling call, G = fan, W = heat. Close R-to-Y and 24V energizes the contactor coil outside; the contactor's contacts slam shut and put 240V across the compressor and condenser fan. Close R-to-G and the furnace board runs the blower. That's the whole sequence of operation for cooling: stat calls → contactor pulls in → compressor + fans run → stat satisfied → contactor drops.

Capacitors, the #1 part you'll ever replace

  • Run capacitor: stays in circuit, phase-shifts the start winding to keep single-phase motors turning efficiently. Rated in microfarads (µF) and voltage. Dual round caps serve compressor + fan: terminals C (common), HERM (compressor), FAN.
  • Test with a meter's capacitance mode, out of circuit, discharged. Replace when reading falls outside ±6% of rating. Symptoms: humming motor that won't start, fan that needs a stick-push, breaker trips.
  • Start capacitor/hard-start kit: high µF, in circuit only for the first fraction of a second via a potential relay.
  • Discharge before touching. Insulated screwdriver across terminals is the field method; a resistor is the polite method.

MVAC electrical chain

Button press → HVAC control head requests → ECM/BCM sanity-checks: pressure transducer in range (not too low, not too high), evaporator temp sensor above freeze-up threshold (~34°F), engine not at wide-open throttle → computer grounds the compressor clutch relay → relay feeds 12V to the clutch coil → clutch locks the pulley to the shaft. Every "AC quit" diagnosis walks that chain: fuse → relay → pressure switch/transducer → clutch coil resistance (~3-5Ω typical) → commanded ground from the ECM. A clutch that clicks rapidly is the low-pressure cutoff protecting a starved system, electrical symptom, refrigerant cause.

Meter skills that separate duct guys from service techs

  • Voltage checks live circuits, continuity checks dead ones. Never ohm a live circuit.
  • Voltage drop across a closed switch/contactor should be ~0V; reading source voltage across it means it's open. Fastest diagnostic logic there is.
  • Amp clamp on the compressor common vs nameplate RLA (rated load amps); locked rotor pulls LRA for an instant. Chronic near-LRA = failing start components or a dying compressor.
  • Sequence: verify 24V at the stat wires, verify contactor coil voltage, verify line voltage through the contactor, verify capacitor, then condemn the motor. In that order, cheap to expensive.

The gauntlet
