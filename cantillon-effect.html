01 / Fundamentals

What Is the Cantillon Effect?

Richard Cantillon was a French-Irish economist who wrote what is considered the first systematic treatise on economic theory around 1730. His Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en Général (Essay on the Nature of Trade in General) was published posthumously in 1755. Within it, he described something that modern economists now call the Cantillon Effect, though most people only understand the surface version of it.

The Version Everyone Knows

Cantillon used a hypothetical country that discovers a silver mine. New money starts entering the system. The people closest to the mine, the first to receive this new money, get to spend it at existing prices. They get the full benefit. As that money trickles through the economy, prices begin to rise. The people at the very end of the chain are stuck paying higher prices while their wages haven't moved. They're worse off than before the mine opened.

Most people apply this to the Federal Reserve: the Fed creates new money, banks and well-connected institutions get first access, and by the time it reaches ordinary people, prices have already adjusted upward. That's correct, but it's only the beginning of what Cantillon actually described.

The Part Most People Miss

Cantillon went much deeper. As new money enters the system and drives prices up, two things happen simultaneously. First, inhabitants who can't keep up with rising prices, those who haven't received any benefit from the new money, start leaving. They bail out of the economy entirely. Second, and this is the critical mechanism, rising domestic prices create demand for foreign trade.

The neighboring state looks at your inflated prices and says: I can sell you this product just as cheap, if not cheaper, than what you're manufacturing domestically. Foreign imports start flooding in. And where does the money go to pay for those imports? It heads out of the country.

💡 The Core Insight

The Cantillon Effect isn't just about who gets the money first. It's about how money creation systematically destroys domestic manufacturing and replaces it with foreign dependency. The silver mine doesn't just cause inflation. It kills the economy that hosts it.

As this cycle accelerates, even more money enters the system, even more foreign imports come in, even more cash leaves. The people at the end of the line don't just deal with higher prices, they have less money circulating in their economy than they did before. It becomes progressively worse with each iteration.

02 / The Mechanism

The Manufacturing Death Spiral

Here's the full cycle that Cantillon described, and that has played out repeatedly throughout history. It doesn't happen in one administration or one decade. This is a multi-generational process.

01

Surplus Wealth

A state produces more than it consumes. Manufacturing output exceeds domestic demand. This surplus acts like Cantillon's silver mine, a new source of money flooding the system.

02

Rising Standard of Living

With surplus money, people start increasing their standard of living. They buy more, demand more. This demand starts pushing prices upward across the entire economy.

03

Foreign Competition Enters

Neighboring states see inflated domestic prices and say: we can produce that cheaper. Foreign imports start flowing in. The cost of domestic labor and manufacturing makes competing increasingly difficult.

04

Money Flows Out

Payments for imports drain money from the domestic economy. The people who benefit most are importers, distributors of foreign goods, and the foreigners themselves. Money leaves the country.

05

Manufacturing Collapses

Domestic manufacturers can't compete with foreign prices. Factories close. Workers leave or lose jobs. The state transitions from producer to consumer, from exporter to importer.

06

Dependency Locks In

Without manufacturing capacity, the state is now structurally dependent on foreign imports for survival. The only thing keeping it afloat is whatever makes foreigners still willing to accept its currency.

💡 Key Point

This isn't about one bad policy or one president. This process plays out over many generations and decades. It's the natural course of economic evolution that Cantillon identified nearly 300 years ago, and it maps almost perfectly onto the American experience.

03 / The American Story

From Manufacturer to Importer

The United States was once the biggest manufacturer in the world. It produced more goods and distributed more to the rest of the world than any other nation in history. That completely reversed. The U.S. is now the world's biggest importer.

Cantillon's cycle explains exactly how this happened. When the U.S. was the dominant manufacturer, it was producing more than it could consume. That surplus of wealth acted like the silver mine. Americans started raising their standard of living, buying more, demanding more. Prices went up. Foreign competition saw an opportunity and moved in. Imports started replacing domestic production. And here we are.

Then (1950s-1960s)

  • Largest manufacturer on Earth
  • Exported goods to the world
  • Trade surplus generated wealth
  • Manufacturing jobs everywhere
  • Money flowed into the country

Now

  • Largest importer on Earth
  • Imports goods from the world
  • Trade deficit drains wealth
  • Manufacturing jobs gone
  • Money flows out of the country

Who Benefits From This System?

The Cantillon Effect tells you exactly who comes out on top in this arrangement: the people who have first access to the money (the Federal Reserve and its cronies), the importers and distributors who bring foreign goods into the country, and the foreigners themselves who are exporting into the U.S. market. Everyone else, the workers, the domestic producers, the people at the end of the line, gets progressively worse off.

The Paper Dollar Problem

So what does the U.S. trade for all these foreign imports? Paper dollars. And why would anybody in the world want U.S. dollars? There's only one real reason: the dollar is the world reserve currency. That's it. The U.S. doesn't manufacture things other countries need. It manufactures dollars. The Federal Reserve is the silver mine.

"We don't manufacture here in the United States. We are dependent on foreign imports. And if there ever came a time when the foreign importers say 'why are we trading our stuff for these dollars, this doesn't make any sense,' then we are going to be in one hell of a situation since we don't manufacture anything here."

04 / Real-World Examples

Semiconductors & Surgical Masks

The Semiconductor Fantasy

The U.S. has been planning to ramp up domestic semiconductor production for national security reasons, reducing reliance on foreign producers like Taiwan. The idea sounds great on paper: secure the domestic supply chain, protect critical infrastructure, bring manufacturing home.

The problem is that Cantillon already explained why this can't work. If the U.S. ramps up semiconductor production domestically, those semiconductors will be more expensive than virtually anywhere else in the world. Foreign competitors will see the price the U.S. charges and immediately try to compete. Granted, U.S. semiconductors might start out as higher quality, but it's only a matter of time before foreign producers match that quality at a lower price point.

The U.S. can respond with tariffs on foreign semiconductors or subsidies for domestic manufacturers. But both of those just add even more cost to the product, making it even more expensive, which gives foreign producers even more incentive to compete. It's a self-reinforcing trap.

⚠️ The Feedback Loop

Tariffs and subsidies don't break the cycle. They accelerate it. Every dollar spent protecting domestic production raises the price foreign competitors are trying to undercut. The more you protect, the bigger the target you paint on your industry.

The Surgical Mask Precedent

This already played out in miniature during COVID. When the pandemic hit, foreign supply chains broke down. The U.S. suddenly needed domestic surgical mask production for national security. Domestic manufacturers ramped up, hired people, built out facilities.

Then the pandemic ended. Demand dropped. And the hospitals, the primary bulk purchasers, went right back to foreign producers because they could get masks for a fraction of the domestic price. The domestic manufacturers were left with expensive facilities, excess inventory, and no customers. They now need either tariffs on imported masks or government subsidies just to stay alive.

"The perfect example of this was the domestic surgical mask producers who ramped up production during the COVID outbreak. Once the demand had fallen, the hospitals were going to avoid the domestic manufacturers because they could get the foreign producers to sell to them for a much cheaper price. Same thing will happen with semiconductors."

05 / From the Source

What Cantillon Actually Wrote

The second video reads directly from Chapter 8 of Cantillon's essay, "Further Reflections on the Increase and Decrease of Money in a State." These passages were written nearly 300 years ago, yet they describe the modern American economy with eerie precision. Here's the progression Cantillon laid out.

Phase 1: The Rise

"It is by this last means [a positive balance of trade] that a state grows most substantially, especially when its trade is accompanied and supported by ample shipping and by significant output of raw materials necessary for the production of exported manufactured goods."

Cantillon, Essai, Chapter 8

This describes the United States from roughly the 1940s through the late 1960s: the world's dominant manufacturer, exporting goods globally, supported by massive shipping infrastructure and abundant raw materials. Money was flowing in.

Phase 2: The Turn

"However, the continuation of this commerce gradually introduces a great abundance of money and little by little increases consumption. Foreign products must be imported to meet this demand, and must be paid for by the reduction of the annual balance of trade."

Cantillon, Essai, Chapter 8

As wealth accumulated, Americans wanted more and cheaper consumer goods. The demand for foreign imports grew. The trade surplus started shrinking. This was the 1970s and 1980s.

Phase 3: The Competition

"It often happens that some foreign countries try to set up the same kinds of factories for themselves, in which case they stop buying those of the state in question. Although these newly established factories and manufacturers are not perfect at first, they do reduce and even prevent the exporting of those goods from the neighboring state."

Cantillon, Essai, Chapter 8

Foreign nations saw American prices and built their own factories. At first the quality wasn't as good, but given time, they caught up. American exports declined. This is exactly the semiconductor scenario playing out today: foreign competitors will eventually match domestic quality at lower cost.

Phase 4: The Collapse

"When a state has arrived at its highest point of wealth... it will inevitably fall back into poverty by the ordinary course of things. Too great an abundance of money, which gives power to the states so long as it lasts, throws them back imperceptibly but naturally into poverty."

Cantillon, Essai, Chapter 8

The wealth that made the nation powerful is the very mechanism that destroys it. The money itself, through the Cantillon Effect, drives out manufacturing, creates foreign dependency, and hollows the economy from the inside. Cantillon says this isn't a policy failure. It's the ordinary course of things.

Phase 5: What Leaders Should Have Done

"Thus it would seem that when a state expands by trade and the abundance of money raises prices of land and labor, the prince or the legislator ought to withdraw money from circulation, keep it for emergencies, and try to slow down the circulation... to prevent its goods from becoming too expensive and avoid the drawbacks of luxury."

Cantillon, Essai, Chapter 8

Cantillon's prescription was to pull money out when a state is at its peak, to slow the cycle, to prevent the luxury spiral that sends money abroad. In other words: fiscal discipline at the exact moment when everyone feels richest. Predictably, no leader in history has ever done this.

💡 On Leadership

Cantillon observed that leaders never manage this. They use the abundance to "extend their power and to insult other countries on the most frivolous pretext," leaving monuments to their own glory. "According to the natural course of humanity, the state must collapse on its own. They only accelerate its fall a little."

06 / The Current Situation

The Dollar Trap

Here's where it gets genuinely alarming. The United States doesn't manufacture. It imports. The only reason foreign nations send us their goods is that they want dollars, because the dollar is the world reserve currency. The Federal Reserve is, functionally, the silver mine. It can print money, hand it to Americans, and Americans use that money to buy foreign products. The whole arrangement only works because there's demand for dollars outside the United States.

⚠️ The Existential Question

How much longer will the world continue to want the U.S. dollar as the world reserve currency? If that demand disappears, and the U.S. has no manufacturing base, we have nothing to fall back on. No factories. No production. No way to provide for ourselves. We are dependent.

The China Mirror

Interestingly, China is experiencing its own version of this effect from the opposite side. China is the manufacturer, and the money flowing in from American imports is creating inflationary pressure domestically, particularly in their property markets. People want to blame this on easy money policies, but it could be the same Cantillon dynamic: manufacturing more than you consume, surplus money flowing in, prices rising, the people at the end getting squeezed. Same theory, different position in the cycle.

If Gold Becomes Reserve Currency

If for any reason gold or another asset displaced the dollar as world reserve currency, the U.S. would immediately lose the ability to convince the world to send us their goods in exchange for paper. Other nations would be sending their goods to whoever holds the reserve asset. The United States, with no manufacturing base, would have nothing to offer in return. This is the scenario that makes the current situation so precarious.

07 / Where This Is Headed

CBDCs, UBI, and the Next Recession

Why They're Pushing UBI

Once you understand the Cantillon Effect, the push for Universal Basic Income starts making a different kind of sense. The people who benefit most from the current system (those with first access to money, the importers, the foreign producers) know that if the world ever decides it doesn't want the dollar, Americans are screwed. We don't make things. We don't have jobs producing goods. So what do you do with a population that has no manufacturing base and an economy entirely dependent on imports?

You give them money. You give them money so they can continue buying foreign products. Because we're not manufacturing them ourselves and since we don't really do anything, what else is there?

Central Bank Digital Currencies

China has already implemented a central bank digital currency (CBDC) through its existing digital payment apps. The two most common forms of payment in China are already digital, phone-based. The central bank just added a CBDC option to those same apps. You can use regular money or the new digital currency. The question is: why would anyone switch?

The answer is simple: if they just give it to you, you'll use it.

How It Comes to America

1

Attach it to existing platforms

The CBDC gets integrated into apps Americans already use: PayPal, Venmo, Cash App. A new tab or button. No new app to download, no learning curve. Just another payment option on the platform you already have.

2

Wait for a recession

The best guess is the next recession triggers it. Congress will have pre-authorized emergency measures: a catastrophe bond, recession bond, whatever they call it. If GDP drops below a threshold or unemployment spikes past a line, the mechanism activates automatically without a new vote.

3

Digital wallets with free money

An app appears with free money loaded in it. Digital wallets, pre-funded. People won't resist this. They'll beg for it. They'll download it and start spending immediately. The Federal Reserve will have had "all kinds of tools ready" for exactly this moment.

4

Adoption happens overnight

Most people won't even notice the difference. They'll be using central bank digital currency through the same apps they already use, and they probably won't realize anything changed. "FedCoin" or whatever it's called will be in play, and nobody will have been "convinced." They'll have been nudged during a crisis.

💡 The Pattern

The convincing doesn't happen through argument. It happens through crisis. When people are desperate, they don't ask questions about the payment mechanism, they just want the money. The infrastructure will already be built. The authorization will already be signed. The trigger just needs to be pulled.

08 / The Structural Trap

Why There's No Reversing Course

This is the most uncomfortable part of the Cantillon Effect: once you're deep enough in the cycle, there is no fix. You can't just decide to start manufacturing again.

The Tariff Trap

Tariffs raise the price of foreign imports, theoretically making domestic production competitive. But they also raise the cost for every American consumer and every domestic business that uses imported components. And foreign producers don't just accept it. They find workarounds, route through third countries, or simply wait for the political winds to change. Tariffs are a bandaid on a structural wound.

The Subsidy Trap

Subsidies keep domestic manufacturers alive artificially, but they add cost to the system. Those subsidies come from somewhere: taxes, debt, money printing. Each of those feeds back into the Cantillon cycle. Subsidize domestic semiconductors, and you're adding money to the system that will eventually raise prices, attract more foreign competition, and require even more subsidies.

The Competition Problem

Even if you successfully restart domestic manufacturing, you're competing against countries with lower labor costs, existing infrastructure, and decades of accumulated expertise. You'd have to be willing to take a loss for years, possibly decades, just to get back to competitive. And every year you're taking that loss, foreign competition is improving and finding new ways to undercut you.

"You cannot fix this system. You could go ahead and start to try and manufacture all that you want, but you're going to be in competition with foreign imports. And unless you're willing to take a loss for a while, it's going to be very difficult to come up with a product that is going to be cheaper than what somebody else can provide outside of our borders."

Cantillon's Verdict

Cantillon himself observed that once a state reaches its peak wealth, the decline is inevitable. "Few years are needed to raise the abundance to the highest point in the state; however still fewer are needed to bring it to poverty for lack of commerce and manufacturing." He cites historical examples: Venice, the Dutch Republic, France's manufacturing golden age from 1646 to 1684. Every one of them followed the same arc. Every one of them declined by the same mechanism.

⚠️ The Timeline

It doesn't take one bad administration. This is something that plays out over generations and decades. But once it's in motion, Cantillon says, it cannot be reversed. The state must collapse on its own. Leaders only accelerate the fall.

Where That Leaves Us

The United States once was a manufacturer. Now it's an importer. Because of that, and because of the world reserve currency, it's stuck in a situation where it has to continuously import foreign goods. If there's no manufacturing base to produce money the old-fashioned way (through actual production), you need a job to make money, but without manufacturing, what jobs are there? The Fed becomes the mine. Print it up, hand it out, helicopter money, go spend it. We're not buying our own stuff anyway. We're buying manufactured goods from overseas. It doesn't matter if you just print it and give it away.

That is, until the rest of the world stops wanting the paper.
