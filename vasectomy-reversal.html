The Procedure

What Actually Happens

The surgeon doesn't know which of the two techniques you'll need until they're in there looking at fluid under a microscope mid-surgery. This is a critical detail — you can't plan for one or the other in advance.

1

General Anesthesia

Completely asleep. Outpatient — go home same day.

2

Small Incision

Less than an inch on each side of the scrotum. Surgeon locates the two severed ends of the vas deferens.

3

Fluid Check Under Microscope

Surgeon examines fluid from the testicular side. If sperm or clear fluid is present, they proceed with the simpler reconnection. If thick/pasty with no sperm, a secondary blockage has formed and they do the bypass instead.

4

Microsurgical Reconnection

Using sutures finer than a human hair under 30–40x magnification. 6–10 stitches create a watertight two-layer closure. About 2–3 hours total.

Two Possible Techniques

Vasovasostomy (VV) — the simpler, more common one. Direct reconnection of the two cut ends of the vas deferens. 90–95% patency rate. This is what the VA urologist said is most likely given the short interval since vasectomy.

Vasoepididymostomy (VE) — the complex bypass. Only needed if a secondary blockage has developed upstream closer to the testicle. 65–70% patency rate. This is why surgeon selection matters — not all urologists can perform VE, and you need someone who can do both so they can switch mid-surgery if needed.

Recovery

About a week of light duty. 2–4 weeks to full activity. No sexual activity for 3 weeks to protect the delicate reconnection. Sperm typically returns in weeks to months. First semen analysis usually at 6–8 weeks post-op.

VA Coverage

What the VA Covers

The VA covers vasectomy reversal as a fertility/contraceptive service for enrolled veterans under VHA Directive 1332 (Fertility Management) and VHA Directive 1102.2 (Elective Sterilization and Reversal). This doesn't require service-connected disability — just enrollment in VA healthcare.

Durham VA doesn't do the surgery in-house (most VAs don't have microsurgery capability). So they're referring out to Community Care, which means a private surgeon does the procedure and the VA pays the provider directly. Zero cost to the veteran.

The VA confirmed they cover everything except IVF. Here's what each of those things actually is and how they fit together:

✓

Vasovasostomy (VV) — Direct Reconnection

The simpler of the two surgical options. The surgeon stitches the two cut ends of the vas deferens (the tube that carries sperm from the testicle) back together. Think of it like splicing two ends of a garden hose. This is the most likely procedure given the short time since vasectomy. 90–95% success rate for sperm returning.

✓

Vasoepididymostomy (VE) — Bypass Surgery

The more complex backup procedure. Sometimes a secondary blockage develops upstream, closer to the testicle, in a coiled tube called the epididymis. If the surgeon finds that during the operation, they bypass the blockage by connecting the vas deferens directly to the epididymis — skipping the clogged section entirely. Lower success rate (65–70%) but still very effective. Not all surgeons can do this, which is why picking someone trained in both is critical.

✓

Sperm Aspiration (TESA/MESA) — Extracting Sperm Directly

If for whatever reason the reversal doesn't produce enough viable sperm in the ejaculate, doctors can go get sperm straight from the source. TESA (Testicular Sperm Aspiration) uses a needle to pull sperm directly from the testicle. MESA (Microsurgical Epididymal Sperm Aspiration) pulls sperm from the epididymis under a microscope. The extracted sperm can then be used for IUI or frozen for later. This can also be done at the same time as the reversal surgery as a backup — "while we're in the neighborhood, grab some and put it on ice."

✓

IUI (Intrauterine Insemination) — The "Turkey Baster" Method

Colloquially called the turkey baster method, though it's a bit more refined than that. A doctor takes a sperm sample (either from ejaculate or from aspiration), washes and concentrates the best swimmers, and injects them directly into the uterus through a thin catheter. Bypasses the cervix entirely, putting sperm closer to the egg. It's done in a clinic, takes about 15 minutes, feels like a Pap smear. Often paired with fertility medications to time ovulation. Much less invasive and cheaper than IVF — and the VA covers it.

✓

All Follow-ups, Semen Analyses, and Evaluations

Post-surgery semen analyses (typically at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months) to check if sperm are returning and in what quantity/quality. Fertility evaluations for both partners. Hormone testing. All covered.

✗

IVF (In Vitro Fertilization) — The Lab Baby Method

IVF is the big one most people have heard of. Eggs are harvested from the woman's ovaries (after hormone stimulation), fertilized with sperm in a lab dish, grown into embryos for a few days, then transferred into the uterus. It's the most effective but most invasive and expensive fertility treatment — $15,000–$25,000 per cycle out of pocket. The VA only covers IVF if a veteran has a service-connected disability that directly caused their infertility. An elective vasectomy doesn't count. More on this below.

The IVF Question

What If We Need IVF?

The VA's IVF rule is strict: a veteran must have a VBA-adjudicated service-connected disability (or its treatment) that caused the infertility. The vasectomy was elective — not caused by military service — so it doesn't qualify under Benny's benefits.

Bekah is also a Marine veteran with 60% service-connected disability. In theory, if she had a service-connected condition that caused infertility on her end, she could qualify for IVF through her own VA benefits, and Benny as her lawful spouse would also be covered. But she doesn't currently have infertility claimed as a service-connected condition, and there's no indication of any fertility issues on her side. So this pathway isn't relevant right now — just worth knowing exists in case it ever matters.

There's also a bill in Congress right now — the Veterans Infertility Treatment Act of 2025 (H.R. 220) — that would expand VA IVF to any enrolled veteran with infertility, regardless of whether it's service-connected. If it passes, it would cover up to 3 completed IVF cycles or 10 attempts. It's been introduced but hasn't passed yet.

BOTTOM LINE ON IVF

We almost certainly won't need it. IVF is the nuclear option for when everything else has failed. Between the reversal surgery, sperm aspiration backup, and IUI, there are multiple layers of covered treatments before IVF would even enter the conversation. And if it ever does, private IVF runs $15–25k per cycle, so that's the out-of-pocket exposure in a worst-case scenario.

Realistic Assessment

How Many Things Would Have to Go Wrong

Here's the full escalation path from "easiest" to "last resort," and what would have to fail at each step before moving to the next. Think of it as layers of backup plans.

1

Reversal Works, Natural Conception ← Most Likely

97% chance sperm returns. 76–80%+ chance of pregnancy with both partners young and healthy. This is the expected outcome. Reversal is done, sperm comes back in weeks to months, and you conceive naturally with no further medical intervention. The vast majority of couples in this situation stop here.

2

Reversal Works but Sperm Quality Is Low → IUI

What went wrong: Sperm returned but count or motility is too low for natural conception. Maybe anti-sperm antibodies are interfering. Solution: IUI — concentrate the best sperm and inject directly into the uterus. Often combined with fertility meds for Bekah to optimize timing. VA covers this. Multiple attempts are normal. Success rate per cycle is 10–20%, but over several cycles the cumulative odds are solid.

3

Reversal Fails (Re-stenosis) → Sperm Aspiration + IUI

What went wrong: Scar tissue closed the reconnection site months after surgery (happens in roughly 5–10% of cases). Sperm count drops back to zero. Solution: If sperm was banked during the original surgery, use frozen sperm for IUI. If not, TESA or MESA can retrieve sperm directly from the testicle or epididymis. VA covers all of this. Could also do a second reversal attempt.

4

Everything Above Fails → IVF (Out of Pocket)

What went wrong: Reversal failed, re-stenosis occurred, sperm aspiration yields very few sperm, IUI hasn't worked after multiple cycles, or an unexpected female factor fertility issue surfaces. Solution: IVF — eggs harvested, fertilized in lab with whatever sperm is available (even very low numbers work for IVF with ICSI, where a single sperm is injected directly into an egg). VA won't cover this unless the law changes. Out of pocket: $15–25k per cycle. This is genuinely the last resort.

5

IVF Doesn't Work → We're Talking Extreme Edge Cases

What went wrong: Multiple IVF cycles failed with own gametes, or there's an underlying issue nobody caught. Reality check: With both partners young and otherwise healthy, this scenario requires a catastrophic combination of surgical failure, sperm production failure, AND an undiagnosed female factor. The statistical probability of ending up here from the starting position is extremely low — we're talking low single-digit percentages.
