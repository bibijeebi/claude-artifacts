A FIELD GUIDE
A SOCIETY-PAGE FIELD GUIDE

The Country Club universe

Pastels and Nantucket reds. Tweed and tall boots. Bows tied at the bottom of every braid. A field guide to the leisure-class aesthetic that ties pink Lilly Pulitzer dresses, hunt-seat equestrian disciplines, and a Holland & Holland over-and-under into one coherent universe.
— from the archives —
I.

The thesis.

Country Club Prep is not aspirational. It is the actual leisure class the yuppie was trying to imitate. Old money, sporting, optimistic, and deeply patterned with bows.

The glue holding this whole aesthetic together is one specific idea: leisure-class sport. Riding, sailing, shooting, tennis, golf, croquet. Not sport for fitness, sport as tradition. Every visual detail in this universe either is sporting equipment or has been styled to look sporting-adjacent. That's why the same person can be in a tweed shooting jacket on Saturday morning and a pink dress at brunch the same afternoon. They're both costumes for "I have a place in the country."

"It is the manner in which one ties the bow, not the bow itself."

The yuppie performed wealth from a desk. Country Club Prep performs wealth from a stable. The first one is anxious, the second one is serene. That's the whole spread. Once you see the sport-leisure thesis, every Lilly Pulitzer print and every Barbour wax-cotton field jacket and every grosgrain ribbon belt clicks into the same picture.

II.

The three palettes.

Three color systems live inside the same closet. Each maps to a season, a sport, and a setting.
SUMMER · LEISURE · FEMININE-LEANING

Pastels.

Sherbet, butter, mint, robin's egg, watermelon, flamingo. The Lilly Pulitzer zone. Palm Beach, Easter brunch, the Kentucky Derby, garden parties, club rosé. Saturated, optimistic, almost dessert-coded.

SUMMER · SAILING · UNISEX

Nautical.

Navy, Nantucket red, white, brass, signal-flag yellow. Brass-button blazers, Breton stripes, regatta bunting. Newport, the Hamptons, Marblehead. Most masculine-coded slice but everyone wears it on a boat.

AUTUMN / WINTER · SPORTING · MASCULINE-LEANING

Tweed & earth.

Heather greens, oxblood, mustard, peat brown, hunt pink. Riding habits, shooting jackets, Wellingtons, Barbour wax cotton. The English country flavor. Where the equestrian and skeet stuff actually lives.

III.

The bows & ribbons.

The universal accent. Not a fourth palette. The thing that sits on top of all three and makes the whole register feminine, soft, and a little tongue-in-cheek.

The bow is how you gentle any serious sporting garment. Grosgrain ribbon belts in club stripes (navy/red, hunter/cream, watermelon/lime) cinch a polo dress. A wide silk bow tied at the bottom of a long French braid softens a hunt habit. Bow-front Belgian loafers replace any other shoe whenever indoors.

Ribbon shows up on hats (the band around a straw hat), on gift wrap (curling ribbon, monogrammed), on hair, on dress sashes, on shoes, on collars. Monograms count as cousins of the bow: stationery, towels, totes, silver cuffs, anything inherited.

Without the bow, the same closet reads as British country gentleman or naval officer. With the bow, it reads as club.

IV.

The three sports.

The visible expressions of the leisure-class thesis. Each one carries its own dress code, its own season, and its own corner of the wardrobe.
i.

Equestrian.

Hunt seat is the cleanest, most disciplined slice of the whole universe. Navy hunt coat with brass buttons, white stock tie, tan breeches, polished black tall boots, velvet hunt cap. The dress code descends directly from English fox hunting and gets dressed-up for show jumping competition.

ii.

Sporting.

Skeet, sporting clays, and bird shooting. Tweed Norfolk jackets, knit ties, moleskin trousers, leather gaiters. A broken-open over-and-under English shotgun in the crook of the arm. A Lab at heel. This is the autumn-coded, masculine, warm-earthy side.

iii.

Sailing.

Brass-button blazer over white linen trousers. Topsiders without socks. Red sweater tied over the shoulders. Signal-flag bunting strung along the rigging of a wooden Herreshoff at the Newport regatta. The sun-bleached, summer-coded, unisex middle of the diagram.

VI.

The sub-flavors.

One universe, six dialects. Each one has the same grammar (sport, money, ribbon) but a different accent.
i.

Palm Beach.

PEAK PASTEL

Lilly, Pucci, jeweled sandals, citrus print, lots of pink, even more green. Worth Avenue. Where the bow lives loudest.

ii.

The Hamptons.

NAUTICAL CORE

Hydrangeas, cedar shingles, hydrangeas, white Volvo wagons, hydrangeas. Quieter than Palm Beach. Money in beige.

iii.

Nantucket.

WHALES & ROSE

Whales on everything. Faded Nantucket reds. Lobster bake. Cobblestones. Even older money in beige than the Hamptons.

iv.

Bluegrass.

DERBY & HORSES

Giant pastel hats, mint juleps, bourbon on the porch, white fences. Equestrian-leaning, Southern, dressier than Hamptons.

v.

Sloane.

ENGLISH COUNTRY

Barbour, tweed, Hermès silk square, Wellingtons, pheasants, dogs. The whole shooting and field-sport flavor. Tatler.

vi.

Yacht Club.

REGATTA · BLAZER

Brass-button blazers, signal flags, white trousers, regimental ties, the most uniformed slice of the whole universe.

VIII.

The canon.

If you read, watch, and absorb only six things, these are the six.
  • Slim Aarons: Once Upon a Time— photo book The visual bible. Croquet on lawns, ladies in pastels, poolside Palm Beach. Required.
  • The Official Preppy Handbook— Lisa Birnbach, 1980 Half satire, half instruction, fully canonical. The original codex.
  • True Prep— Lisa Birnbach, 2010 The 30-years-later sequel. The aesthetic in its post-yuppie, modern-revival form.
  • Take Ivy— Hayashida et al., 1965 Japanese photographers documenting Ivy League menswear. The granular kit reference.
  • Metropolitan— Whit Stillman, 1990 Debutante balls, drawing rooms, the whole tribe in full deployment. The Stillman trilogy is required.
  • The Philadelphia Story— Cukor, 1940 Tracy Lord. Hepburn in jodhpurs. The grandmother of the entire register.
IX.
