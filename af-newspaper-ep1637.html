"If This Is the Best We Have to Offer, People Are Going with Latino Futurism": Fuentes Defends Bad Bunny, Torches Kid Rock and the Conservative Halftime Meltdown

In a show-length broadside against the right-wing reaction to the Super Bowl, the host argued the conservative movement has become the very thing it once mocked: humorless, bitter, and self-ghettoizing.

Fuentes opened Monday's broadcast with an extended, often satirical meditation on the Super Bowl LX halftime show, which featured Puerto Rican reggaeton artist Bad Bunny performing an entirely Spanish-language set that concluded with flags from every country in the Americas and a "God Bless America" sendoff. Conservative commentators had spent the weekend calling the performance anti-American, culturally imperialist, and a deliberate provocation during the ICE deportation campaign. Fuentes rejected nearly all of it.

"I did not feel antagonized by the show," he said. "I didn't feel like the show was taunting me. I didn't feel like it was defiant. I didn't feel like it was militant or anti-American." He argued that, viewed through the eyes of a politically disengaged viewer, the performance was "basically just an inoffensive Latin themed show" that was "propositionally positive" rather than militantly woke. He acknowledged the political subtext, noting that Bad Bunny had said "fuck ICE" at the Grammys and that the display of Latin American flags carried obvious significance during mass deportations, but maintained that the show was implicitly political rather than explicitly antagonistic, and therefore far more effective.

"If the white right, if the best we have to offer is the same sad dying gasp of white culture, it's like some white trash country hip hop artist from Detroit from the 90s. If that's the best we have to offer, people are going with liberal hegemony."

"Loser Festival": The Turning Point Alternative

The host reserved his sharpest criticism not for Bad Bunny but for the Turning Point USA "All American Halftime Show," a parallel event headlined by Kid Rock and a roster of country acts. Fuentes said he watched the entire broadcast and found it unwatchable. "Nobody under the age of 40 knows a single Kid Rock song," he said. "The costumes suck, the production sucks, the songs suck, the lyrics are weird." He singled out songs whose lyrics he described as entirely about culture-war grievance, including one he paraphrased as "I can't be country no more cause I'm getting canceled for saying boys can't be girls." He contrasted this with Bad Bunny's setlist: "If you translate some of the Bad Bunny songs, they're about like taking pictures with your friends at a party."

The asymmetry, Fuentes argued, was devastating. The Bad Bunny performance felt "triumphant" and projected "an expansive, inspiring vision," while the Turning Point show projected weakness and victimhood. "One was about the music and dancing and fun, and one was really weirdly political and just kind of goofy," he said. He compared the conservative withdrawal to the behavior that alienated normies from the left a decade ago: performative outrage, self-ghettoization, and a refusal to participate in mainstream culture.

The Post-Woke Left

Fuentes situated the halftime debate within a broader thesis he called "post-woke leftism." He argued that the American left began shifting around 2021 after the contradictions of BLM, COVID lockdowns, and critical race theory alienated ordinary people. Rather than doubling down, he said, the left adapted. He cited the "dirtbag left" comedians, Kamala Harris's selection of Tim Walz as a white Midwestern olive branch, the "White Dudes for Kamala" campaign, and a general loosening of progressive speech codes as evidence of a left that had synthesized its critics' arguments and emerged more formidable. "If the left stops being militantly anti-white, they actually become formidable again," he warned.

He cautioned that running the 2016 playbook in 2026, the "Matt Walsh grumpy, spiteful thing," would fail against this evolved opposition. Trump succeeded in 2016 not because of culture-war grievance, Fuentes argued, but because he projected glamour, celebrity, sex appeal, and a forward-thinking energy that felt progressive. "He came from New York City, an actual real, respectable world class city. And he came from Hollywood and he was famous and a billionaire and he talked like a celebrity." The right, Fuentes said, had forgotten this "secret ingredient" and fully squandered its cultural momentum within twelve months of the 2024 election.
