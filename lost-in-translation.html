A linguistic field guide

Lost in Translation

Kinship terms, honorifics, and the social hierarchies English forgot

English flattened its address system centuries ago. We lost thou, forgot formal you, and ended up calling everyone from our boss to our dog "dude." Other languages kept the receipts, encoding age, gender, respect, and relationship into every interaction.

Chinese (Mandarin)

Birth order matters
哥哥
gēge
Older brother
General/affectionate term. The doubled character adds warmth.
大哥
dàgē
Eldest brother / "Big bro"
Also used for older male strangers, or in organized crime contexts. Context is everything.
Street usage
弟弟
dìdi
Younger brother
The counterpart. Chinese always distinguishes older vs younger.
二哥 / 三哥
èrgē / sāngē
Second / Third brother
Birth order encoded directly. No ambiguity about who's who.

The rabbit hole: Chinese kinship terminology distinguishes paternal vs maternal side AND birth order. The English word "cousin" maps to about 8 different Chinese terms depending on whose side, older/younger, male/female.

Vietnamese

The final boss

Pronouns That Shift With Every Conversation

Vietnamese has no fixed "I" or "you." Pronouns change based on relative age AND the speaker's gender. "I love you" is a completely different sentence depending on who's older.

anh
anh
Older brother / I / you
"I" if you're the older male. "You" if talking to an older male. It's all relative.
chị
chị
Older sister / I / you
Same deal, female version. Restructures the entire sentence.
em
em
Younger sibling / I / you
"I" if you're younger. "You" to someone younger. Romantic partners often use anh/em.
Romantic use
cô / chú / bác
cô / chú / bác
Aunt / uncle tiers
Based on age relative to your parents. Multiple levels of aunt/uncle.

The T-V Distinction

European formal/informal "you"

Most European languages kept what English lost: two versions of "you." Using the wrong one is a social signal, too familiar or too cold.

tu / vous
French
Informal / formal
The classic. "On se tutoie?" = asking to switch to informal. A whole social negotiation.
du / Sie
German
Informal / formal
Workplace Sie, friend du. Getting the "du" offer from your boss is a milestone.
tú / usted
Spanish
Informal / formal
Plus "vos" in Argentina, with its own conjugation. Three levels of "you."
ты / вы
Russian (ty / vy)
Informal / formal
Вы also doubles as plural "you." Context determines respect vs quantity.

The Pattern

Languages with Confucian, hierarchical, or collectivist roots tend to encode social relationships directly into address terms. You can't speak without declaring your position relative to the other person. English flattened most of this, so these systems feel foreign to us, but they're actually the norm globally. We're the weird ones.
